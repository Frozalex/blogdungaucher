You've been playing tournaments back to back for four months. Your rating stalls, then drops. You start making mistakes that don't look like you: not subtle strategic errors, but hanging pieces, elementary tactical patterns you'd have solved without thinking a year ago.

You conclude that you're regressing. That you've hit your ceiling. That you should work harder.

There's another explanation, and it's physiological. It doesn't concern your level: it concerns the state you're in while you use that level.

## Two Kinds of Stress That Don't Do the Same Thing

Let's start with the distinction this article exists to make, because it's counterintuitive and rarely explained.

Stress isn't a scale running from a little to a lot. These are **two different regimes**, and on the cognitive side, their effects are opposite.

### Acute Stress Improves Things

A brief spike of cortisol, in a one-off situation, produces effects that run in the right direction.

Attention narrows, something often described as a tunnel effect. Memory improves, especially the encoding of salient events. Energy mobilisation goes up.

That's no accident: it's the function of the system. It's built to improve performance in the face of a one-off threat, then return to baseline.

That's the regime described in our article on [managing tournament stress](/en/blog/echecs-stress-tournoi/), and it's also why a dose of tension before an important game isn't a problem to be eliminated.

### Chronic Stress Degrades Things

When the elevation stretches over weeks or months, the picture flips.

Prolonged exposure to glucocorticoids affects neurons and eventually impairs the functioning of two structures in particular.

**The hippocampus.** The first region, after the hypothalamus, identified as a target of glucocorticoids. Prolonged exposure produces atrophy there, with direct consequences for declarative memory.

**The prefrontal cortex.** Sustained cortisol elevation causes comparable dendritic retraction there, and the data show associated working memory deficits, that memory resting on the interaction between the prefrontal cortex and the hippocampus.

Put another way: prolonged stress attacks precisely **the two structures chess depends on**. Our article on [working memory and chess](/en/blog/echecs-memoire-de-travail/) describes why that resource is the bottleneck in calculating variations.

Another documented nuance: prolonged stress impairs cognition-based tasks, like verbal recall or spatial memory, while **facilitating** emotion-based ones, notably fear conditioning. Which suggests that in this state, you learn positions less well and apprehensions better. The mechanism is the one described in our article on [classical conditioning in chess](/en/blog/conditionnement-classique-aux-echecs/).

## Allostatic Load

The most useful theoretical framework for thinking about this accumulation comes from Bruce McEwen, in a paper published in 1998.

He starts from a distinction. **Homeostasis** refers to the maintenance of vital constants. **Allostasis** refers to stability achieved **through change**: the body modifies its parameters to adapt to a constraint. Speeding up the heart, mobilising glucose, raising vigilance.

That's a healthy mechanism. It becomes costly when it doesn't disengage.

**Allostatic load** is the name for that cost: the wear produced by repeated or prolonged activation of the stress response systems. It isn't stress that damages you, it's **the failure to return to baseline**.

The formulation is useful because it redirects the question. It doesn't ask whether your life is stressful, which has no usable answer. It asks: **how many times has the system switched on, and did it come back down between times?**

## What Produces a Chronic Load in a Player

Four sources, and none of them is dramatic.

**The competitive season.** An isolated tournament is acute stress, with recovery. Eight weekends in four months, with short nights and travel, produce an activation that never fully comes back down.

**Continuous rating pressure.** This is probably the source most specific to chess. Unlike almost any other hobby, your evaluation is permanent, public and updated after every game. There's no off-season. Our article on [social comparison in chess](/en/blog/comparaison-sociale-aux-echecs/) describes that setup.

**Late-night online play.** Often neglected and probably decisive. An evening blitz session combines competitive activation, light exposure and a sequence that's hard to break off, for the reasons laid out in our article on [operant conditioning in chess](/en/blog/conditionnement-operant-aux-echecs/). It pushes back the time you fall asleep, which degrades cortisol regulation the next day, which increases vulnerability to stress, which makes the evening session more appealing.

**The rest of your life.** This has to be said, because chess articles systematically forget it: your job, your family situation and your health weigh far more than your tournament calendar. Cortisol doesn't distinguish between sources.

## The Three Boundaries

This subject has close neighbours, and confusing them leads you to apply the wrong remedy.

**Acute stress** is one-off, improves cognition at moderate doses, and is treated with immediate regulation techniques: breathing, routine, preparation.

**Chronic stress**, the subject of this article, is a prolonged physiological state that degrades cognition. It isn't treated with in-the-moment management techniques, because there is no moment. It's treated by reducing load and restoring recovery.

**Burnout** is a fully formed psychological syndrome, with its three characteristic dimensions, described in our article on [burnout in chess](/en/blog/burnout-chess/). You can be chronically stressed without burnout, and prolonged chronic stress is a risk factor for it.

## The Useful Signs

Emotional indicators aren't very reliable here, because you get used to your own state. **Functional** indicators are better, and chess supplies excellent ones.

**Degraded working memory.** The most specific marker. You lose the thread of a variation you'd have held easily six months ago. You have to recalculate from the start because you can no longer retain the intermediate position. It's measurable: your long calculation exercises are harder than they used to be at equal difficulty.

**Errors on simple patterns.** Not subtle positional mistakes, which are a matter of level, but missed forks and hanging pieces. Those patterns are overlearned: failing them signals a resource problem, not a knowledge problem.

**Sleep that degrades in a lasting way.** Delayed sleep onset, night waking, unrefreshing sleep, over several weeks. Our article on [chess and sleep](/en/blog/echecs-et-sommeil/) details the links with performance.

**Persistent irritability after games.** Not tilt, which is acute and subsides, but a background annoyance that doesn't subside.

**And a simple test:** does your online rating at fast time controls, where preparation counts for little, drop below your usual level over several weeks? It's a crude signal but a real one.

## The Protocol

The order matters, and it isn't the one you'd spontaneously adopt.

### 1. Sleep First

This is the lever whose effect is best established, and it conditions all the others. Cortisol regulation depends on the circadian rhythm, and sleep is the first thing to degrade in a heavy season.

One rule, if you keep only one: **no online games in the hour before bed**. It's the most avoidable source of degradation, and the one that feeds the loop described above.

### 2. Schedule Recovery, Don't Hope for It

Allostatic load comes from the failure to return to baseline. And that return doesn't happen on its own in a saturated calendar.

Concretely: after a tournament, a week with no rated games. Not a week "without chess," which is unrealistic and pointless, but a week **with nothing at stake**. Analysing, reading, replaying games: all of that is possible without competitive activation.

### 3. Space Out Your Competitions

One tournament a month with recovery produces better results than two tournaments in a degraded state. It isn't a question of motivation, it's a question of available cognitive resource.

### 4. Reduce Your Exposure to the Rating

Since the pressure is continuous and permanent, the only variable you can act on is how often you check. Once a week is plenty, and in between, the number contains nothing but noise anyway.

### 5. And the Point That Has Nothing to Do With Chess

If the main source of your load is elsewhere, no modification of your tournament calendar will change anything. Chess will then be the place where you **notice** the problem, not its cause. That's useful information, and it's probably the best thing this game can give you in that situation: a daily cognitive test, quantified and honest.

## What to Take Away

Stress isn't an intensity, it's two regimes. A brief spike of cortisol sharpens attention and improves memory, which is exactly its function. A prolonged elevation produces the opposite effect, by impairing the hippocampus and the prefrontal cortex, that is the two structures working memory and calculating variations depend on.

McEwen's concept of allostatic load shifts the right question: it isn't about whether your life is stressful, it's about whether your system comes back down between two activations.

In chess, four sources add up, two of which are specific to this game: a permanent public evaluation with no off-season, and late-night online play that degrades sleep, which degrades cortisol regulation.

The most specific sign isn't emotional. It's working memory dropping off, and errors on patterns you had mastered. When those signs show up together, the hypothesis "I'm regressing" is probably wrong.

**After reading:** note what time you played your last online game yesterday evening. If it was in the hour before you went to bed, you're holding the highest-return lever in this whole article.
