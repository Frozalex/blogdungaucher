You're analyzing your game. Move 22, complicated position. You want to know what you should have played.

You open the analysis module. Before you've even started thinking, the number appears at the top of the screen: **+1.4**.

From that moment on, you can no longer evaluate this position. You can understand it, explore it, learn variations from it. But the independent estimate you would have produced will never exist, and you won't know how far off it would have been.

This isn't laziness. It's a mechanism that works even when you want to resist it, and even when you know the number is absurd.

## The Rigged Wheel

The founding experiment dates from 1974, in a *Science* paper by Amos Tversky and [Daniel Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchoring_effect) that would go down as one of the century's most influential.

The setup is provocatively simple. In front of the participant, a wheel of fortune marked 0 to 100 is spun. It stops on a number. In reality it's rigged and can only stop on **10** or **65**.

Two questions follow. First: is the percentage of African countries in the UN higher or lower than that number? Then: what is that percentage, in your opinion?

Participants who saw the wheel stop on 10 answered **25%** on average. Those who saw 65 answered **45%**.

Twenty points of difference, produced by a number drawn at random **before their eyes**, with no connection whatsoever to the question.

That's what makes the result so unsettling. It isn't about trusting a source. Nobody thinks a lottery wheel holds information about the composition of the UN. The number acts anyway, because it becomes the starting point from which the mind adjusts, and **the adjustment is systematically insufficient**.

### An Effect That Survived

This deserves flagging, because this series has spent a lot of time dismantling classics.

Anchoring isn't one of them. It's among the effects tested by the Many Labs project, which re-ran famous results across dozens of samples in several countries. Anchoring was **solidly replicated**, with effects sometimes larger than in the original study.

In other words: when this article tells you the engine's number shifts your judgment, that's not an extrapolation from a fragile result. It's one of the best-established phenomena in the psychology of decision-making.

## What Separates Anchoring From Plain Deference

A necessary clarification, because the subject resembles the one in our article on [obedience to authority in chess](/en/blog/obeissance-a-l-autorite-aux-echecs/), and it differs from it on one decisive point.

Deference means accepting a verdict **because the source is competent**. That's a piece of reasoning, and it's often correct: the engine is right, going along with it is rational.

Anchoring asks nothing of the source. It works with a lottery wheel. It isn't a decision to trust: it's an effect of the **numerical value itself**, which installs itself as a starting point before any reasoning happens.

Two practical consequences follow from that difference.

**Knowing the number is dubious doesn't protect you.** Participants knew the wheel was random. It changed nothing.

**An accurate number anchors even better than a false one.** Since the effect doesn't depend on credibility and works all the better when you have a reason not to resist, the engine's evaluation combines the worst possible conditions: it's numerical, precise, and correct.

## The Chess Player's Four Anchors

### 1. The Engine Evaluation

This is the main one, and historically it's new.

Before the 2000s, a player analyzing a position **had to produce his own judgment**. There was no alternative. Independent evaluation was a constraint of the setup, not a personal discipline.

Today the number appears by default, often before you've even looked at the position. And it updates continuously while you explore.

The result is that the engine era has **industrialized anchoring**. Not because players have become lazy, but because the order in which information appears has been reversed. The number arrives before the judgment, when it should arrive after.

And the effect doesn't stop at the overall evaluation. Once anchored on +1.4, you're no longer looking for the same things: you're looking for why it's winning, you've stopped looking for whether it's winning. The anchor steers the search, which ties it directly to [confirmation bias](/en/blog/biais-de-confirmation-aux-echecs/).

### 2. Your Opponent's Rating

Careful with the distinction from [the halo effect](/en/blog/effet-de-halo-aux-echecs/), which concerns the **perceived quality** of his moves. Here we're talking about a numerical estimate: what are my chances in this game.

A player sees "1890" and puts his chances at 20%. The Elo formula gives a precise value, and for a 200-point gap it indicates roughly 24% for the lower-rated player. Most club players underestimate that figure, sometimes by a lot, because they adjust from an anchor supplied by the displayed gap rather than actually computing.

### 3. The Theoretical Verdict

The most discreet of the four, because it doesn't look like a number.

An opening line annotated "equal" in a book hands you an anchor that will survive fifteen moves of real play. You keep evaluating the position as balanced when precise choices have created an imbalance.

What makes this anchor particularly stubborn is that it's perceived as a **fact** rather than an evaluation. But it is an evaluation, produced on a given date, often by an author working with tools from another generation. Our article on [survivorship bias in chess](/en/blog/biais-du-survivant-aux-echecs/) shows the filter through which that body of theory was assembled.

### 4. The Clock

This one goes completely unnoticed.

Your starting time anchors your sense of what a normal thinking time is. In 90 minutes, three minutes on a move seems reasonable. In 15 minutes, three minutes seems enormous.

But the time a position deserves doesn't depend on the time control: it depends on the position. The time control only sets the total budget. A player anchored on his time control allocates his time proportionally instead of allocating it by difficulty.

## The Protocol

### 1. Write Your Evaluation Before Displaying Its Own

That's the central rule, and it matches the one given in two other articles in this series. That convergence isn't repetition: it's the sign that a single habit protects against three different mechanisms.

Two requirements, and the second is the one people skip.

**A number, not an impression.** Write "+0.5" or "slightly better for White, about +0.5." A vague evaluation can't be compared, so it measures nothing.

**In writing.** This is essential, and not out of pedantry. Once you know the engine's number, your memory of what you thought will be adjusted in its direction. That's the mechanism described in our article on [false memories in chess](/en/blog/faux-souvenirs-aux-echecs/). Without a note made beforehand, you'll sincerely believe you thought something close to it.

### 2. Analyze With the Engine Off First

Take a game, pick the three critical positions and work them through completely without assistance: candidate moves, variations, final evaluation. Twenty minutes. Then turn it on.

The goal isn't to find the same answers as the machine, you won't. The goal is to **produce an independent judgment whose gap can be measured**, because that gap is the only thing that tells you anything about your real level.

### 3. Hide the Number While Exploring

Most interfaces let you hide the evaluation. Explore variations with it hidden, and reveal it only at the end of each line.

Otherwise the anchor updates continuously and all you're doing is following a slope, which produces the very pleasant and completely misleading feeling of understanding the position.

### 4. Date Your Theoretical Anchors

When a book or a video announces a line is equal, note when that evaluation was made and with what tools. A 2005 evaluation of a sharp tactical position doesn't have the same status as a 2024 one.

## Key Takeaways

A rigged wheel stopping on 10 or 65 shifts by twenty points the estimate people make of the UN's composition. The number is random, they watch it being drawn, and it acts anyway. That's anchoring, and unlike many social psychology classics, it has held up solidly under replication.

In chess, this mechanism changed scale with engines. The problem isn't that the machine is right, it is right. The problem is **the order of appearance**: the number arrives before your judgment, when it only has teaching value afterward.

And knowing all this doesn't protect you, since the effect works even on numbers you know to be absurd. The only defense is procedural, and it takes ten seconds: write your evaluation, then display its own, then look at the gap.

That gap is your progress. The engine's evaluation teaches you nothing about yourself.

**After reading:** open your last game, pick three positions, and write a numerical evaluation of each before switching anything on. Keep the note: in six months, redoing the exercise will tell you whether the gap has narrowed.
