Your heart rate has increased. Your hands are slightly sweaty. The clock ticks away and the position is difficult: not lost, but difficult. You need to calculate, yet your attention repeatedly returns to your rating.

Tournament stress is not a character flaw. You can look for a way to play with that tension rather than wait to become perfectly calm.

## What stress can change

The meta-analysis by Shields, Sazma and Yonelinas (2016) finds adverse effects of acute stress on working memory and cognitive flexibility, with more nuanced effects on inhibition. Findings vary by the conditions studied. The authors also emphasise that stress effects are not the same as the effects of administering cortisol.

This research helps explain why a task may become harder under pressure. It demonstrates neither an optimal hormonal threshold for each player nor an automatic list of chess mistakes.

At the board, you can observe your own reactions: exchanging to escape an uncomfortable position, moving quickly without checking a reply, or repeatedly returning to the consequences of defeat. These are observation prompts, not signs that diagnose a precise physiological reaction. For a comparison, see [what researchers measure during a game](/en/blog/echecs-et-le-corps/#what-researchers-measure), from heart rate to energy expenditure.

## Returning to a concrete task

You cannot control the result on every move. You can, however, choose a working question:

- What is my opponent’s threat?
- Which pieces are insufficiently defended?
- Which candidate moves address the problem?
- Which opponent reply have I forgotten?

The distinction between a task and its stakes can be a personal reference point. It does not divide players into two categories, one always succeeding under pressure and the other collapsing.

## Breathing without a magic stopwatch

Zaccaro and colleagues (2018) examine research on slow breathing and its physiological and psychological correlates. Protocols and findings are heterogeneous. Their review does not justify promising lower cortisol in a player after three cycles of 4-7-8 breathing.

If you want to try it, start outside competition: comfortable, slightly slower breathing without forcing or holding your breath. Stop if it causes discomfort. During a game, also keep the clock in view: a method meant to help should not become another constraint.

## Changing your interpretation of tension

Jamieson and colleagues (2010) study reappraisal of arousal before the GRE, a standardised examination. They observe a benefit for mathematics performance in that setting. This does not demonstrate an improvement in chess rating.

One phrase to try is: “I feel tension, and I can still examine this position.” You acknowledge the feeling while retaining an action you can take. If that wording does not help, there is no need to turn it into an obligation.

## Preparing for the tournament and reviewing the experience

Before the tournament, prepare equipment and travel to avoid extra rushing. Choose a goal you can describe afterwards, such as checking threats before moving a piece. Try your routine in training rather than discovering it in a decisive round.

After the game, distinguish how you felt from your decisions. When were you tense? Which move did you play? What options did you have? A won game can contain a rushed decision; a lost game can contain good calculation.

Experience may make some situations more familiar. It guarantees neither an absence of anxiety nor consistently lower physiological markers. You can track what changes for you without comparing yourself with champions’ accounts whose conditions you do not know.

Further reading: [resilience after a loss](/en/blog/chess-resilience/), [concentration](/en/blog/chess-and-concentration/) and [flow](/en/blog/chess-and-flow/).

## The first move after the surprise

Suppose you prepared a particular line for White before the round. On move three, your opponent exchanges a knight that was supposed to play a central role in your analysis. The surprise has a very concrete consequence. The continuation you had rehearsed concerned a piece which is no longer on the board. You can use that fact as a way back into the actual position. What did the exchange open? Which piece can recapture? Which squares have lost a defender? There is something to calculate even if the uncomfortable feeling has not gone away.

Try rehearsing this situation at the club. Ask a partner to choose a sound but unfamiliar deviation in an opening you think you know. From that point play five moves each at a comfortable time control. At the end the two players answer different questions. Your partner identifies the first moment when they felt you had created a concrete threat. You identify the first moment you found a decision you understood again. Compare the two positions on the board. You might have had a sensible plan two moves before you felt comfortable again. That difference is useful information for the next tournament: an unsettled player may still be able to find a purposeful move.

It can also go the other way. A familiar structure appears, you feel relieved, and you carry out a standard manoeuvre without noticing that a pawn has not reached the square where it usually belongs. Show the partner exactly which missing pawn move changed the line. If you make a note for later, write the square and the consequence, not just “I was too nervous”. One entry can become a future exercise. The other records how you felt, which matters, but does not yet tell you what to put on the training board.

## What the laboratory can tell a player in the hall

The review of acute stress and executive functions cited below brings together experimental tasks. A model or dataset summarising such tasks can be relevant when you ask what demands an unfamiliar position makes on attention and working memory. It does not supply a move-by-move interpretation of your scoresheet. Neither the strength of your opponent nor a number printed by your analysis engine measures your state of mind during move nineteen. A chess mistake can have a technical explanation even on a day when the player genuinely feels under pressure.

To keep that distinction in your notes, divide a page into observations made during the event and things you inferred later. “I looked at the clock three times before deciding between the two recaptures” belongs to the first side if you actually remember doing it. “I lost because I was scared of the opponent's rating” is an interpretation. Test what you can. Could you show the important difference between the two recaptures now, with ample time? If you cannot, you may have uncovered a technical difficulty to study. If you can, time pressure, attention and the help of having seen the position before are all still plausible parts of the story.

The most useful comparison may involve two games you won. In the first you felt tense but stayed aware of the opponent's threats. In the second you felt comfortable and missed a simple reply which the opponent also overlooked. A notebook that admits only losses would hide both examples. Save the position, the time remaining if known and an account of what you remember seeing before you checked the game. Leave an explicit space for uncertainty. Your future self will be in a better position to use the note if it separates observation from reconstruction.

## Shortening a routine as the clock runs down

A long routine can be sensible during a slow training game. You can compare threats, list candidates and examine several replies. In a blitz finish, trying to remember a long list can become an extra task competing with the position itself. For a training session, pick a single prompt to retain when time is low. An example is checking whether your proposed move permits an immediate check. This is deliberately narrow. It will miss other dangers. Its value is that you can assess whether it helped in this particular game, then change the prompt if the recurring problem turns out to be something else.

Do one slow trial first. At the critical moment say the opponent's possible check aloud and show its landing square. Then repeat from a neighbouring position with the time reduced. After the exercise, decide whether the missing information was the existence of the check, the safety of the king's escape square, or the piece left loose in the process. Different answers point to different work. There is no prize for maintaining a ritual which uses your last seconds without improving the decision.

Finally, separate preparations that belong outside the playing hall from decisions that have to happen at the board. Checking the route, the event rules, the round time and where to meet a teammate can settle genuine logistical uncertainties before you sit down. The opponent's third move cannot be settled in that way. If it comes as a surprise, the exchanged knight and the newly open file will give you a better starting point than trying to recreate the entire morning's preparation.

After the last round, keep one practical change for next time and one diagram for the club. The practical change might be reaching the hall early enough to find your board. The diagram might concern the first decision you understood after your opponent's deviation. These actions answer different parts of the experience. A disappointing tournament can still give you a usable improvement in each area without requiring a diagnosis or a promise about how confident you will feel at the next one.
