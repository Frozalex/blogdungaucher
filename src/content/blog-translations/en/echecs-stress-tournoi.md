Your heart rate has increased. Your hands are slightly sweaty. The clock ticks away and the position is difficult: not lost, but difficult. You need to calculate, yet your attention repeatedly returns to your rating.

Tournament stress is not a character flaw. You can look for a way to play with that tension rather than wait to become perfectly calm.

## What stress can change

The meta-analysis by Shields, Sazma and Yonelinas (2016) finds adverse effects of acute stress on working memory and cognitive flexibility, with more nuanced effects on inhibition. Findings vary by the conditions studied. The authors also emphasise that stress effects are not the same as the effects of administering cortisol.

This research helps explain why a task may become harder under pressure. It demonstrates neither an optimal hormonal threshold for each player nor an automatic list of chess mistakes.

At the board, you can observe your own reactions: exchanging to escape an uncomfortable position, moving quickly without checking a reply, or repeatedly returning to the consequences of defeat. These are observation prompts, not signs that diagnose a precise physiological reaction.

## Returning to a concrete task

You cannot control the result on every move. You can, however, choose a working question:

- What is my opponent’s threat?
- Which pieces are insufficiently defended?
- Which candidate moves address the problem?
- Which opponent reply have I forgotten?

The distinction between a task and its stakes can be a personal reference point. It does not divide players into two categories, one always succeeding under pressure and the other collapsing.

## Breathing without a magic stopwatch

Zaccaro and colleagues (2018) examine research on slow breathing and its physiological and psychological correlates. Protocols and findings are heterogeneous. Their review does not justify promising lower cortisol in a player after three cycles of 4-7-8 breathing.

If you want to try it, start outside competition: comfortable, slightly slower breathing without forcing or holding your breath. Stop if it causes discomfort. During a game, also keep the clock in view: a method meant to help should not become another constraint.

## Changing your interpretation of tension

Jamieson and colleagues (2010) study reappraisal of arousal before the GRE, a standardised examination. They observe a benefit for mathematics performance in that setting. This does not demonstrate an improvement in chess rating.

One phrase to try is: “I feel tension, and I can still examine this position.” You acknowledge the feeling while retaining an action you can take. If that wording does not help, there is no need to turn it into an obligation.

## Preparing for the tournament and reviewing the experience

Before the tournament, prepare equipment and travel to avoid extra rushing. Choose a goal you can describe afterwards, such as checking threats before moving a piece. Try your routine in training rather than discovering it in a decisive round.

After the game, distinguish how you felt from your decisions. When were you tense? Which move did you play? What options did you have? A won game can contain a rushed decision; a lost game can contain good calculation.

Experience may make some situations more familiar. It guarantees neither an absence of anxiety nor consistently lower physiological markers. You can track what changes for you without comparing yourself with champions’ accounts whose conditions you do not know.
