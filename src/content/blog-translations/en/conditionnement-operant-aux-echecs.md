You said one game. It's one in the morning and you've played seventeen.

Every time, the reasoning was the same: that one didn't really count, I'll play a clean one to finish. And with every loss, the urge to start another got stronger, not weaker.

This behavior has been described precisely, measured, and modeled. Seventy years ago, with pigeons.

## Skinner, and the Question of the Schedule

[Burrhus Frederic Skinner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operant_conditioning) formalized the principle of operant conditioning from the 1930s onward: a behavior followed by a favorable consequence becomes more frequent, a behavior followed by an unfavorable consequence becomes rarer.

Put that way, it's almost trivial. The decisive contribution lies elsewhere, and it comes from a book published in 1957 with Charles Ferster, *Schedules of Reinforcement*.

Their question: does the frequency of a behavior depend only on the reward, or also on **the rule by which it's delivered**?

The answer: the rule matters enormously. They distinguish four basic schedules.

**Fixed ratio**: the reward arrives after a fixed number of actions. One food pellet every ten presses.

**Fixed interval**: after a fixed duration. A monthly paycheck.

**Variable interval**: after an unpredictable duration. Checking your messages.

**Variable ratio**: after an unpredictable number of actions. Sometimes three, sometimes twenty-seven.

And this last schedule has two remarkable properties, both measured and reproducible.

**It produces the highest response rate.** Faster, more sustained, more constant than all the others.

**It produces the strongest resistance to extinction.** When you stop rewarding entirely, behaviors maintained by the other schedules die out fairly quickly. The one maintained by a variable ratio persists for a very long time.

The reason for that resistance follows a merciless logic: under a variable ratio, **the absence of reward is not information**. A long streak with nothing is indistinguishable from a normal unlucky streak. So there's no moment at which the animal, or you, can conclude that it isn't working anymore.

That's the principle behind slot machines. And it's also, exactly, the principle behind your blitz session.

## The Schedule Behind Online Blitz

Look at the structure, with no moral judgment.

**The result is unpredictable.** At your level, against matched opponents, you win about one game in two, without being able to predict which.

**The reward is immediate.** Three minutes, sometimes less.

**The cost of playing again is zero.** One click, no travel, no organizing, no partner to find.

**The reward is double.** The pleasure of winning, plus a number that goes up, which makes the reinforcement measurable and cumulative.

So we have an almost perfect variable ratio, with a very short delay and a zero entry cost. From the model's point of view, that's the configuration that produces the most intense behavior and the hardest to interrupt.

This isn't an accusation of malicious design. That structure isn't the result of a choice: it follows naturally from a fast competitive game where you can play one after another. Our article on [chess and addiction](/en/blog/les-echecs-et-l-addiction/) covers the clinical side, and the one on [dopamine and chess](/en/blog/echecs-et-dopamine/) the neurochemical side. What concerns us here is purely behavioral: which delivery rule, which effect on frequency.

### Why losing makes you want to play again

This point deserves to be isolated, because it looks illogical.

Under a variable ratio, a loss isn't a stop signal. It's a **normal step in the sequence**. The optimal behavior, from the schedule's point of view, is to keep going, since the reward comes after an unpredictable number of attempts and each failure statistically brings the next one closer.

Skinner's animal doesn't stop either. It speeds up.

## The Real Problem: What the Schedule Reinforces

Here's the central point of this article, and it's more disturbing than the simple question of time spent.

The reinforcement isn't contingent on the **quality of your play**. It's contingent on the **result**.

And at club level, those two things are far from equivalent. Winning also rewards, and sometimes mostly rewards:

- setting traps in objectively worse positions;
- applying pressure through the clock rather than through the position;
- exploiting crude blunders from your opponent;
- fast, superficial play that puts your opponent in time trouble.

Each of these strategies produces immediate wins. None of them produces strength in the long run.

So operant conditioning shapes your behavior **toward what produces points**, which is exactly what it's meant to do, and not at all what you wanted.

And the effect is all the more insidious because it doesn't go through a decision. You don't choose to become a trap player. You get reinforced, a few thousand times, in that direction.

It's a behavioral explanation for something every coach observes: players who rack up thousands of fast games and whose actual strength doesn't move. They aren't wasting their time, they're training efficiently. Just at something else.

## Using the Mechanism Instead of Suffering It

Skinner didn't only describe a trap: he described a tool. Three direct applications.

### 1. Move the reinforcement onto quality

This is the main lever. If the result reinforces the result, you need to create reinforcement contingent on what you want to develop.

In practice: after each game, score yourself on a **process** criterion, regardless of the result. For example, did I run my check before every important move? Did I look for the refutation of my own plan?

That kind of score holds up over two or three games a day, not seventeen. And it rewards what you want to see increase. A game lost with the protocol respected has to count as a success, otherwise the schedule keeps shaping you toward short-term victory.

### 2. Use shaping

Shaping, or shaping by successive approximations, is Skinner's other major contribution. You don't wait for the final behavior before rewarding: you reward each step that gets closer to it.

Applied to training, that means difficulty has to be calibrated to produce frequent successes. Exercises that are too hard reinforce nothing, since they produce no successes. That's also what comes out of the model of learning control described in our article on [learned helplessness in chess](/en/blog/impuissance-apprise-aux-echecs/).

### 3. Break the variable ratio with an external rule

Since the absence of a reward isn't a stop signal, no stop signal will come from the schedule itself. So it has to come from outside.

A numeric rule, decided in a calm moment, of the "three games and I stop" kind, works for a precise reason: it doesn't depend on your state or on the result. It's the same principle laid out in our article on [emotional intelligence in chess](/en/blog/intelligence-emotionnelle-aux-echecs/).

Add a physical obstacle. Ten seconds of friction is often enough to turn an impulse into a decision.

## Key Takeaways

Ferster and Skinner established in 1957 that the rule for delivering a reward matters as much as the reward itself. The variable ratio schedule, where the number of required actions is unpredictable, produces the most intense behavior and the most resistant to extinction, because no losing streak amounts to usable information.

Online blitz realizes that schedule almost perfectly: unpredictable outcome, reward in three minutes, free restart, and a number that makes the reinforcement cumulative. Hence the difficulty of stopping, and the fact that a loss increases the urge to play again instead of reducing it.

But the most expensive problem isn't the time spent. It's that the reinforcement bears on the **result** and not on the quality of the play. At club level, those two things diverge enough that thousands of games efficiently shape a player toward strategies that pay in the short term and lead nowhere.

The remedy isn't to play less out of virtue. It's to create reinforcement that bears on what you want to become.

**After reading:** choose one process criterion, just one, checkable at the end of a game. Score yourself on it over your next three games, regardless of the result. You've just created a second reinforcement schedule, and you're the one setting its rule.
