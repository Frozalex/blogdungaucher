You finish a game late. You could sleep, but a position keeps turning over in your mind. You open the analysis, then another game. The next day you struggle to keep variations in mind.

Sleep has a place in tournament preparation. Discussing it accurately means avoiding conversion of every laboratory finding into Elo points or a percentage of combinations found.

## Alertness and decisions: what studies examine

Yvonne Harrison and James Horne (2000) review the effects of sleep deprivation on decision-making. They discuss demands of complex tasks and limitations of the tests used. Their article does not concern a 20–25% decline in chess players’ calculation.

For your practice, the distinction matters: difficulty staying alert and difficulty understanding an endgame are not measured in the same way. One fatigue coefficient cannot be applied to both.

## Repeated restriction: specify the protocol

Dinges and colleagues (1997) study a week of sleep restricted to four or five hours per night. They observe deteriorating vigilance and increased sleepiness. That protocol is much more specific than “losing an hour or two over several days”.

It also does not establish a curve of errors by tournament round. Graphs showing a sudden rise in round four would turn extrapolation into observed data.

## Making real room for sleep

The CDC recommends at least seven hours for adults aged 18 to 60 and offers different guidance for other ages. Quality and regularity matter too. This is general-health advice, not a protocol guaranteeing a win.

Before a tournament, examine schedules: travel, meals, the likely end of the last round and the next morning’s start. If late analysis reduces available sleeping time, record the position and return to it later. You do not need to understand the whole game before switching off the screen.

## A nap is not an interchangeable short night

An awake break, a short nap and a longer period of sleep are different. Their effects also depend on timing, previous sleep and the task studied. This article therefore establishes no twenty- or ninety-minute rule for every player.

Nor does it promise 34% better position memory after a nap: the evidence presented does not support that chess figure. Allow time to wake up and reach the next round; avoid trying an unfamiliar arrangement on the day of an important competition.

## Learning: avoid shortcuts about sleep stages

You cannot assign every variation to deep sleep and every tactical pattern to REM sleep as if they were independent compartments. General sleep-and-memory research does not demonstrate that studying a pin in the evening guarantees recognising it faster tomorrow.

Keep a simple training method: record what you studied and return to the positions after an interval. Examine the answers you actually find rather than treating a night’s sleep as proof of learning.

## Between rounds

Brief recovery may suit you, but does not automatically replace a shortened night. Identify what you need: quiet, food, movement, conversation or rest. Avoid adding an obligation to analyse to every available interval.

If sleep difficulties recur or disrupt your days, discuss them with a health professional. A tournament schedule does not necessarily explain the whole problem.

The article on [breaks](/en/blog/benefits-of-breaks-in-chess/) covers organising awake sessions; this one concerns sleep and the limits of findings transferable to chess.

## What a study of actual tournament players can tell us

Frode Moen, Maja Olsen and Maria Hrozanova followed fourteen Norwegian chess players over a period of 120 days. This matters because it brings the question closer to real training and tournament schedules than an experiment involving a different task. It also introduces a difficulty that is easy to miss in a headline. One hundred and twenty nights from one person are not one hundred and twenty independent people. Repeated observations help describe a player's pattern, but a result still depends on the relatively small group who supplied the data. [Study and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32849090/)

The comparison related sleep patterns to changes in playing performance. A rating change reflects games against particular opponents, at particular times, from particular positions. It cannot tell you by itself whether a player failed to spot a check, forgot a line or simply met an opponent who played exceptionally well. Equally, measuring the nights around those games does not assign anyone to a sleep programme. If you report the finding to a team, keep the two observations in the sentence: a pattern in the sleep data occurred alongside a change in performance in this group. A prescription for gaining a specific number of rating points would require a different kind of evidence.

An everyday example makes the distinction easier. A player entering an international event may have a long journey, an unfamiliar room and much stronger opposition. A player enjoying a quiet month at home may have none of these. A record covering that month could pick up a real difference in both sleep and results. To identify what sleep contributed, you would need to know more about the journeys, the opponents, the training and the schedule. The point isn't that a real-world study is worthless. It tells us which questions a better comparison should record instead of assuming that all tournament weeks look alike.

## Separate a lapse of attention from a gap in knowledge

Suppose you know the procedure for mating with a rook and king, yet overlook a sideways check in an unrelated rook ending late in a tournament. That single oversight doesn't establish that you have lost your endgame knowledge. Conversely, feeling completely alert will not teach you a position you have never studied. On the analysis board ask which question failed: did you recognise the plan, calculate the reply, or notice that the last move changed a line of attack? A bad result bundles all of these possibilities together.

A useful after-round note can be very short. Write the move number, the candidate you considered and one reply to check tomorrow. That is enough to preserve the puzzle without requiring you to finish a complete analysis at night. On the following day, set the diagram up again without the engine. If you now find the reply, you have learned that you could solve it on a second attempt under different conditions. Familiarity with the question, the extra time and a different state of alertness may all have contributed. It would be overconfident to award the whole improvement to a particular night of sleep.

## Work backwards from the trip to the board

Build a schedule around the time you must leave rather than the first move on the pairing sheet. A round at nine might require arriving early to find the room, collect information or allow for an uncertain connection. Put the actual departure time on paper. Then estimate how much of the morning is needed for getting ready and eating, and prepare the bag and route beforehand where possible. This changes something concrete even if no rating point follows: there is less administrative work competing for the final minutes before departure.

Do the same for the return journey. A long evening round does not end when the clocks stop. There may be a scoresheet to check, a bus to catch, a meal and a period to wind down. If the next morning's timetable already assumes those things take no time, a plan for an extra hour of analysis is built on a missing hour. Keep two versions of the evening: one for a short game and one for a game that reaches the final session. Choose in advance which optional activity you would postpone in the latter case. Keeping a game file for tomorrow is a decision you can make before you are disappointed by today's result.

## What to learn from the end of the week

Include wins as well as losses when looking back at your schedule. If you only remember the short nights before a loss, you will overlook short nights before wins and perfectly ordinary nights before mistakes. Also distinguish exhaustion you noticed during the day from explanations you added after seeing the result. Your notes may reveal that late travel was the awkward link in the week, or that a promised analysis stream repeatedly displaced the time you had intended for rest. Either observation points to a practical change for the next competition.

Keep the goal in proportion. A timetable helps you make room for sleep and discuss recurring problems with a qualified professional if needed. It does not measure sleep stages, diagnose a disorder or assign an Elo penalty to an hour. For age-related guidance, use the public-health source below rather than a coach's guess about what a particular rating requires. For chess training, keep asking a narrower, useful question: which parts of this week can I rearrange so that the opportunity to sleep survives both a quick draw and an unexpectedly long game?

For a team event, compare the organisers' timetable with the one your players actually experienced. A round might finish on time while the last available bus has already left. Recording that mismatch gives the captain something specific to raise before the return fixture. The discussion can be about a lift, a later departure or a different meeting point, without turning one player's sleep or result into a judgement about the rest of the team.
