"You're an intuitive player. You spot tactical possibilities quickly, but you sometimes play too fast in positions that require patience. You're most comfortable when you have the initiative. Under pressure, you occasionally tend to rush."

Sound like you? Probably.

It also sounds like just about everybody. And that's exactly the point.

## Forer, 1949

The experiment is one of the most elegant in psychology, and it fits inside a single class hour.

Bertram Forer, an American psychologist, gave his 39 students what he presented as a personality assessment instrument, the "Diagnostic Interest Blank." They filled in the questionnaire conscientiously.

A week later, each received a personalized profile. Thirteen statements describing their personality. They were asked to rate, from 0 to 5, how well the description matched them.

**Average: 4.26 out of 5.**

Then Forer revealed two things. First: everyone had received **exactly the same text**. Second, and crueller: he hadn't written it. He'd assembled it by copying sentences out of an astrology book bought at a newsstand.

Among the thirteen statements were: "you have a need for other people to like and admire you, and yet you tend to be critical of yourself"; "you have a great deal of unused capacity which you have not turned to your advantage"; "disciplined and self-controlled on the outside, you tend to be worrisome and insecure on the inside."

The paper's title is perfect: "The fallacy of personal validation."

### Why It Works

Three ingredients, and they're reproducible at will.

**Two-sidedness.** Each statement contains two opposite poles, which makes it unfalsifiable. "Sociable but sometimes reserved": everyone recognizes themselves in one of the two, and nobody can be contradicted.

**Acceptable flattery.** The flaws mentioned are always presentable ones: too demanding of yourself, sometimes too impatient. Never lazy, never dishonest.

**The interpreter's labour.** This is the central mechanism, and the most counterintuitive. The text does almost nothing: **the reader supplies the precision**. When you read "you sometimes rush," you immediately go looking for an example in your memory, and you find one, because you've played thousands of games. You produced the data. The text merely opened a file.

## Playing Style, Ideal Terrain

Chess offers the technique exceptional conditions.

**The vocabulary already exists.** Attacking, positional, intuitive, technical, solid, creative. These are words chess culture has used for a century, which gives them the air of established categories when none of them has an operational definition.

**The material is infinite.** To illustrate any claim about your play, you have hundreds of games available. Whatever the sentence, the example exists.

**The subject is the most interesting one there is.** A chess player is intensely curious about his own workings, which is the reason this entire series exists.

**And the ambiguity is structural.** Nobody really knows what "positional player" means. Definitional vagueness is the Barnum effect's best ally, because it makes refutation impossible.

Look at how you manufacture a universal statement about a chess player: "you play better when you're confident," "you tend to underestimate lower-rated opponents," "you sometimes lose the thread in long games." All three apply to the entire chess-playing population, world champions included.

## The Decisive Difference: The Stock of Evidence

Here's why this article isn't just a call to scepticism.

In astrology, the problem is insoluble. No data can test whether you're "a typical Scorpio," because no measurement of being a typical Scorpio exists.

In chess, that isn't the case. **Every game you've played is recorded.**

Which means a claim about your style is, in principle, a **testable hypothesis**. And that possibility completely changes the nature of the problem.

So two things that get constantly conflated need separating very clearly.

**A statistic is a measurement.** Your win rate in rook endgames. Your average time per move between move 15 and move 25. Your results with Black against 1.e4. Your score in positions where queens come off before move 20. These are facts, calculated on your games, and they aren't Barnum.

**A prose description is an interpretation.** "Intuitive player with attacking tendencies" isn't a measurement. It's a category applied to data, often unverifiable, phrased in a register that invites recognition rather than verification.

The practical test is simple: **a statement that can't be false can't teach you anything**. Ask yourself what would make you conclude it's inaccurate. If nothing could, it's decorative.

## The Real Cost

You might judge all this harmless. A flattering profile hurts nobody.

Except that it produces two measurable effects on your improvement.

**It converts a regularity into an identity.** There's an enormous difference between "over my last 200 games, I score 41% in rook endgames" and "I'm an intuitive player, not a technical one." The first phrasing designates a project. The second designates a nature, and a nature can't be worked on: it can only be accepted.

That's the mechanism feeding the fixed beliefs described in our article on [learned helplessness in chess](/en/blog/impuissance-apprise-aux-echecs/). An identity as an "attacking player" is an excellent reason never to study endgames.

**It supplies a ready-made explanation for losses.** "I lost because it was a technical position, and I'm an intuitive player." The explanation is available, coherent, socially acceptable, and it closes the analysis. That's exactly the function described in our article on [cognitive dissonance in chess](/en/blog/dissonance-cognitive-aux-echecs/).

## The Replacement Protocol

**1. Write your profile, in three sentences.** As you'd say it to someone. "I'm more of an attacker, I'm better at tactics than endgames, I play badly against solid players."

**2. Translate each sentence into a measurement.** "Better at tactics than endgames" has to become a comparable number: for instance, your percentage of points in games that reach an endgame, against your overall percentage.

If a sentence in your profile can't be translated into a measurement, note that. It's already a result: that sentence says nothing.

**3. Go and get the numbers.** All the major platforms provide statistics by opening, by time control, by phase, by colour. It's an hour of work, once.

**4. Compare.** Some of your beliefs will be confirmed, which is useful. Others will be false, which is far more useful. Players often discover that their declared weakness isn't the real one, and that the real one appeared nowhere in their profile, because they'd never thought of it.

**5. Phrase things as projects, not identities.** Replace "I'm an intuitive player" with "I score 41% in rook endgames, against 52% overall." The second phrasing contains an objective, a measurement and a possible deadline. The first contains nothing.

## What to Take Away

Thirty-nine students rated a personality profile 4.26 out of 5, identical for all of them, copied out of an astrology book. The text wasn't describing them: it was supplying a frame vague enough for them to fill in themselves with their own memories.

Playing-style descriptions work the same way, and in chess they enjoy particularly favourable ground: an old and vague vocabulary, unlimited material, and players passionately interested in themselves.

But chess also offers the exit, and that's what separates this field from astrology: **your games are archived**. Any claim about your style is a hypothesis, and it's testable in your own data.

A sentence that can't be false teaches you nothing. A percentage does.

**After reading:** write in three sentences the player you think you are. Then go and find, for each one, the corresponding number in your statistics. The one with no number is the one you needed to throw away.
