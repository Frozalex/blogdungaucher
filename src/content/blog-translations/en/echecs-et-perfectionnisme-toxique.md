Finding a good move and understanding a mistake are part of chess’s enjoyment. Difficulty arises when you can no longer decide, stop an analysis or play a game that might be imperfect.

“Toxic” here describes the cost these demands may have for you. It is not a diagnosis, and time spent on a position is not enough to establish one.

## Noticing what costs you

You can examine three situations: endlessly repeating the same calculation, extending an analysis after it stops adding anything, or postponing a game because your preparation never feels sufficient.

Each can have several explanations. A position may require lengthy checking. Training may be postponed because you lack time. Avoid turning one episode into a personality portrait.

Instead, ask whether repeating the behaviour prevents you from doing what you wanted: playing, learning or simply enjoying the session.

## In what sense is a decision good enough?

Simon (1955) proposes a model of choice that accounts for limited computational capacity and information. Searching for an option meeting an aspiration level can replace an exhaustive search for an optimum.

This framework helps explain *satisficing*, the search for a sufficiently satisfactory option. It does not demonstrate that playing the first acceptable move is always superior to deeper calculation. A position may offer only one defence, and the chosen threshold may be too low.

Searching for an optimum, a personal preference for comparing many options and clinical perfectionism are not interchangeable concepts. Simon’s model is neither a personality test nor a treatment for perfectionism.

## Giving your calculation a question

Before revisiting a line, identify what remains uncertain. Is it the opponent’s reply? A resulting endgame? A move order? You can then check that point rather than restart the entire tree.

If recalculating teaches you nothing new, compare the cost of another minute with the time needed for the rest of the game. That requires judgement; a fixed ceiling identical for every move also risks making you play too quickly.

## Finishing a useful analysis

After a game, choose one or two moments to understand. Record a technical conclusion and, if needed, a question to revisit. You do not have to exhaust every variation for the session to have value.

If you consult the engine, ask why the line works. A sequence of unfavourable evaluations is not, by itself, analysis. Conversely, engine help does not necessarily bypass learning: it depends on how you use it.

## Returning to a manageable practice

You can try a less demanding format, share an uncertainty with a partner or set an end to a session. Treat the change as a practical experiment, then see whether it helps.

There is no need to claim that the best players are always detached from results, or that *flow*, absorption in an activity, is incompatible with all high standards. Quotations from champions do not replace examining your own experience.

If these demands become a lasting source of distress, do not respond only with another training rule. You can seek appropriate help while keeping your desire to improve.
