Finding a good move and [understanding a mistake](/en/blog/how-to-analyze-chess-games/) are part of chess’s enjoyment. Difficulty arises when you can no longer decide, stop an analysis or play a game that might be imperfect.

“Toxic” here describes the cost these demands may have for you. It is not a diagnosis, and time spent on a position is not enough to establish one.

## Noticing what costs you

You can examine three situations: endlessly repeating the same calculation, extending an analysis after it stops adding anything, or postponing a game because your preparation never feels sufficient.

Each can have several explanations. A position may require lengthy checking. Training may be postponed because you lack time. Avoid turning one episode into a personality portrait.

Instead, ask whether repeating the behaviour prevents you from doing what you wanted: playing, learning or simply enjoying the session.

## In what sense is a decision good enough?

Simon (1955) proposes a model of choice that accounts for limited computational capacity and information. Searching for an option meeting an aspiration level can replace an exhaustive search for an optimum.

This framework helps explain *satisficing*, the search for a sufficiently satisfactory option. It does not demonstrate that playing the first acceptable move is always superior to deeper calculation. A position may offer only one defence, and the chosen threshold may be too low.

Searching for an optimum, a personal preference for comparing many options and clinical perfectionism are not interchangeable concepts. Simon’s model is neither a personality test nor a treatment for perfectionism.

## Giving your calculation a question

Before revisiting a line, identify what remains uncertain. Is it the opponent’s reply? A resulting endgame? A move order? You can then check that point rather than restart the entire tree.

If recalculating teaches you nothing new, compare the cost of another minute with the time needed for the rest of the game. That requires judgement; a fixed ceiling identical for every move also risks making you play too quickly.

## Finishing a useful analysis

After a game, choose one or two moments to understand. Record a technical conclusion and, if needed, a question to revisit. You do not have to exhaust every variation for the session to have value.

If you consult the engine, ask why the line works. A sequence of unfavourable evaluations is not, by itself, analysis. Conversely, engine help does not necessarily bypass learning: it depends on how you use it.

## Returning to a manageable practice

You can try a less demanding format, share an uncertainty with a partner or set an end to a session. Treat the change as a practical experiment, then see whether it helps.

There is no need to claim that the best players are always detached from results, or that *flow*, absorption in an activity, is incompatible with all high standards. Quotations from champions do not replace examining your own experience.

If these demands become a lasting source of distress, do not respond only with another training rule. You can seek appropriate help while keeping your desire to improve.

## Work out what you would accept as an answer

Consider a question with a definite end. From the starting position, play **1.f3 e5 2.g4**. Black can give mate in one. Find the move, then give an explanation that a beginner could check on the board. The queen travels from d8 through e7, f6 and g5 to h4. Those intermediate squares are empty. From h4 it attacks the white king along g3 and f2. The white f-pawn has left f2. The queen is giving check. If you wrote only “the king is exposed,” you would have named a real weakness without establishing either the move or its consequence.

After **2...Qh4#**, consider the three kinds of reply to a sliding check. Can White capture the queen? No white piece can reach h4 legally. Can White interpose on g3 or f2? The g-pawn has already advanced to g4 and cannot return to g3. The f-pawn cannot go backwards to f2. No other piece can occupy either square in one move. Finally, can the king escape? Its own pieces and pawns occupy d1, d2, e2 and f1, while f2 lies on the queen's diagonal. You have now shown why the move is mate. There is no need to calculate what Black would do on move three; the game has ended.

The exercise provides a stopping rule because the question was narrow. If the question had been “What is a good opening for Black against an early f-pawn move?”, checking one mate would plainly be inadequate. White need not play g4. A player could spend an hour producing flawless analysis of the mating position while learning almost nothing about the other branches of the opening. On the other hand, a learner practising how to prove a checkmate can reasonably finish here. The same amount of analysis may be too much or too little depending on the problem you meant to solve.

## Where another minute might change your decision

Take a position from a game in which you lost most of your time over a single move. Set a board next to the score and reconstruct what you actually calculated. Perhaps you checked an exchange three times because you kept forgetting which rook would remain. In home practice, distinguish the rooks by naming their starting files and write down the resulting position once. Check the uncertain recapture against the diagram. If you now choose the move readily, the next improvement is a more reliable way to hold the line in mind. Simply forcing yourself to decide faster may reproduce the original confusion.

Another player may know exactly which rook will remain but keep looking for a move that preserves a pawn, prevents all checks and maintains an attack. The board may offer no such move. Give each candidate a price in plain language. One allows a check before your king reaches shelter. Another gives up the outside pawn but produces an active rook. Which concession is relevant to the position? Playing well can require choosing between two real costs. Spending another minute searching for a cost-free choice may make the clock a third cost without changing the alternatives at all.

Before investing that minute, name the discovery that would change your mind. “If Black can check on the back rank before I promote, the pawn push fails” is a useful reason to keep calculating. Trace where the check would come from and whether anything blocks the file. “There must be something more elegant” offers no comparable direction. In training you can come back to the question of elegance after selecting a move. In a tournament you must also leave time for positions that have not yet arrived. The next hard decision has a claim on the same clock.

## An annotation another person can use

Imagine handing a game to a teammate who has one evening to prepare against the same opening. A file containing every engine line you tried may be impressively large and quite difficult to use. Give the teammate the starting position, the opposing threat, your recommended response and the strongest objection you have checked. Mark any unresolved branch as unresolved. A sentence such as “I prefer the rook ending, but I have not worked out the pawn ending after the queens come off” enables the reader to decide where their own time will be best spent.

Ask the teammate to set up the position using your note alone. If they reach the wrong position because a recapture is missing, revise the note. If they reach the right one but cannot tell why your move matters, add the reply it prevents. If they can reproduce the idea and explain the remaining uncertainty, more analysis may be welcome but the original annotation is already usable. The reader gives you a practical test of whether the work is finished for this purpose. You do not have to prove that no stronger move will ever be discovered before sharing a useful piece of analysis.

A puzzle for beginners has a different audience. In the short mate above, an advanced reader will not need every escape square listed. A beginner who thinks the g-pawn can step back may need exactly that explanation. An ambitious teaching note chooses detail for a reason. Removing every possible repetition can make the explanation too terse for its reader, just as including every calculation can bury the central idea. Try the note on one person and see where they stop. That observation is a better reason to revise than a general feeling that the text ought to be perfect.

## Notice what the higher standard costs and what it buys

Compare two study sessions. In one, a higher standard prompts you to test an opponent's best defence and discover that your original line loses. Keep that part of the routine. In the other, you already understand the defence but spend the entire evening polishing variations you never use, cancel a practice game and close the notebook frustrated. The standard may need a boundary in that setting. A time limit for annotation, a named reader or a clearly stated question can supply one. Pick the boundary that addresses the actual cost you noticed.

Review the change after a few sessions. Did you share an analysis you had previously kept unfinished? Can you still explain its central position? Did the faster decision leave enough time to handle the next one? Include any real loss of quality in your answer. If the new limit repeatedly cuts off necessary checks, make room for those checks. An adjustment should let you keep what you value about careful chess while making it possible to finish a decision, close a notebook and sit down for the next game you want to play.
