A grandmaster recognises a position you still need to work through. It is tempting to imagine that a brain scan would immediately show what makes them strong. Research on players’ brains exists, but it cannot explain the origins of their expertise quite so simply.

## Observing a difference and understanding its cause

A study may compare experts with beginners and find a difference in brain structure or activity. It is then measuring an association with expertise. The groups may also differ in their histories, selection, starting ages, environments or other activities.

Establishing an effect of training would require, among other things, following changes over time and using an appropriate comparison. A correlation between a brain measurement and years of practice is not enough, by itself, to distinguish all the possible explanations.

This does not make imaging useless. It clarifies the question it answers: “Do these groups differ on this measure?” is a different question from “Did chess produce this difference?”

## The xiangqi case: check which game was studied

Li and colleagues (2015) published a multimodal MRI dataset involving 29 professional xiangqi players and 29 beginners. Xiangqi is the game often called Chinese chess; these participants should not be presented as a sample of experts in Western chess.

The work makes structural, diffusion and resting-state images available, alongside participant characteristics. It is a resource for studying expertise, not a trial in which beginners were randomly assigned to different training programmes.

Another precaution: several publications may use the same dataset. They do not then represent as many independent replications. Before counting “studies that confirm” a finding, check the participants, the game and whether data have been reused.

## Executive functions: what Ramos actually shows

Ramos, Arán Filippetti and Krumm (2018) compare 65 children aged 8 to 12: 30 play chess and 35 have no experience of it. The authors report better performance in the chess group on the executive functions assessed.

The design is cross-sectional and *ex post facto*: the groups existed before measurement. This is neither a ten-week training intervention nor random assignment. We therefore cannot conclude that chess alone created the observed difference.

The reference year to retain is 2018, the year of the journal issue and publication indicated by the journal, even though the manuscript circulated in 2017. The coauthor’s name is Arán Filippetti, not “Ar”.

## From improving at chess to improving elsewhere

You can learn to spot a fork without becoming better at every attention task. This distinction between specialised learning and far transfer is central to the review by Sala and Gobet (2017).

Their review examines findings from chess, music and working-memory training. Conclusions about transfer become less favourable when experiments better control expectations and the activities offered to other groups.

A group receiving chess lessons also benefits from an adult, a structured setting and a new activity. Comparing it only with a group receiving no additional activity leaves those explanations open. An active control helps distinguish them.

It would therefore be premature to promise a general increase in intelligence, better emotional control or a “stronger prefrontal cortex”. For links with school learning, see [the article on chess and mathematics](/en/blog/chess-and-mathematics/).

## Ageing: an activity, not a guarantee

Playing can give you a stimulating activity and an opportunity to meet other people. That does not prove that chess prevents Alzheimer’s disease.

The WHO dementia fact sheet presents several modifiable risk factors and notes that there is no cure for dementia. It does not allow us to attribute a quantified reduction in an individual’s risk to chess. Confusing an association between leisure activities and health with a preventive prescription would be misleading.

## How to read the next study

Before remembering a spectacular number, ask four questions: which game do the participants play? How were the groups formed? Does the measurement concern a task, a brain image or everyday life? Has the finding been replicated in other people?

For your training, keep observable goals: explaining a position better, remembering a pattern, checking a threat before moving. You can appreciate those improvements without making them carry a promise of general brain transformation.

## What a brain scan can tell you about a move

Suppose you read that two groups differ on an MRI measure. Which measurement was it? A structural scan estimates properties of anatomy. Diffusion imaging measures how water moves through tissue. Functional MRI tracks changes related to blood oxygenation over time. A brighter patch on a contrast map cannot tell you that a player has just spotted mate. To connect a measurement to a decision, you need to know the task, the comparison and how the players performed. The resting-state scans in the xiangqi dataset were acquired without a move-selection task.

## What does an experienced player look at?

In an [eye-tracking experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11913751/), experts and intermediate players chose a move in each of five positions. Where a player looked gave researchers a way to examine which information attracted attention. A fixation does not reveal the variation the player calculated.

**Try this constructed example.** Play 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5. Cover the board. Which pawn in each camp is attacked by a bishop and defended only by its king? The bishop on c4 looks along d5 and e6 towards f7. Its counterpart on c5 looks along d4 and e3 towards f2. Remembering those relationships lets you reconstruct the threats even if you forget where an unrelated pawn stands. Count the attackers before sacrificing: the bishop alone has no second attacker on its target.

## How would you test whether lessons caused an improvement?

Imagine that children in a chess club score higher on an attention test than children outside the club. That comparison leaves open who chose to join. Assigning volunteers at random to lessons or a waiting list addresses that question, but only the first group gets a teacher and a new group activity. A stronger test would give both groups equally demanding activities with comparable teaching time, measure both groups before and after, and specify the outcome in advance. If the claim concerns mathematics, test mathematics directly.

You can use the same precision in your own practice. Before consulting an engine, write down your opponent’s threat at three turning points in one game. If you overlooked a bishop’s line, find a different position with that motif and explain the threat before moving. Recalling yesterday’s answer and recognising the same relationship on a new board are two different achievements.

## Reconstruct the decision behind a correct answer

Two players can find the same move by quite different routes. Set up a board with both kings on their starting squares, a white knight on c7 and a black rook on a8. Leave the other squares empty. The knight attacks the king on e8 and the rook on a8. Black must respond to the check. In this simplified diagram moving the king to d7 is legal, after which White can capture the rook on a8. The point of the exercise is small and visible: a player can learn to recognise the geometry of two knight attacks without searching every legal move.

Now put a white pawn on c6, return the king to e8, and ask what changed. The original knight attacks exactly the same squares. The king still has moves, but no longer has the specific escape d7, which the pawn now covers. Someone repeating the first line from memory will get the geometry of the fork right and the defence wrong. Someone who traces each relevant attack afresh may reach a better answer while taking longer. Measuring only how fast the player says “fork” would hide this difference.

Use two columns on a piece of paper: “What I recognised” and “What I checked.” A player can write “knight attacks king and rook” in the first column and “is the proposed king move legal?” in the second. That small separation tells a coach more about the missed reply than a general statement about concentration. It also shows how a training task differs from a controlled scientific study. We intentionally gave away the pattern in the question. An experiment about spontaneous recognition would have to consider how much the wording disclosed and which other patterns the participants expected.

## Three conclusions from the same training evening

Imagine a learner solves the diagram immediately after the demonstration, solves it again next week, and then solves a fresh problem with the same geometry on different squares. The first result could reflect a memory of the instruction. The second tells you the answer survived a delay. The third provides a little evidence that the learner can reuse the relation in another chess position. None of these tasks measures performance in the classroom or ability to navigate an unfamiliar city. Each conclusion is tied to the question that was actually asked.

To make this a useful exercise for your own games, find a missed double attack in your score sheet and redraw it from the opponent's side. Ask a partner to alter a single defender or escape square and keep the change secret until you start. After you answer, put the old and new diagrams next to each other. Name the exact relation the change broke. That is a concrete piece of chess learning you can describe without guessing which part of your brain produced it.
