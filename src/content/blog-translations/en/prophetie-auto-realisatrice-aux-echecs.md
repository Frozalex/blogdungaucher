You don't work on endgames.

Not out of laziness: you own a book, you've opened it twice. It's just that you know it isn't your thing. You're a middlegame player, always have been. Endgames, you don't get them.

So when a position starts heading toward an endgame, you offer a draw a bit early. You trade more reluctantly. You play faster once only rooks are left, because what's the point anyway.

Three years later, your statistics are unambiguous: you really are bad in endgames.

The belief was right. It was right because it was believed.

## Merton, 1948

The concept was framed by a sociologist, [Robert Merton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-fulfilling_prophecy), in a paper published in 1948 in the *Antioch Review*.

His definition fits in one sentence: a self-fulfilling prophecy is **an initially false definition of the situation, which evokes new behavior that makes the definition true**.

His canonical example is a solvent bank. A rumor of insolvency spreads. Worried customers withdraw their deposits. The bank, unable to cover mass withdrawals, goes under. The rumor was false at the start, it's true at the end, and everyone concludes that the people who pulled their money out had seen clearly.

That last point is the heart of it: **the loop produces its own confirmation**. Not only does the belief come true, it looks in hindsight like insight.

## The Pygmalion Effect, and Its Problems

The most famous experimental branch dates from 1968. Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson published *Pygmalion in the Classroom*.

The protocol: in an elementary school, students take a test, then teachers are told which children are about to go through a significant intellectual growth spurt. In reality, those children were **picked at random**. The only thing distinguishing them is the expectation planted in the teacher.

Reported result: those students gained more on the intelligence test by the end of the year.

The book had enormous reach and became a pillar of teacher training worldwide.

### Except

The criticism arrived immediately, and it was technical.

As early as 1968, Robert Thorndike, a psychometrics specialist, published a review showing that **the instrument used to measure intelligence was seriously defective**. Some baseline scores were implausible, so low that small variations mechanically produced spectacular "gains."

In 1971, Janet Elashoff and Richard Snow reanalyzed the data and showed that the reported effect **rested on a handful of extreme scores concentrated in one or two classrooms**. Take those out and the effect largely vanishes.

And in 1984, Stephen Raudenbush published the most useful synthesis: a meta-analysis of **eighteen experiments** that had manipulated teacher expectations and then measured students' IQ.

Average effect: **d = 0.11**. That's small. Well below what's usually considered a meaningful educational intervention.

## The Moderator, and This Is Where It Gets Interesting

Raudenbush didn't stop at the average figure. He looked for what separated the studies where an effect showed up from those where nothing happened.

His finding is decisive: **the effect depends on when the expectation is installed**.

When the false information reached the teacher **within the first two weeks** of meeting the student, the effect was strongest. When it arrived later, once the teacher had already had time to observe the student firsthand, the effect collapsed.

Restated, that gives a general rule that holds far beyond the classroom:

**An expectation only bites where no independent information exists to contradict it.**

A teacher who has watched a student work for two months has their own data. The label slides off it. A teacher who has observed nothing yet has only the label, and the label organizes their perception.

That's exactly the situation you're in with your own supposed weaknesses.

## Why Chess Is Fertile Ground

Apply Raudenbush's moderator to your case.

What independent information do you have about your actual endgame level?

Almost none. You have memories, and our article on [false memories in chess](/en/blog/faux-souvenirs-aux-echecs/) covered what those are worth. You have a few memorable losses, selected for being memorable rather than representative, which is [survivorship bias](/en/blog/biais-du-survivant-aux-echecs/) applied to your own memory.

What you don't have is the number.

And the irony is complete, because that number **exists**. It's in your statistics, three clicks away. You've simply never looked at it, because you had no need to look: you already knew.

That's why chess is such fertile ground for self-fulfilling prophecies. Not because the information is missing, but because it's **available and unconsulted**. The belief occupies the seat the measurement should have taken.

## The Three Loops

### 1. Avoiding the work

The most direct one. "I'm hopeless at X" leads to not working on X. The lack of work produces the weakness. The weakness confirms the belief.

What makes this loop so stable is that it's **rational at every step**. Spending your limited time on what you do well rather than what you do badly looks like sensible allocation. Only when you add up three years of reasonable decisions do you get the result.

### 2. Avoiding it during the game

Subtler, and more expensive in the short term.

If you believe you play closed positions badly, you'll dodge them in your move choices. You'll take a slightly inferior option to stay out of them. You'll simplify too early, or not enough.

Those choices genuinely degrade your position, which produces bad results, which confirms that you play that type of position badly. When the bad result came from the detour, not the structure.

### 3. Asymmetric interpretation

The most insidious one, because it changes no behavior, only how facts get read.

You lose an endgame: that confirms it. You win an endgame: your opponent played badly, or you got lucky. No data can contradict the belief, since the sorting happens on the way in.

This is [confirmation bias](/en/blog/biais-de-confirmation-aux-echecs/) turned on yourself, and it's what makes lived experience so uninformative.

## The Reverse Version Exists Too

The other direction deserves a mention, less discussed and entirely real.

"I'm an attacking player" produces exactly the same loop with the opposite sign. You look for attacks, you take risks, you play open positions, and you do genuinely get better at attacking. The prophecy comes true, and its result is positive.

The cost sits elsewhere: in everything the identity excluded. An attacking player who never works on technique hits a ceiling, and that ceiling is produced by the same belief that drove the improvement.

That's also why style profiles, covered in our article on [the Barnum effect in chess](/en/blog/effet-barnum-aux-echecs/), aren't harmless: a description turns into a program.

## The Protocol

### 1. Write down your prophecies

List the sentences you say about your own game. "I'm hopeless in endgames." "I play badly against solid players." "I fall apart in time trouble." "I don't understand closed positions at all."

Three to five sentences is enough. They come fast, which is already a clue.

### 2. Translate each one into a measurement

Every sentence is a hypothesis. Make it testable.

"Hopeless in endgames" becomes: my score percentage in games going past move 40, compared to my overall percentage. "I fall apart in time trouble" becomes: my rate of serious errors in the last ten moves when I have under five minutes left.

### 3. Go get the numbers

This is the decisive step, and it's exactly the counter-signal Raudenbush's moderator points to: **the independent information that keeps the expectation from working**.

The results are often surprising. Plenty of players discover that their declared weakness is average, and that the real one is somewhere else, in an area they'd never mentioned because they'd never thought about it.

### 4. Change the wording

A belief phrased as an identity can't be attacked. Phrased as a measurement, it becomes a project.

"I'm hopeless in endgames" calls for no action. "I score 43% in rook endgames against 51% overall" points to a piece of work, a target, and a deadline.

### 5. Force yourself into the avoided territory

Thirty games where you don't run from the structure you dread. The goal isn't to win, it's to **produce data** in a zone where you've never produced any. It's the same principle as the learning of control described in our article on [learned helplessness in chess](/en/blog/impuissance-apprise-aux-echecs/).

## What to Take Away

Merton described the mechanism in 1948: a false definition of the situation produces conduct that makes it true, and looks like clear-sightedness after the fact.

The Pygmalion effect, its most famous demonstration, is far shakier than the story suggests. The 1968 study rested on a defective instrument and on a few extreme scores, and the meta-analysis of eighteen experiments puts the average effect at 0.11.

But it handed over the most useful key along the way: **the expectancy effect only operates where independent information is missing**. Once you have clean data, the label slides off.

That's precisely the state of your beliefs about your own game. They hold not because they're true, and not because you're gullible. They hold because you've never looked at the number that's sitting right there in your statistics.

**After reading:** write down the sentence you repeat most often about your own weakness, then go find the matching percentage. It's ten minutes, and it's the only move that breaks the loop.
