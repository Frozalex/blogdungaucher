You know someone who quit chess.

Not someone who disliked it: someone who gave it years, reached a decent level, and one day just stopped opening the app. Usually with no explicit decision. They simply stopped.

Ask them why and the answer is vague. No time, other priorities, tired of it.

There's a framework that makes those dropouts predictable, and that also explains why some people come back ten years later.

## Three Needs

From the 1980s onward, Edward Deci and Richard Ryan developed a theory of motivation that became the standard in educational and sport psychology.

Their central proposal: lasting motivation doesn't depend on the strength of the desire or the size of the reward. It depends on the satisfaction of **three psychological needs**.

**Autonomy.** The sense of being the origin of your own actions. Not independence, but the fact that what you do comes from you rather than from a constraint.

**Competence.** The sense of being effective, of progressing, of having a grip on the outcome.

**Relatedness.** The sense of being connected to others, of mattering to someone, of belonging.

The strong claim of the theory is that these three needs are **necessary together**. An activity that satisfies two out of three can run for a long time, but it's fragile, and its abandonment will look inexplicable to the person abandoning it, since nothing degraded on the side of the other two.

### What Holds Up and What Gets Argued About

The usual caveat has to go in here.

The best-supported empirical strand concerns **autonomy**. The link between an environment that supports autonomy and persistence in an activity is documented across many contexts: schools, sport, health, work.

The most contested strand is the claim that these three needs are **fundamental and universal**. That proposal is hard to falsify, since you can almost always reframe an awkward case in terms of an unmet need. Its cross-cultural reach has been debated too, particularly on autonomy, whose expression varies with context.

So take the framework as **a useful diagnostic grid** rather than as a theory of human needs. That's already plenty, and it's what we need here.

One more boundary worth drawing, with the previous article in this series. [Intrinsic motivation](/en/blog/motivation-intrinseque-aux-echecs/) dealt with a precise mechanism, a reward substituting for a reason to act. Here we're dealing with the general framework that describes what sustains a practice over time.

## Chess, Need by Need

Apply the grid, and the result is a lot more clear-cut than you'd expect.

### Autonomy: Excellent

Recreational chess satisfies this need almost perfectly, especially online.

You choose when you play, against whom, at what time control, with which openings, on which study plan. Nobody imposes anything. No institution grades you, no obligation weighs on you.

It's one of the most autonomous activities there is. Nothing to book, nobody to convince, no equipment required.

So this need is almost never the cause of an adult player quitting. It can be for a child pushed by their parents, which is a different configuration, covered in our article on [attachment theory in chess](/en/blog/theorie-de-l-attachement-aux-echecs/).

### Competence: Ambivalent

Here the picture gets complicated, and that's the interesting part.

Chess offers the best feedback of any hobby: an objective rating, immediate and precise. As long as it climbs, the competence need is fed remarkably well.

But that same mechanism turns hostile the moment progress stops. A plateau isn't experienced as an absence of information: it's experienced as **permanent negative information**, public and quantified.

And a rating is structurally a poor learning signal, for the reasons laid out in our article on [learned helplessness in chess](/en/blog/impuissance-apprise-aux-echecs/): slow, noisy, non-attributable. It informs you very well about the result and very badly about progress.

Hence a prediction: the competence need is **overfed during progress and attacked during a plateau**, with no middle state. That's a risky trajectory.

### Relatedness: And This Is Where It All Plays Out

Here's the central point of this article.

Chess is, structurally, a solitary activity. Our article on [loneliness in chess](/en/blog/echecs-et-la-solitude/) makes the point: by the rules you're alone, no teammate, no coach during the game.

And online play, which today accounts for most of the practice, removed what little was left.

You play a username. You don't talk. You'll never meet again, and if you did, neither of you would know. Nothing in the setup builds a relationship.

Put another way: **online chess is excellent on autonomy, good then bad on competence, and close to zero on relatedness**.

Two needs out of three, with the third absent by design. That's exactly the configuration the theory describes as fragile.

## What the Framework Predicts

And the predictions match phenomena clubs and federations know well.

**The silent dropout.** Someone who stops can't explain why, because nothing degraded on the autonomy side and competence looks like a technical problem. The missing need is the one whose absence goes unnoticed, never having been satisfied in the first place.

**Quitting after a plateau.** When the second need gives way, only autonomy is left, and autonomy alone isn't enough to sustain a practice.

**The return to the club.** Plenty of players who pick the game back up after years come back **to a club** rather than online, and often say it isn't the same thing. The framework explains that without mystery: a club is bad on autonomy, constrained by a schedule and a place, and it's the only thing that satisfies relatedness.

**The paradox of very active players who fade out.** Thousands of online games can feed two needs intensely and never touch a third. Volume doesn't compensate for absence, and it's one of the roads to [burnout in chess](/en/blog/burnout-chess/).

## The Protocol

**1. Run the three-box diagnosis.** Score yourself 0 to 5 on each need for this month. Autonomy: am I really choosing what I do? Competence: do I perceive progress I can attribute to something? Relatedness: does anyone know what I'm playing?

The lowest box is your work site. It's almost never the one you assumed.

**2. Fix relatedness first, it's the cheapest.** It's the most neglected need and the one whose satisfaction costs the least effort. A regular analysis partner is enough. One person you send your games to, who sends you theirs.

This isn't social consolation: it's what turns a loss into a shared object rather than a private balance sheet.

**3. Fix competence through the signal, not the result.** A rating plateau isn't an absence of progress, it's an absence of progress measurement. Adopt a short-loop indicator, as detailed in our article on [learned helplessness in chess](/en/blog/impuissance-apprise-aux-echecs/).

**4. Protect autonomy when you add structure.** A rigid training program can degrade the one need your practice was satisfying well. Keep a share of play with no objective, chosen on the spot.

**5. If you coach players, look at relatedness.** For a club or a coach it's the highest-return lever, because it's the only one online play doesn't supply and therefore the only one that makes a club irreplaceable.

## What to Take Away

Deci and Ryan propose that lasting motivation depends on three needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness. The framework is solidly supported on autonomy, more debatable in its claim to universality, and very useful as a diagnostic grid.

Applied to chess, it gives a clean result. Autonomy is almost perfectly satisfied. Competence is overfed while the rating climbs and attacked as soon as it stalls. Relatedness sits close to zero in online play, by design rather than by accident.

Which explains the characteristic shape of the dropouts: they happen after a plateau, with no reason the person quitting can identify, and they often hit very active players.

And it points to the highest-return lever, which has nothing to do with training: find someone to tell your games to.

**After reading:** score your three needs out of 5. Then work only on the lowest one for a month. There's a good chance it isn't the one you were working on.
