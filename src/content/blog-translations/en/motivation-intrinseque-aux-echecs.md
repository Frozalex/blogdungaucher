Think back to your first games. Before the apps, before the rating, before the statistics.

You played because it was beautiful, or because you wanted to beat your brother, or because you liked the position. There was no number.

Today you open the app and you know before you play how many points are at stake. After the game, you look at the graph before you look at the position.

The question in this article is whether the number replaced something. The answer hinges on an experimental detail that almost every summary leaves out.

## The Marker Experiment

In 1973, Mark Lepper, David Greene and Richard Nisbett published in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* a study that would launch an entire field.

The setup takes place in a nursery school. The researchers first observe the children during free play periods, and identify those who **draw spontaneously** with markers, without being asked.

Those children are then split into three groups.

The first are **promised a reward** for drawing: a nice certificate with a ribbon. The second draw and receive **the same reward without expecting it**, as a surprise. The third draw and **receive nothing**.

One to two weeks later, markers are put out again during a free play period, with no announcement, and the time each child spends drawing is measured.

Result: the children in the **first group**, the ones who'd expected a reward, draw about **half as much** as the others. The two other groups are indistinguishable.

The interpretation became famous under the name **overjustification effect**. The child who drew because they liked it now has another explanation available: they drew for the certificate. When the certificate disappears, the reason to draw disappears with it.

## The Condition Everyone Drops

Here's the most important point in this article.

The final sample was **51 children**, and they hadn't been picked at random. They'd been **selected** on an explicit criterion: prior observation had to establish that they already found the activity **intrinsically interesting**.

That condition is almost always missing from popular summaries, and leaving it out changes everything.

What the study demonstrates isn't that rewards demotivate. It demonstrates that rewards **degrade pre-existing intrinsic motivation**.

That's a far narrower claim, and a far more useful one, because it immediately tells you who it applies to. For a child who didn't like drawing, there's nothing to destroy. The reward may even be the only thing that gets them to try.

## What the Meta-Analysis Established

The field then went through a serious controversy. In 1994, Judy Cameron and David Pierce published a meta-analysis concluding that the effect was weak and limited. In 1999, Edward Deci, Richard Koestner and Richard Ryan replied with a meta-analysis covering **128 studies**, arguing that the previous one was methodologically flawed.

Their conclusions, which are today widely treated as the reference, are finer-grained than the slogan:

**Expected tangible rewards degrade intrinsic motivation.** Whether they're tied to simply taking part, to completing the task, or to the level of performance.

**Unexpected rewards don't produce that effect.** Since they can't become the reason for acting, not being known in advance.

**Positive verbal feedback doesn't degrade motivation**, and can strengthen it. A comment on what was done well informs you about your competence without standing in for your reason to act.

That last distinction is the most usable one: what matters is less the reward than **the function it occupies**. Information about your competence feeds motivation. Payment for your activity replaces it.

## The Case of the Rating

Now apply all of that to Elo, keeping Lepper's condition in mind.

A rating has several properties of an expected external reward. It's **known in advance**: you know how many points are at stake. It's **contingent on performance**: it depends only on the result. It's **salient**: displayed everywhere, updated instantly. And it's **quantified**, which makes it directly comparable from one session to the next.

According to the model, that's exactly the profile that degrades intrinsic motivation.

But the 1973 condition imposes a decisive nuance, and it's **biographical**:

**If you played for the game before you discovered the rating**, there's a prior motivation that could be replaced. The mechanism can operate.

**If the rating is what brought you to chess**, there's nothing to degrade. The number didn't replace your enjoyment, it was your way in. And it can perfectly well stay that way with no damage done.

So the question isn't "is Elo harmful," which has no general answer. It's: **what were you doing before, and are you still doing it?**

### The Signs of Replacement

Three fairly reliable indicators.

**You no longer replay the positions you enjoyed.** There was a time when a beautiful endgame kept you at the board for ten minutes after the game. Now you move on to the next one.

**A win without points doesn't count.** A friendly, unrated game feels empty, as if it hadn't happened.

**You avoid stronger opponents.** Not out of fear of losing the game, but out of fear of losing points. That's the clearest sign, because it leads you to avoid exactly the games that teach you the most. ## Four Practices

**Keep a space with nothing at stake.** One unrated game a week, or a study session on a position simply because it's beautiful. The goal isn't to improve but to keep alive a reason to act that doesn't depend on the number.

**Shift your feedback from result to content.** This is the best-established distinction in the field. After a game, note what you played well, not what you won. Informative feedback feeds motivation; a numbered payoff replaces it.

**Reduce the salience, not the number.** The problem isn't that the rating exists, it's that it's on display all the time. That's also the conclusion of our article on [social comparison in chess](/en/blog/comparaison-sociale-aux-echecs/): how often you check matters more than the value.

**Find what brought you here.** Plenty of players arrived through a book, a famous game, a film, a person. Coming back to that thing occasionally sustains a motivation the rating can't supply.

## Key Takeaways

The 1973 experiment shows that promising a reward for an activity you enjoy reduces how much time you later spend on it spontaneously. But it shows this on 51 children **selected because they already liked the activity**, a condition almost always cut from summaries.

That condition is what makes the result usable, because it tells you who it's for. The meta-analysis of 128 studies fills in the rest: it's expected tangible rewards that degrade motivation, not verbal feedback, which strengthens it.

The Elo rating has the profile of an expected external reward. But whether it has cost you anything isn't a question of general psychology: it's a question about your own history. It can only have replaced what existed before it.

**After reading:** try to remember why you started playing chess, before any rating. If you find the reason, just check that it's still somewhere in how you play today.
