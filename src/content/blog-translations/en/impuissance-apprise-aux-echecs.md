You've been at 1,550 for fourteen months. You've tried things. You bought a book, you did puzzles for a few weeks, you switched openings.

The number doesn't move.

So you keep playing, but differently. You resign earlier. You no longer bother hunting for the defensive resource in tough positions. You've started saying, half joking, that you've hit your level.

There's a name for this state. What's interesting is that the man who named it published a paper fifty years later explaining that he'd been wrong about the mechanism, and that what you should actually do is close to the opposite of what everyone believed.

## The 1967 Experiment

In 1967, [Martin Seligman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learned_helplessness) and Steven Maier, then at the University of Pennsylvania, ran an experiment that became a classic.

The setup had three groups, which matters and is often forgotten.

The first group of animals received mild electric shocks they **could stop** through an action. The second received exactly the same shocks, same duration and same intensity, but **with no way to stop them**: their situation was yoked to the first group's. The third received nothing.

That detail is what gives the protocol its value: the first two groups received the same amount of shock. The only difference was **control**.

Afterward, all the animals were placed in a new situation where clearing a low barrier was enough to avoid the shock. It's easy, and it's visible.

Animals from the first and third groups quickly learned to cross the barrier. Those from the second group, in large proportion, **didn't even try**. They stayed put, and they took it.

Seligman drew a theory from this: the organism had **learned** that its actions had no effect, and it generalized that learning to a situation where its actions would have worked. The concept was later extended to human depression and became one of the most influential in twentieth-century psychology.

## The 2016 Reversal

In July 2016, Steven Maier and Martin Seligman published a paper in *Psychological Review* titled "Learned Helplessness at Fifty: Insights from Neuroscience."

Fifty years of neuroscience had made it possible to map the biological mechanism. And their conclusion leaves no room for doubt: **the original theory had the mechanism backwards.**

Here's what the data show.

Passivity in the face of prolonged aversive events **is not learned**. It's the mammal's default, unlearned response. It's produced by serotonergic activity in the dorsal raphe nucleus, in the brainstem, which inhibits escape behavior.

Put differently: the animal in the second group learned nothing. It simply let a response that had been there all along express itself.

What gets learned is **the opposite**. The medial prefrontal cortex detects the presence of effective control over the situation, and when it detects it, it **automatically inhibits** the dorsal raphe response. The first group didn't escape helplessness: it learned to detect that it had a hand in things, and that detection switched off the default passivity.

Stated simply: **you don't learn helplessness, you learn control**. And when that learning doesn't happen, what's left is the baseline state.

## Why This Reversal Changes Everything

This isn't an academic subtlety. The two versions lead to opposite interventions.

**In the old version**, a stuck player has learned something false, namely that their efforts have no effect. So the fix is to **unlearn** it: convince them, re-motivate them, restore their confidence. That's the register of talk and persuasion, and it's exactly what a coach does when they tell a student not to get discouraged.

**In the new version**, there's nothing to unlearn. The player is simply in the default state, because the control-detection circuit **detected nothing**. So the fix isn't to convince them: it's to give them situations where control is **effective, visible, and attributable**.

Which shifts the question entirely. It's no longer "how do I find my motivation again," which is a matter of willpower. It becomes **"where is the feedback that would let me detect that my actions have an effect"**, which is a matter of design.

And framed that way, the question has a very concrete answer in chess.

## The Structural Problem with Improvement

Look at the main feedback signal a player has: rating.

**It's slow.** Several weeks before a change of method becomes visible.

**It's noisy.** Normal fluctuation swamps the effect of a month of serious work. A good streak and a bad streak look alike, statistically.

**It depends on someone else.** Your result depends as much on your opponent's form as on yours.

**It isn't attributable.** When it goes up, you don't know what worked. When it drops, you don't know what failed.

In other words, rating has **every property of a signal from which no control can be extracted**. That's exactly the condition of Seligman's second group: events happen, they're real, and the link to your action is undetectable.

Which suggests a counterintuitive conclusion: **prolonged stagnation isn't primarily a training-method problem, it's a measurement problem**. The work may well be producing effects, but inside a signal where they're undetectable. And an undetectable effect doesn't trigger the learning of control.

## The Three Forms in Chess

**Early resignation.** The most visible one. You resign a difficult but defensible position, then you'll explain that it was lost. The thing to notice is that you didn't look for the resource: you didn't cross the low barrier.

**Effort collapsing mid-session.** After a loss, the following games get played at half power. This isn't tilt in the anger sense, described in our article on [chess and anger](/en/blog/echecs-et-colere-ragequit/): it's colder, more resigned. The behavior is still there, the investment isn't.

**Frozen beliefs.** "I'm hopeless in endgames." "I don't understand closed positions at all." These sentences are zones declared out of reach, therefore never worked on, which makes them true. They also work as self-fulfilling prophecies, which is the subject of an upcoming article in this series.

## The Protocol, Derived from the Right Mechanism

The whole logic of what follows fits in one sentence: **give the brain repeated chances to detect effective control**. Not encouragement.

### 1. Change the signal

This is the main lever. Temporarily drop rating as your measure of progress, and adopt short-loop indicators, where the link between what you do and the result is immediate and attributable.

A tactical puzzle solved or missed is a verdict in fifteen seconds, with no opponent, no luck, no noise. A theoretical endgame learned and then tested on ten positions gives you a score that nothing else explains. A pattern you studied and then recognized in a game is a direct link between an action and an effect.

These aren't consolation prizes for a discouraged player. From the standpoint of the 2016 mechanism, they're the **only usable signals** for the circuit that has to learn control.

### 2. Make progress attributable

Progress whose cause you don't know teaches nothing. Work on one thing at a time, long enough that the result can be pinned on it, and write down what you're working on and what you're measuring.

That's also what separates a training plan from a pile of content.

### 3. Ban yourself from resigning for a month

A mechanical rule: for thirty games, you're not allowed to resign. You play until mate or until your opponent offers a draw.

The goal isn't to save lost games, it's to **produce cases where you see that a position you held can swing**. Every game rescued from a losing position is a detection of control, in the exact sense of the model. And there will be some, because at your level opponents go wrong too.

### 4. Treat frozen beliefs as testable hypotheses

"I'm hopeless in endgames" is a checkable claim. Go find your actual percentage in rook endgames. It's often less bad than the belief, and sometimes it's accurate, in which case you're holding a work plan instead of an identity.

## What to Take Away

The 1967 experiment still holds: for an equal amount of shock, what separates the two groups is control, and the absence of control produces a passivity that generalizes.

But the explanation was overturned by its own authors. Passivity isn't learned, it's the mammal's default state when facing prolonged aversive events. What gets learned is the detection of control, which then inhibits that state.

The practical consequence is clear and it contradicts intuition. A stuck player doesn't need convincing: they need situations where the link between their action and the result is **short, sharp, and attributable**. And rating, the signal everybody stares at, is precisely the worst possible signal for that: slow, noisy, dependent on others, and not attributable.

Changing signal isn't lying to yourself about your progress. It's giving the right circuit information it can actually process.

**After reading:** pick one short-loop indicator for the next thirty days, just one thing, measurable in under a minute. Rating, you'll look at in March.
