Balanced position, middlegame. You see an idea: give up a pawn to open the b-file and tie down his light-squared bishop. The evaluation says it's good. Your intuition says it's good.

You don't play it.

You play the solid move, you keep your pawn, and fifty moves later you lose a slightly inferior endgame.

That isn't a calculation error. It's an asymmetry in how your brain handles what gets added and what gets taken away.

## What Kahneman and Tversky Proposed

In 1979, [Daniel Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman) and Amos Tversky published a paper in *Econometrica* that would restructure behavioural economics: "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk."

Their target was the standard model of rational decision-making, in which an agent evaluates options according to the final wealth they produce. Kahneman and Tversky proposed something quite different.

**People don't evaluate states, they evaluate changes** relative to a reference point.

And above all: the function linking those changes to their psychological value is **not symmetrical**. It's steeper on the loss side than on the gain side. Losing a hundred euros produces a stronger effect than gaining a hundred euros.

The ratio commonly cited is roughly **two to one**, a figure that made its fortune far beyond the field.

Two consequences follow directly from this asymmetry, and both are visible on a chessboard.

**The reference point determines everything.** The same position can be a gain or a loss depending on what you compare it to. The question "how does the reference point get set" becomes central.

**People take risks to avoid a loss.** In the domain of gains you're fairly cautious; in the domain of losses you become a gambler, because a chance of cancelling the loss beats the certain loss.

## The Critique, Which Is Serious

This has to be said immediately, because the two-to-one figure circulates like a constant of nature.

In 2018, David Gal and Derek Rucker published a paper in the *Journal of Consumer Psychology* under an ironic title: "The Loss of Loss Aversion: Will It Loom Larger Than Its Gain?"

Their review concludes that **the available data don't establish a general tendency for losses to weigh more than gains**. On their account everything depends on context: across populations, stakes, the nature of the good and the situation, the asymmetry appears, disappears, or reverses.

They also point out that the empirical base for the famous two-to-one ratio is thinner than its cultural usage suggests: it comes from limited samples faced with hypothetical choices.

The paper set off a lively debate, and several responses defended a **contingent** version of loss aversion: it exists, but under conditions, and those conditions have to be specified.

That's the honest position today. **The asymmetry isn't a universal law with a fixed coefficient. It's a contextual phenomenon**, and the useful question is which contexts make it strong.

Which is what makes chess interesting, because it assembles just about every favourable condition.

## Why Chess Amplifies the Phenomenon

Three reasons, and the third is the most important.

### 1. The Loss Is Visible, the Compensation Isn't

When you sacrifice a pawn, the loss is a **physical object leaving the board**. Anyone can observe it, immediately, with no particular skill.

The compensation exists nowhere. It isn't an object, it's an **evaluation**: an open file, a strong square, a badly placed king, a tempo. It has to be perceived, understood and judged.

So you're comparing a certain fact to an opinion. And the asymmetry is already there before psychology gets involved.

### 2. The Loss Is Instant, the Compensation Is Deferred

The pawn goes now. The advantage materializes, if it materializes, in fifteen moves.

The whole decision-making literature converges here: an immediate, certain cost against a future, uncertain benefit is the most unfavourable configuration there is. That's the subject of an upcoming article in this series on delayed gratification.

### 3. Material Isn't the Objective, and People Forget

Here's the chess-specific point, and it runs deeper than the first two.

In economics, money is both the measure and, to a large extent, the objective. Losing money is a real loss.

In chess, **material isn't the goal**. The goal is checkmate. Material is only an **indicator** of your probability of getting there, an excellent indicator, reliable in the vast majority of positions, but an indicator all the same.

Which means aversion to material loss is, literally, **aversion to the degradation of a measuring instrument**. You defend the thermometer instead of the temperature.

That's what makes the phenomenon particularly absurd here, and particularly stubborn: the material count is learned very early, it's simple, it's numerical, and it works well enough to be reinforced thousands of times before you meet the positions where it misleads.

## The Reference Point Problem

Kahneman and Tversky showed that everything depends on the reference point. In chess, that question has a default answer, and it's a bad one.

Almost every player's spontaneous reference point is **the material equality of the starting position**. You count in deviations: I'm a pawn down, I'm up the exchange.

But that reference point has no justification. The starting position isn't a norm you should return to: it's simply where the game began.

The correct reference point is **the evaluation of the current position**. And an evaluation folds material in among other factors, instead of treating it as a balance to be preserved.

This confusion produces identifiable behaviours:

**Recapturing by reflex.** An exchange gets accepted because not recapturing would leave a deficit, without examining whether the resulting structure is good.

**Clinging to a doomed pawn.** The pawn is lost, the evaluation has already absorbed that, but you spend six moves defending it instead of improving your position. You're defending an already obsolete number.

**Refusing to give material back.** The reverse situation, and a very expensive one: you've won a piece, you're under pressure, and returning that piece would defuse the entire attack for a winning position. You don't do it, because giving back is a loss relative to a reference point that updated five moves ago.

This last case is the most instructive, because it shows the reference point **moves** as soon as the advantage is acquired. What Kahneman called the endowment effect.

## The Protocol

### 1. Name the Compensation

This is the main lever, and it consists of equalizing the visibility of the two terms.

Before turning down a sacrifice, force yourself to state the counterpart in **verifiable terms**: which precise square I obtain, which file opens, which piece becomes bad, how many tempi I gain, which king becomes uncomfortable.

If you can't name three concrete elements, the sacrifice is probably bad and your refusal is justified. If you can name three, you've just made the compensation as concrete as the pawn, and the decision then gets made on comparable ground.

### 2. Re-evaluate From the Position, Never From the History

At every evaluation, the question is: **if I discovered this position now, without knowing how we got here, what would I say?**

It's the same remedy as for anchoring bias, and for good reason: in both cases it's a matter of refusing an inherited reference point. Our [overview of cognitive biases](/en/blog/5-biais-cognitifs-blunder/) details this mechanism.

### 3. Train on Sacrifice Positions

This is the most effective remedy, and it's almost uniquely available in chess. Work through collections of positional sacrifices, where the solution consists precisely of giving material for lasting compensation.

The objective isn't to memorize those positions. It's to **build the patterns that make compensation visible**. A recognized compensation is no longer an opinion, it's a pattern, and a pattern can be compared to a pawn.

That's also the lesson of the Cowley and Byrne study mentioned in our article on [confirmation bias in chess](/en/blog/biais-de-confirmation-aux-echecs/): what you take for quality of judgment is often a consequence of the pattern repertoire available to you.

### 4. Watch the Domain of Losses

The other side of the theory is less well known and just as expensive: in an already bad position, you become too much of a gambler. You launch desperate attacks, refuse holdable draws, complicate for the sake of complicating.

The useful rule is mechanical: in a position you judge to be losing, drop the risk level your intuition proposes by one notch. It's distorted by the direction, not by the position.

## What to Take Away

Kahneman and Tversky proposed in 1979 that people evaluate changes relative to a reference point, and that losses weigh more than equivalent gains. The two-to-one ratio made its fortune, but it doesn't hold as a constant: Gal and Rucker's 2018 review concludes that the asymmetry is contextual, and the reasonable position today is that of a conditional phenomenon rather than a law.

Chess assembles precisely the conditions that amplify it. The loss is a visible object, the compensation is an evaluation. The loss is immediate, the compensation deferred. And above all, material isn't the objective of the game: it's an indicator of the objective, which players end up defending against it.

The spontaneous reference point, material equality at the start, has no legitimacy. The only relevant reference point is the evaluation of the position in front of you.

**After reading:** in your next game, when you discard a sacrifice, write down the three concrete elements of compensation you'd identified. If you can't write three, you know what you still need to learn to see.
