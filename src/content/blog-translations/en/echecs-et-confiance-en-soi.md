You can feel confident before a game and miss a simple tactic. You can also doubt yourself and find an excellent defence. Confidence and competence interact, but they are not interchangeable.

To work usefully, ask a more precise question than “Am I a good player?”: what do I think I can do in this position?

## Self-efficacy: a belief tied to a situation

Bandura (1977) proposes the concept of self-efficacy: expectations about one’s ability to carry out an action. His framework distinguishes mastery experiences, observing others, verbal persuasion and physiological states as sources of information.

This theoretical text draws on work concerning behavioural change. It is not a study showing that expert athletes’ confidence predicts performance better than talent, nor a validation of a chess protocol.

In chess, you can use this distinction to formulate a testable expectation: “I can explain the defence in this endgame” is more precise than “I should beat them”. You still need to check the endgame: a specific belief can also be poorly calibrated. Likewise, [feeling better after a pre-game ritual](/en/blog/placebo-effect-in-chess/) does not establish that the ritual improved your play.

## Building concrete reference points

Choose a problem you want to handle better: recognising a tactical pattern, calculating a forcing line or understanding a pawn structure. After a session, note what you can explain without looking at the solution.

A success can provide a reference point. A mistake can identify a current limitation. Neither sums up your ability in every area.

If an exercise becomes too easy because you know the answer, change the position or ask for a justification. Recognising a solution is not necessarily the ability to find it in a new situation.

## After a losing streak

Start with a few games rather than a global conclusion. Did you miss similar threats? Spend time without a precise question? Misunderstand the transition into an endgame?

You can ask a partner to help distinguish a technical mistake from an interpretation about yourself. “I missed that intermediate check” suggests something to work on. “I cannot calculate” goes beyond what the game demonstrates.

[Post-game analysis](/en/blog/how-to-analyze-chess-games/) is a proposed way of working. It is not established here as the best scientific method for building confidence, nor as a guarantee of improvement.

## Using ratings with perspective

A rating summarises results within a system and time control. It measures neither your personal worth nor every skill you have. A rise or fall deserves to be viewed in context: activity, opponents, period and number of games.

Comparing yourself with your past can help, but comparison with an opponent can also inform preparation. There is no need to condemn one interpretation to keep the other.

Doubts about belonging are discussed separately in the article on [impostor syndrome](/en/blog/impostor-syndrome-in-chess/). Knowing your technical difficulties and feeling that you belong are different questions.

## Before the next game

Choose a goal you can put into practice: examine the opponent’s threat, compare your candidates, keep some time in reserve. A result goal can remain motivating, but you do not control its achievement alone.

You can feel nervous without concluding that everything will go badly. Try a simple routine and see whether it helps you begin. Confidence does not require the absence of doubt; it can leave room for verification.

## A prediction that survives a lost game

At the beginning of a club night, write down something smaller than “I expect to win”. You could expect to notice when a rook ending contains a possible check from behind, to compare two recaptures before choosing one, or to leave your king an escape square before moving the last defender. These are choices you can find again on the board. An opponent can play better than you and win without making every one of those predictions false. Equally, your opponent can lose on time while none of your intended checks ever took place.

Choose one prediction whose outcome you will be able to distinguish from lack of opportunity. Suppose you want to check captures before exchanging queens. If the queens never come into contact, write “no opportunity”. Calling that a success because you did not make an error inflates the score. Calling it a failure because the game went badly loses the same information in the opposite direction. A blank category makes your next practice decision easier: you might need to start a training game one move before the exchange to get a chance to try the skill at all.

Try the same distinction in a post-game conversation. A partner says you looked confident in a difficult ending. Ask what they noticed. They may remember that you played a rook check quickly. Perhaps you knew the idea; perhaps you were short of time and could think of nothing else. Only one of those explanations would support a claim that you had mastered the checking mechanism. Show the partner where the defending king could go, and ask them to choose the square they consider most troublesome. How you answer that different move tells you more about the skill than how you appeared from the other side of the table.

## The confidence slips on four boards

Have a friend select four positions from earlier training sessions without telling you which contain combinations. Write down a candidate move, one reply you have checked and a judgement in ordinary language: “I would play this now” or “I would like another minute”. Your friend keeps the slips in order. When you later check the positions together, save the variations that explain the moves. A mark for right or wrong offers little help in deciding when to spend that extra minute next time.

The fourth board becomes particularly interesting if you ask for time and still find the best move. What did you want to check? If you can name the one countercheck that worried you, another minute would have had a specific job. If you were going through the same line and reaching the same conclusion each time, the hesitation may have concerned your ability to accept a small amount of uncertainty. Conversely, you can play a poor move instantly because a similar-looking idea worked the previous night. A single label of “confident player” would hide those differences.

Take one position home and make a near neighbour. A piece that supplied an escape square changes its location, or a defender which could answer a check is now pinned. Verify that the new position is legal and that the new detail actually changes the calculation you want to test. Hide your original slip and work it out again. Can you explain which branch now needs a different answer? That gives you a reference point which means more than being able to recite the solution to the original board.

If possible, ask your friend to mix the new diagram among two unrelated ones. When all the exercises announce a pin, remembering to look for the pin is relatively easy. When the theme is unknown, you must decide for yourself which checks to apply. You may find that your calculation is reliable once the danger is pointed out, while your decision to look for it is inconsistent. These are separable questions. A collection of unfamiliar mixed positions would serve the second better than repeatedly solving the same diagram.

## An invitation you are not sure you deserve

A club organiser asks you to demonstrate one of your games to beginners. Two recent losses make you hesitate. Find out what the task really is. “Show how you chose between two possible recaptures” calls for a specific piece of preparation. It does not require you to know every rook ending or to have a higher rating this month than last. Set up the board at the choice, write down the two continuations and identify the one reply you would like the audience to try. The task has become something you can rehearse.

Separate the version you saw during the game from the one a teammate found afterwards. At first you might say, “I expected the rook to return to the open file.” Then give the audience the other colour and let somebody try the move that surprised you. The demonstration can succeed even if a beginner finds an improvement. The learning objective is to show how a player compares two recaptures against resistance. The success of the evening need not depend on the speaker making a flawless claim in advance.

Ask for feedback at the right resolution. “Was I good enough?” gives a teammate who wants to be kind very little to work with. “Which diagram would you leave up for another minute?” can produce a change you can actually make. If the answer is that the departure square of the rook was hard to see from the back, you have a useful fix. If the answer concerns the most important defence, you have a subject for the next practice session.

There may also be a perfectly ordinary reason to decline. Perhaps the organiser really wants an hour on an opening you have never played, and you have only one evening to prepare. Suggest a game that you know or a member who plays that opening. Accepting an appropriate invitation and declining one outside your current preparation are both evidence of a reasonably specific assessment of your skills. Neither requires you to reach a definitive opinion on your worth as a chess player. The relevant question for the next invitation is something you can now answer: what can you demonstrate on an actual board in the time available?

A clear answer also tells the organiser which audience is likely to benefit and lets you prepare a diagram that someone can actually replay.
