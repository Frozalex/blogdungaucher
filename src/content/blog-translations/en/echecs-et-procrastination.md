Monday evening is supposed to be for reviewing your weekend game. You open the score sheet, remember how the game ended, search for a video about the opening and watch two rapid games on the way back. Half an hour later you know the name of a new variation and still cannot say what you missed on move seventeen. Did you procrastinate? That depends partly on what you intended to do, what else needed doing and whether you had the information to begin. Calling every detour a lack of discipline makes it harder to find the obstacle you can actually change.

## A delay can do different jobs

Say the game is in a handwritten notebook at the club and you are home without it. You genuinely cannot reconstruct the position around move seventeen. Spending the evening studying another game may be the best available use of the time. Send a message asking for a photograph of the score sheet and put an appointment on the calendar. The analysis has been delayed, but the missing condition for starting it has changed.

Consider a different scene. The correct position is on screen and you keep opening a comfortable line from the beginning instead. You know how to explain it, whereas the middlegame makes you feel foolish. Going back to the opening provides immediate relief and postpones an uncomfortable question. In a [paper on mood regulation](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12011), Fuschia Sirois and Timothy Pychyl discuss why an activity that feels aversive now can lose out to short-term relief even when the delay will cost us later. That account offers a hypothesis for this particular evening. It does not show that every player who studies the opening is regulating a negative emotion.

A third player postpones analysis because a child needs dinner or a bus leaves in five minutes. Their intentions and priorities are quite different. A notebook with space for a date and the necessary materials is more useful here than a programme to overcome fear of losing. The same outward action, closing a game, can be sensible planning, unavoidable interruption or a pattern the player regrets. Start with what happened rather than trying to identify a personality from the action.

## Define a finish you can recognise

“Analyse my games” is easy to fail because it does not specify how many games, which decisions or what the finished work should look like. Try one question from an actual position: “When my opponent offered the exchange of rooks, could my king still reach the passed pawn?” A finished first pass might be a diagram, your proposed route and the first reply that stops it. That is useful even if the complete ending remains unsolved. You can send the result to a friend, take it to the club or revisit it after finding a relevant lesson.

Now consider the equipment the question requires. If you need the score only as far as the rook offer, do you really need to enter all sixty moves, choose the perfect annotation symbols and sort last year's opening files first? Those are legitimate tasks. On this evening they can become a route around the harder decision. Enter just the relevant position if you can verify it from the score, including whose turn it is. Give the file a name that contains the question. When you next see it in a list, you will know what work is waiting inside.

There is a useful exception. Sometimes reconstructing the preceding moves is necessary because a castling right, the last pawn move or an inaccurate diagram changes the answer. In that case the setup is part of the analysis. Write down exactly what you need from it. “Check whether the king moved earlier” is a bounded job. “Organise my entire database before thinking about this” has no comparable endpoint. You can stop the preparation as soon as it answers the question that justified it.

## Does the clock solve the problem?

Over the board a clock forces a result if a player spends all their time. It does not guarantee a well-timed choice. You can spend ten minutes repeatedly checking the line you like, play it anyway and leave yourself thirty seconds for a harder decision. The deadline changed when you had to move; it did not select which variations deserved attention.

A productive think leaves traces. On the first pass you find a candidate. On the second you notice an intermediate check. On the third you verify the square to which the king can move. Even if the final choice stays the same, those minutes answered different questions. Contrast that with replaying the same exchange three times without checking a new reply or comparing a new candidate. If you catch yourself doing the latter, ask “what information do I still need before moving?” If the answer is concrete and time remains, investigate it. If it is merely a wish to feel certain, choose the best-supported option the position and the clock allow.

The touch-move provisions in the [FIDE Laws of Chess](https://handbook.fide.com/chapter/E012023) regulate what happens when a player intentionally touches a piece in the relevant circumstances. They are rules of competition, not a demonstration that playing timed games teaches a general ability to make commitments. A chess player may be decisive over the board and still put off a difficult phone call. If a technique from chess helps with that call, say what crossed over: perhaps writing the number and the first question on a card made starting easier. That is a specific action you can try again, without needing a claim about all chess players.

## The two folders experiment

If your collection of unreviewed losses has become daunting, make two temporary lists. Call the first “one question ready.” A game belongs there only if you can identify a decision in a sentence, such as “could I decline the draw and still stop the passed pawn?” Call the second “needs a starting point.” A game goes there when you remember the result but cannot yet identify an instructive moment. The distinction is about readiness, not quality. A brilliant game can be hard to learn from until someone points out which decision to revisit.

Choose one game from the first list and spend a short, prearranged session on it. Before consulting an engine, record the reply you expected during the game and the reply that actually appeared. If they were identical, the problem may be evaluating the ending rather than anticipating the opponent. If they differed, locate the first point of divergence. Stop when you can describe the uncertainty more accurately than before. It is all right to leave with a better question instead of a completed answer.

Now open one game from the second list. Your entire job this time is to move it into the first. Replay until you find a decision you remember debating, a long think on the clock record or a change in pawn structure you cannot explain. Select just one. As soon as its question is written down, the session has met its purpose. This is a useful job for a day when you have ten minutes and no energy for deep calculation. Tomorrow's harder work now has an entry point. If the game offers no question relevant to your training, archive it. You are allowed to choose which games deserve your limited time.

## What to change after missing an appointment

Suppose you planned to work on a lost ending after dinner on Thursday and missed the appointment again. Before adding another alarm, reconstruct Thursday. Was the board accessible? Was the question still too broad? Did a work commitment use the only quiet hour? Did opening the loss make you want to play a new game immediately? These obstacles call for different changes. Keep a set on the table if setup is the barrier. Ask a stronger player to choose a single decision if choosing is the barrier. Move the appointment if the time slot never existed in practice.

If embarrassment is the obstacle, a partner can change the terms of the review. Send a diagram without the players' names or the result and ask which candidate they would consider first. After they answer, reveal your choice and explain what you feared. You get an independent starting point and an opportunity to contribute your own recollection. Agree in advance that one exchange of messages is enough. Otherwise the obligation to conduct a perfect post-mortem can quietly replace the smaller obligation you were already avoiding.

One way to test whether your new arrangement worked is to leave a note for your future self at the stopping point. Write the next move you want to investigate and the answer that would let you close the file. When you reopen it, notice how much of the session goes into remembering what you meant to do. If the note merely says “check tactics,” it has moved the vague task into the future. If it says “after Black checks from behind, try stepping towards the pawn instead of returning to the same square,” a concrete experiment is waiting. You can reject the idea quickly and still have begun the review you intended.

At the end of the week, count something modest: how many saved positions now have a question you could take to the next club night? One well-posed question about a rook ending can be worth more to your next game than an hour of videos chosen to avoid it. If the videos taught something you wanted to learn, give them their own place in the schedule. The gain is being able to choose them on purpose, knowing which question is waiting when you return to the board.
