Tell me about a game you played three years ago that you're proud of.

You'll describe a clear plan, moves that follow from one another, the moment you spotted the combination. The story holds together, it has a beginning, a turning point and an end.

That game didn't go that way. Not because you're lying, but because the story you just told is the product of twenty or so successive reconstructions, each one a little more coherent than the last.

And the unsettling part is that **every time you tell it, you change it again**.

## Bartlett, 1932

The founder of the field is a British psychologist, Frederic Bartlett, who published a book called *Remembering* in 1932.

His best known protocol is deceptively simple. He had British students read a Native American folk tale, "The War of the Ghosts." The text has one essential property: it's **foreign** to its readers. Its narrative structure, its causal logic, its supernatural elements match nothing familiar to them.

Then he asked them to retell it. Once shortly afterward, then again days, weeks, sometimes months later.

The results form a pattern of remarkable regularity.

**The story gets shorter.** Details judged secondary disappear.

**It gets rationalized.** Inexplicable elements pick up explanations that weren't in the text.

**It gets normalized.** Unfamiliar terms are swapped for familiar equivalents; a canoe becomes a boat, seal hunting becomes a fishing trip.

**And above all, it becomes coherent.** What stayed obscure or contradictory in the original gradually organizes itself into a story that works.

Bartlett's conclusion introduces the concept that would carry the whole field. Memory isn't a recording stored and then played back. It's **a reconstruction, guided by schemas**, meaning by prior knowledge structures about how the world works.

Which has an immediate consequence: the distortion is **not random**. It runs in a predictable direction, toward the subject's schema.

## What 2000 Added

For a long time it was reasonable to think that a memory formed, consolidated, then stayed put, even if it slowly faded.

In 2000, a team led by Karim Nader with Glenn Schafe and Joseph LeDoux published a result in *Nature* that changed that picture. They showed that an already consolidated memory, when it's **recalled**, becomes temporarily unstable again, and has to be stored a second time to persist. This is **reconsolidation**.

The implication is striking, and it's the heart of this article: **remembering isn't a neutral act**. It isn't pulling out a file and putting it back identical. It's opening the file, making it editable, and rewriting it as you close it.

Every recall is therefore an editing opportunity. And the memory you keep isn't the memory of the event: it's the memory of the last time you remembered it.

## What This Does to Your Games

Two boundaries need drawing first, because this topic has two very close neighbors.

**This isn't position memorization.** The way a strong player takes in a position in a few seconds is a matter of chunks and perceptual expertise, covered in our article on [memorizing chess games](/en/blog/memoriser-parties-aux-echecs/).

**And it isn't implantation by an outside source either.** Our article on [false memories in chess](/en/blog/faux-souvenirs-aux-echecs/) describes what happens when an analysis session or a leading question introduces content that was never there.

What's at stake here is the **normal** mechanism, with no outside interference at all. Nobody is suggesting anything to you. Your memory warps on its own, in the direction of what you already believe about yourself.

### Normalization Toward Your Schema

This is the central point, and it's Bartlett transposed directly.

Your schema, here, is the idea you hold about your own play. Attacking player, weak in endgames, jumpy in time trouble, whatever it happens to be.

Real games don't look like that. They're messy. You found a good move by accident, you burned six minutes on an obvious move, you won a position you didn't understand, you got scared for no reason.

Every time you remember a game, those non conforming details fade a little, exactly the way the canoe became a boat. What survives is what fits.

The result, after a few years, is a remarkably coherent body of memories describing a player with a clean style and clear weaknesses. That player doesn't exist. He's the product of normalization.

### Three Typical Distortions

**The win becomes controlled.** The moments when you had no idea what to play disappear, because they don't fit with having won. The plan looks in hindsight like something you intended from the start.

**The loss becomes doomed.** The reverse. The moments when you were better fade away, and the game turns into one continuous slide. That's what feeds bogus post mortems along the lines of "I was lost from move 20," described in our article on [gaslighting in chess](/en/blog/gaslighting-aux-echecs/), except that here nobody is imposing it on you.

**Causality tightens up.** A game rarely turns on a single move. Memory, though, always picks one out, because a story needs a turning point.

### The Real Cost

It isn't in the accuracy of the memory for its own sake. It's in the **decisions** you make out of that body of memories.

You pick your repertoire, your training program and your goals based on the idea you hold about your play. And that idea comes from memories that have been normalized, over years, toward that same idea.

It's a closed loop. The schema distorts the memories, and the memories confirm the schema. It's also the raw material of the self fulfilling prophecies described in [the corresponding article](/en/blog/prophetie-auto-realisatrice-aux-echecs/) in this series.

## The Upside of Reconstruction

All this might suggest memory is defective. It isn't: it does exactly what it's built to do, which is extract useful regularities rather than archive recordings.

And reconsolidation, which makes a memory editable at the moment of recall, has a consequence you can put to work directly in training.

**Active recall isn't a check, it's the operation that fixes.**

Rereading an annotated game is passive: the memory isn't recalled, it's recognized, which produces a strong feeling of mastery and very little trace. Rebuilding the game **from memory**, on an empty board, forces genuine recall, therefore reconsolidation, therefore reinforcement.

That's the mechanism behind why the most unpleasant exercise is also the most effective. The general method is spelled out in our article on [analyzing your games](/en/blog/analyser-ses-parties/).

## The Protocol

**1. Write it down before reconstruction starts.** It's the same rule as in several articles in this series, and that convergence means something: one single habit protects you against three separate mechanisms. Right after the game, note the two or three moments of hesitation, what you considered, and your evaluation at the time. That note won't reconstruct itself.

**2. Reread your old notes, not your memories.** After a year, comparing what you wrote with what you remember is the most instructive experiment you can run on yourself. The gap measures your own normalization.

**3. Be suspicious of stories that are too clean.** A game you remember as a limpid demonstration has probably been tidied up. Messiness is the sign of a memory that hasn't been retouched much.

**4. Use recall rather than rereading.** Rebuild from memory before you check. It's slower, more painful and considerably more effective, for a mechanical reason: recall is what opens the memory to modification, therefore to reinforcement.

**5. Base your decisions on numbers, not stories.** Your statistics don't normalize. It's the only body of evidence whose content doesn't depend on the idea you hold about yourself.

## Key Takeaways

Bartlett showed in 1932 that a story recalled several times gets shorter, gets rationalized and normalizes toward the expectations of whoever is remembering it. The distortion isn't random: it runs in the direction of the schema.

The discovery of reconsolidation added the mechanism: recalling a memory makes it temporarily unstable before it's filed away again. What you remember, then, isn't the event but the latest version of it you produced.

In chess, this means your body of memories slowly converges toward the idea you hold about your play, erasing whatever doesn't fit. And since you build your training out of that idea, the loop closes.

The same property, flipped around, is what makes active recall so effective: the moment a memory is editable is also the moment it can be reinforced.

**After reading:** take a game you're certain you remember precisely, write down what you remember, then open it. The gap isn't a memory defect, it's memory working normally, and it can be measured.
