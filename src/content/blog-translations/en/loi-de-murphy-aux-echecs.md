You played a decent game, then one mistake changed everything. “Of course, at the worst possible moment.” The phrase captures disappointment. It does not demonstrate that events conspired against you.

Murphy’s law serves as a metaphor here. Understanding your games requires returning to the positions and the way you count mistakes.

## What you call the worst moment

A mistake may be minor in one position and decisive in another. The severity of the result does not, by itself, reveal how often you make that kind of mistake.

You also need to choose the criterion before looking at examples. If the “worst moment” simply becomes the one when you lost the game, you will inevitably find decisive mistakes occurring there. The definition already contains the conclusion.

## A memorable mistake is not a frequency

Tversky and Kahneman (1973) study the availability heuristic: judging frequency or probability from how easily examples come to mind. That ease can inform judgement, but factors other than frequency influence it.

A spectacular loss may therefore come to mind more readily than a quiet game. This is a possible application of the framework to chess, not a measurement demonstrating that your memory always selects defeats.

You can have several accurate memories and an inaccurate overall impression. The denominator is missing: all the games in which the problem could have appeared, including those in which it did not.

## Examining one hypothesis at a time

If you suspect time trouble, record the time available at critical moments when the game history allows it. If you suspect a technical difficulty, compare the relevant positions. A misunderstood endgame and a miscalculated attack do not necessarily require the same work.

Fatigue, stress and complexity may also feature in your account. Avoid turning them into a certain explanation after one defeat. Being disappointed when leaving the playing hall does not give you a measurement of your brain activity.

## Building a practical safeguard

In training, try a short check before moving: checks against your king, hanging pieces and the threat from the opponent’s last move. Adapt it to the time available; it need not become an endless ritual.

If you often miss the conversion of an advantage, work on a few positions of that type. If repeated games exhaust you, plan an end to the session. These choices address observable difficulties without promising to eliminate every mistake.

After a loss, you can say you are frustrated. You do not need a psychological formula to justify that emotion. When ready to analyse, replace “this always happens” with a testable question: in which positions did it happen, and which counterexamples do you know?

## Reconstruct the sequence before naming the mistake

Imagine that you have just lost the last game of a weekend tournament. The winning move you missed is painfully clear in the post-mortem. On the bus home the story becomes “whenever I get a winning attack, my clock runs out”. Open the score and identify the first moment when a decision was still available. Was it the missed move with three seconds left? Or was it the long think six moves earlier when you examined the same forcing line three times? Those two stories place the useful intervention at different moments in the game.

Try a two-column reconstruction. In the left column write what was visible before the decision: remaining time, the opponent's last move and the replies you actually considered. In the right column write what you discovered afterwards. Keep “the move was forced” on the right unless you had established it at the board. Doing this once can change the story considerably. A move that looks absurd when the winning continuation is given as a puzzle may have competed with two perfectly plausible alternatives during play.

Include a position where you coped well under the same pressure. Perhaps you had another game with little time left in which you chose a forcing exchange and reached a draw. If the clock situation and difficulty were comparable, your claim about always collapsing needs revising. If the drawn ending was simple while the loss involved an exposed king, the comparison suggests a narrower question. Do exposed kings demand more checking time than you currently allow? That is a question you can work on without waiting to discover whether you are “unlucky”.

## A miniature audit of the word “always”

Imagine a notebook with eight games. In three you reached the last few minutes of your clock. You missed a decisive check in one of those three. In the other five games you missed such a check once as well. You remember the time-trouble loss best because it settled a match. The count is one miss in three opportunities in one group and one in five in the other. It would be premature to announce a reliable difference from such small groups. It is already possible to reject the much stronger personal story that the mistake happens every time the clock is low.

Now look at the word “opportunity”. In some games there was no decisive check to miss. If the notebook included all games indiscriminately, the denominator mixes positions with and without the danger you meant to measure. A cleaner diary might record positions in which the opponent had a forcing check, noting whether you considered it before moving. It takes more work to collect. It also gives the coach an actual position and a specific question. Think about which count would lead you to change your next practice session, then collect that count.

Another source of apparent bad luck is selective storage. You may save the spectacular loss to your analysis folder and never export the routine win where the same warning sign was handled correctly. At the end of the month the folder has become an anthology of disasters. To sample more fairly, take every game from one event before opening the analysis board. Write the result last. If the easiest example to remember is still the most useful one to train, keep it. You now know why it is in the file.

## Rehearse the interruption

Put a finished game on a board with a partner. At a quiet point have your partner announce that your original plan has failed and that you have thirty seconds to name a playable move. The announcement is part of the exercise, not a claim that the position is objectively bad. First identify what changed on the previous move. Then nominate one move that addresses the immediate issue and one reply that would embarrass it. Play the reply. In thirty seconds you can learn whether your routine still works after an interruption without treating the outcome as a measure of your worth.

Repeat with a diagram in which the opponent's last move created no immediate threat. If your routine always produces a defensive retreat, the safeguard is overcorrecting. Ask whether there is a forcing move of your own worth checking. A short routine should help you notice the board, not compel you to find danger everywhere. Compare the two exercises and agree with your partner on the one line you want to remember when the real clock is ticking.

Apply the same method to practical problems before a tournament. Suppose your fear is arriving late because the posted pairing changed rooms. Save the organiser's contact details and check the round location when you arrive. Now imagine you did everything right and the game still went badly. Would checking the address twice more have improved your position on move twenty? Keeping logistical precautions separate from decisions at the board keeps a disappointing result from creating a dozen superstitious rituals.

A coach can ask one final question after reviewing a bad day: which precaution would you also recommend after a win? Recording move times around a critical decision is equally sensible in either case. Wearing yesterday's shirt because a winning move finally occurred to you is unlikely to reveal much about your analysis. The question reconnects the suggested change with an identifiable decision. It also leaves room to acknowledge that some outcomes are genuinely outside your control, including an opponent finding a remarkably good defence.

## The position you wanted to remember

Choose one moment for the next training session and write the question on the back of the diagram. Avoid giving away the solution on the front. If the question is “Which reply did I fail to examine after my intended capture?”, a partner can present the board again several days later with the colours reversed. Work out the answer before turning over the sheet. If your first guess is wrong, the review still tells you something precise about where your attention went. That is more useful than retelling the tournament story with a different villain.

A variant of this exercise is to exchange diagrams with a teammate who does not know the result. Tell them how much time you had and ask which reply they would check first. Keep their first suggestion, even when it differs from the one in your notes. Afterwards compare the *information available* to each player. Your teammate may have known you were showing them a mistake, while at the board nobody told you it was a critical moment. Next time mix in a position where your original decision was sound. If the partner now identifies the real danger among the ordinary decisions, the exercise begins to test recognition as well as calculation. If both of you keep finding imaginary disasters, you have learned something useful about the way the positions were selected.
