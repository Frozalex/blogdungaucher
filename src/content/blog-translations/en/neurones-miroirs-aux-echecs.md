You look at a position and think you understand what your opponent is preparing. You play a preventive move, then their plan becomes clear. The experience is familiar: you have considered the position from the other side.

Can mirror neurons explain this anticipation? The studies presented here do not establish that. We need to distinguish observing a movement, imagining an action and reasoning about a strategic intention. Grouping them under the same word does not demonstrate that they rely on a single mechanism.

## What “mirror neurons” means

Di Pellegrino and colleagues published a neurophysiological study in macaques in 1992. Some of the neurons studied in the premotor cortex respond both while a motor act is performed and while a corresponding act is observed. This kind of property led to the term “mirror neurons”.

The review by Casile, Caggiano and Ferrari (2011) examines the mirror system, its development and proposed interpretations. It concerns links between observing and performing actions. It does not test a chess player’s ability to anticipate a sacrifice or an endgame plan.

Watching a hand pick up a piece and understanding why a player chooses an exchange are different questions. A result about the first is not enough to answer the second.

## Anticipating a plan involves more than observing a movement

On a chessboard, the movement can be almost identical for two very different decisions. Moving a rook one square may prepare an attack, prevent an invasion or simply wait. Its meaning depends on the position and the possible replies.

To understand the move, you can examine controlled squares, available exchanges and threats. You can also form a hypothesis about the opponent’s preparation or preferences. That is an interpretation you must check against the board.

No study presented in this article establishes that mirror-neuron activity explains this reasoning. The attribution of an imaging study to Susan Polgár is unsupported: it is not a scientific reference on which to base that claim.

## Changing perspective remains an exercise worth trying

You do not need a neural explanation to ask a practical question: “If I had my opponent’s pieces, which move would I examine?” This exercise may help you move beyond calculation centred on your own plan. It neither measures nor guarantees the training of a mirror system.

Here is one way to try it in a training game:

1. Pause before your move and describe your objective.
2. Look at the position from the opponent’s side: which captures, checks or threats deserve examination?
3. Calculate a reply that makes your plan difficult, even if it does not fit the style you attribute to your opponent.
4. Then check your line on the board or with an engine, distinguishing what you saw from what you merely assumed.

These are analysis instructions. They are not attributed here to Botvinnik without a precise reference, nor presented as a validated experimental protocol.

## Avoiding projection

“In their place, I would attack” can be a useful starting point. It can also make you overlook a defence or an exchange you dislike playing yourself. Your hypothesis about the opponent should not close the list of moves to calculate too soon.

A stronger question is: “Which reply makes my idea difficult?” It concerns the position rather than an assumed intention. If several replies work, keep them in the analysis rather than discarding one because it feels unnatural.

![Avoid projecting your own style](/images/en/neurones-miroirs-03-angles-morts-style.svg)

## What post-game analysis can actually clarify

When you talk with your opponent, they can describe their reasoning. This account helps compare your hypothesis with what they report considering. It does not provide direct access to their neural mechanisms, and their memory may be incomplete.

An engine answers a different question: which moves does it find under its search conditions? You can use both perspectives without making them say the same thing. Discussion clarifies reported reasoning; calculation helps check variations.

For your next training session, choose a moment when you misjudged the reply. Write down the move you expected and the move actually played. Then look for what in the position should have prompted you to examine that reply. You will be working on a specific decision without turning a neuroscience hypothesis into a certainty.
