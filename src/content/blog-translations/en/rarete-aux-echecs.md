You've got eight minutes left. Your opponent has twenty-five.

You play faster. Not because the position demands it, but because each minute is now worth far more than it was an hour ago. You're making decisions in fifteen seconds on positions that deserve two minutes, and you know it while you're doing it.

What you're going through there is exactly the mechanism marketing uses to sell you online courses. Same lever, same effect, two contexts nobody ever puts side by side.

This article extends our piece on [the psychology of persuasion in chess](/en/blog/psychologie-de-la-persuasion-aux-echecs/), which lays out the seven levers.

## The Cookies

The reference experiment dates from 1975 and was run by Stephen Worchel, Jerry Lee and Akanbi Adewole.

Participants taste cookies presented in a glass jar, then rate their quality and desirability. The cookies are rigorously **identical** in every condition. Only the contents of the jar change.

Some people see a jar holding **ten** cookies. Others a jar holding **two**.

The cookies from the nearly empty jar are rated more desirable.

That's already striking, but the second part of the experiment is much better.

The researchers add conditions where availability **changes in front of the participant**. In one case, eight cookies are removed from a jar that held ten. In the other, eight are added to a jar that held two.

Result: the cookies that have **become rare** are rated more desirable than the ones that always were.

So it isn't the level of availability that counts. It's **the direction of the change**.

### The Link With Loss

This result ties scarcity directly to a mechanism this series has already covered.

A possibility that disappears isn't simply an unavailable option: it's a **loss**. And the asymmetry between losses and gains, described in our article on [loss aversion in chess](/en/blog/aversion-a-la-perte-aux-echecs/), does the rest.

Hence an immediate practical consequence: a countdown works better than a mention of limited stock, because it puts the disappearance in motion in front of the buyer's eyes.

## The Commercial Side, Which Takes One Line

It's simple, and so is the remedy.

Limited places at a training camp. Offer valid until Sunday midnight. Launch promotion. Closed group of ten students. Countdown displayed on the page.

One observation settles it: **digital content has no inventory**. A video course can be sold to a million people at no marginal cost. A deadline isn't a constraint, then, it's a decision, and it can be extended indefinitely.

Some scarcities are real: an in-person camp has a physical number of seats, a coach has a finite number of hours. The distinction is easy to draw, and you draw it by asking **what would materially limit the offer**.

The remedy is the twenty-four-hour delay mentioned earlier in this series. It's free, it requires no willpower, and it produces information: if the offer has gone, you've dodged a seller who needed you not to think.

## The Side That's Specific to Chess

Here's why this lever deserves better than a marketing article.

**The clock is a scarcity device, built into the game since 1861.**

Look at its properties, and compare them with the jar's.

**The resource is visibly shrinking.** It's displayed, permanently, inside your field of vision.

**It's non-renewable.** Unlike material, which can be won back, time you've spent doesn't come back.

**And its depletion is in motion.** That's exactly the condition that made the cookies most desirable: not few in number, but **in the process of becoming few**.

The outcome is predictable and familiar to every player: as time goes down, its perceived value goes up, until it exceeds the value of what it can buy.

### The Zeitnot Paradox

Time, in chess, has no value in itself. It exists only to be **converted into decision quality**. Time left unspent when the flag falls served no purpose at all.

Yet in zeitnot you see the opposite. The player preserves the resource instead of spending it, including in positions where one more minute would change the result of the game.

It's the same confusion described for material in our article on loss aversion: **you defend the indicator against the objective**. Time is a means, and it turns into an end the moment it gets scarce.

And the cost is double. The badly allocated time, but also the attention consumed by monitoring the resource, taken straight out of the calculation itself.

Our article on [time management in chess](/en/blog/echecs-gestion-du-temps/) deals with the technical side of this question. What concerns us here is the psychological mechanism underneath it, and it's identical to the one in the jars.

### Scarcity as a Weapon

One direct consequence, and plenty of players use it deliberately.

Playing fast when your opponent is short of time doesn't change the position. It speeds up the depletion of a resource whose perceived value is already climbing. It's entirely legitimate psychological play, since the clock is part of the game, and it's brutally effective.

Not to be confused with what happens off the board, covered in our article on [emotional manipulation in chess](/en/blog/manipulation-emotionnelle-aux-echecs/).

## The Third Ground: Rare Openings

One last application, and a more positive one.

A rare line has real practical value, for a reason that has nothing to do with psychology: your opponent has spent less time on it. That isn't perceived scarcity, it's informational scarcity, and it converts into an advantage on the clock and into decision difficulty for the other player.

Watch out for the reversal, though. Systematically hunting for rare lines **because they're rare** is the lever turned against yourself: you end up valuing a variation because nobody plays it, which is exactly the cookie reasoning. The useful criterion is still the quality of the position you get, with rarity as nothing more than a practical bonus.

## Four Practices

**Apply the twenty-four-hour delay.** To any chess-related purchase. Manufactured scarcity is the only lever on Cialdini's list that can't survive waiting.

**Tell real scarcity from produced scarcity.** A camp place is limited by a room. A video file is limited by nothing. The question to ask is: what would materially limit this offer?

**Decide your time allocation before the game.** That's the remedy for zeitnot, and it has to be set while you're calm, because when you're short of time your assessment of a minute's value is distorted by the very mechanism you're trying to compensate for. A simple rule, along the lines of never dropping below a given threshold before a given move, works better than a judgment made in the moment.

**Remember that unspent time is wasted.** That's not a figure of speech: it's arithmetically true. A minute still on the display when the game ends produced no value whatsoever.

## What to Take Away

Identical cookies are rated better when two are left rather than ten, and better still when eight have just been removed. What acts on you isn't scarcity, it's the process of becoming scarce: a possibility that disappears gets treated as a loss.

On the chess market, this lever gets defused by waiting a day, since digital content has no inventory and a deadline is a commercial decision.

But its most interesting ground is built into the game itself. The clock is a perfect scarcity device: a visible resource, shrinking, non-renewable, currently depleting. It produces exactly the documented effect, namely an overvaluation of what's becoming rare, to the point of preserving a resource whose only function is to be spent.

Time is worth nothing in itself. It's worth only what it buys in decision quality.

**After reading:** write down your time allocation rule before your next long game. A threshold, a move number. It's a decision made cool-headed about a resource you'll misjudge when you're hot.
