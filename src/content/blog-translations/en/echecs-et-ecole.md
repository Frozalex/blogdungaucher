Bringing a chessboard into a classroom can let pupils discover a game, explain a choice and meet a partner. That is enough to start an educational discussion. It does not demonstrate that chess raises grades or IQ.

Research offers a mixed picture: a 2016 meta-analysis found a positive average effect, limited by the quality of the comparisons; a large English trial found no improvement in attainment one year after the programme. When deciding where chess belongs in school, we need to distinguish those findings from the cultural, recreational and educational goals a team may choose.

## Real programmes with different goals

### Armenia: a school subject since 2011

The [Armenian Chess Federation](https://chessfed.am/en/static-page/detail/2) states that chess became a compulsory subject in the second to fourth grades of public schools in 2011. That means neither every primary grade nor, by itself, evidence of better mathematics attainment.

This integration shows that a country can organize chess teaching at scale. To establish its effects on other learning, however, evaluations would need to distinguish chess instruction from other features of the school system. A policy decision does not replace that comparison.

### France: the Class'Échecs programme

[Éduscol presents Class'Échecs](https://eduscol.education.gouv.fr/6618/introduction-du-jeu-d-echecs-l-ecole-et-programme-class-echecs) as a French Chess Federation programme to develop chess learning in primary schools, involving volunteer teachers, resources and possible links with clubs.

The institutional presentation sets out educational aims. They should be read as programme goals, without turning their statement into experimental evidence of better grades, memory or confidence for every pupil.

### New York: access through schools and nonprofit organizations

[Chess in the Schools](https://chessintheschools.org/) offers school activities, training and tournaments in New York. It illustrates another route into chess, distinct from a compulsory national subject. An organization's testimonials and participation figures describe its work; they cannot by themselves establish a causal effect on attainment.

### England: a programme tested in a large trial

The Chess in Schools and Communities programme underwent an evaluation funded by the Education Endowment Foundation. Its particular value for this discussion lies in testing an educational proposal against measured attainment, rather than relying only on its popularity with participants.

## Reading the scientific findings together

### Sala and Gobet, 2016: a modest overall effect with caveats

The meta-analysis brings together 24 studies and 40 effect sizes. The observed overall effect is **g = 0.338**, with a **95% confidence interval of 0.242 to 0.435**. The mathematics estimate is **g = 0.382**. These are standardized differences between groups, rather than a percentage increase in grades or a number of IQ points.

The central limitation is the near absence of active control groups: pupils receiving another activity with comparable time and attention. Without them, it is hard to separate chess from novelty, instructor enthusiasm or expectations. The authors do not establish transfer and call for stronger experimental designs.

### The EEF trial: no attainment gain detected one year later

The English evaluation involved **100 schools and 4,009 pupils**. The programme offered **30 hours of chess lessons**. National Key Stage 2 tests, measured one year after the intervention ended, showed no positive effect on mathematics, reading or science. Nor was the result positive for pupils eligible for free school meals.

This is an important finding without proving that no programme could ever have an effect. It concerns this programme, population and measurement interval. Positive views of the lessons among teachers or pupils do not cancel out the lack of detected attainment gains.

### Sala and Gobet, 2017: testing against another activity

Two experiments published in *Learning & Behavior* compared chess with active controls, first checkers and then Go, as well as passive controls. They found no statistically significant differences in mathematical problem solving or metacognition. The 2017 review in *Current Directions in Psychological Science* also highlights the weakness of the case for far transfer.

These publications are not all independent: the review includes earlier literature. They should therefore not be added up as separate, independent replications. Together, they caution against promising general improvements in intelligence or academic attainment.

## Why transfer remains an open question

Learning to spot a fork may help a pupil recognize another fork: the situations remain closely related. Claiming that this learning improves text comprehension or equation solving requires additional evidence. Sharing words such as “logic,” “attention” or “reasoning” does not establish that connection.

A teacher can build a mathematics activity around the board: locating coordinates, counting squares or exploring symmetry. In that case, mathematical content is also being taught directly. Progress on that exercise would not automatically show that freely playing chess improves mathematics in general.

Naming a connection between subjects may be an interesting teaching approach, but it does not guarantee transfer. We still need to test whether pupils can reuse the concept without a chessboard, on a different task and after a delay. Our article on [chess and mathematics](/en/blog/chess-and-mathematics/) develops this distinction.

## Executive functions: using them is not lasting improvement

During a game, pupils may hold a line in memory, prepare a plan, revise an idea and pause before a capture. These examples describe demands of the activity. They do not demonstrate a general increase in working memory, inhibition or flexibility.

Demonstrating improvement in these functions would require appropriate before-and-after measures, a relevant comparison and follow-up. A good game or a higher rating cannot replace these measures. The work presented here does not justify calling general executive-function development the “most robust” or guaranteed educational benefit of chess.

## Designing a programme with honest goals

**Define what is being taught.** Learning the rules, recognizing a simple mate, explaining a move and taking part in a meeting are observable goals within the activity. “Making pupils smarter” is a much broader promise than the evidence supports.

**Prepare the adults.** Knowing how to play is not enough to organize a group of beginners. The EEF evaluation highlights teaching skills and cooperation between the tutor and class teacher. It does not show that an outside coach is always better than the classroom teacher.

**Plan accessible progression.** Start with a few pieces, check understanding of movements, then introduce threats and small positions. These are teaching choices to adapt, rather than a universally proven superior model.

**Choose a realistic amount of time.** Studies establish no magic dose of 25 or 30 hours that necessarily produces transfer. The large EEF trial offered 30 hours without detecting an attainment gain at follow-up. Available time should be discussed alongside its other possible uses.

**Assess without reducing pupils to a rating.** A child can understand an instruction, explain a mistake or play respectfully while losing. Attainment requires separate academic indicators; classroom tournament victories cannot replace them.

## Three classroom activities to try

**Observe before choosing.** Show a small position and ask: “Which pieces are attacked?” Gather several observations before looking for a move. The immediate goal is describing the position, without assuming automatic transfer to science or reading.

**Compare two proposals.** In groups, pupils choose between two moves and explain their preference. The adult can ask for a counterexample to an idea. Clarity of explanation can be assessed within this task, without claiming that discussion generally increases critical thinking.

**Revisit a mistake without humiliation.** Show a move that leaves a piece hanging, then look for an alternative together. This approach should not be attributed to a single, systematic “Russian method” without a precise source. The practical aim is a discussion in which pupils can safely reconsider a choice.

## Preparing equitable access in practice

A school-time activity may reach pupils who do not attend clubs. But offering everyone the same chessboard is not enough: some already know the game, others are discovering the rules, and reading, handling and attention needs differ.

Provide visual explanations, suitable pairings, accessible materials and ways to participate beyond tournaments. Observe who speaks, who waits and who withdraws. Cultural access can be a goal in its own right, without adding an unproven reduction in attainment inequalities.

## Deciding where chess belongs in the timetable

Before starting a series of lessons, specify its goal, cost, staffing, what it replaces and what will be assessed. If the priority is difficulty with mathematics or reading, chess is not a validated replacement for teaching or targeted support in that subject.

A team may nevertheless choose the game for its cultural value, enjoyment or opportunities to meet others. In that case, assess those goals, listen to pupils who enjoy it less and adjust the activity. Chess does not need a promise of higher IQ to have a considered place in school.
