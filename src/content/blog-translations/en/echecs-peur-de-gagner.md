You reached a favourable position and have just let the advantage slip. The idea of a “fear of winning” arrives quickly. Before adopting it, you still need to understand the move: an overlooked defence, an unfamiliar endgame or time trouble may be enough to explain the mistake.

You may also feel particular pressure when trying to finish. Recognising it does not require calling it unconscious sabotage.

## A winning position is not a win already secured

An engine evaluation indicates the position’s possibilities under particular search conditions. It does not mean conversion is obvious to you. A large advantage may still require many precise choices.

When analysing, look for the defence you missed and the way to retain the advantage. If you did not know the technique, there is concrete work to do. A mistake in a favourable position does not prove you wanted to lose.

## Which consequences of winning may weigh on you?

You may worry about new expectations, a club rivalry or the need to confirm a good result. These are possibilities to examine if they match your experience, not causes to attribute from outside to every hesitant player.

Ask what you were thinking at the critical moment. If you mainly remember a variation, start there. If you were thinking about ratings or someone close to you reacting, you can discuss it without claiming that the thought alone caused the bad move.

## The reference point: a potentially useful hypothesis

Kahneman and Tversky’s prospect theory (1979) describes risky choices relative to a reference point. It invites the possibility that an expected win may already be treated as secured in your account, making its loss particularly painful.

The publication does not establish a chess “winner’s anxiety” circuit. It measures neither your cortisol nor your amygdala, and does not demonstrate that losing an advantage is always twice as painful as an equivalent gain.

The framework can inspire a question about expectations; it does not replace observing what happened.

## Working on conversion

Choose a favourable position from your own games. Replay it with a partner or an engine suited to your work. First look for the opponent’s counterplay: checks, threats against your king, activity and drawing chances.

Then ask what makes the continuation easier or harder. A useful exchange may reduce problems; an automatic exchange may erase the advantage. Simplification therefore deserves checking.

Return to the position with a realistic time constraint. This exercise may help you understand the technique and notice your hesitations. It does not guarantee eliminating anxiety or creating “desensitisation to winning”.

## Keeping the question open

If you notice repeated discomfort when trying to finish, record the situations and discuss them with someone you trust. Avoid looking for proof in champions’ biographies: retrospective interpretations of Morphy or Korchnoi do not diagnose your own play.

The article on [fear of losing](/en/blog/fear-of-losing-in-chess/) addresses imagined consequences of defeat; the [analysis guide](/en/blog/how-to-analyze-chess-games/) helps return to decisions. You can examine both dimensions without reducing every mistake to one of them.

## A whole result can turn on one available square

Set up this practice position. White has a king on f6 and a queen on g6. Black has only a king on h8. White to move. Before moving, name the squares available to the black king at this instant. Then compare **Qg7** with **Qf7**. Both moves bring the queen to the seventh rank. Both keep the white king close. Both take away squares. One wins immediately and the other ends the game as a draw. If a player chose the second move with a few seconds left, how much could you really infer about their attitude toward victory?

With **Qg7#**, the queen checks along the short diagonal from g7 to h8. The king cannot take it because the white king protects g7. The queen also covers h7 along its rank and g8 along its file. There is no piece that can interpose or capture from elsewhere. With **Qf7?**, walk through exactly the same questions. The queen does not attack h8. It covers g8 diagonally and h7 across the seventh rank. The black king cannot move to g7 either. Black has no other piece to play. The lack of legal moves now makes a stalemate because the king is not in check.

Give this diagram to a friend without announcing that it contains a trap. Ask for an explanation after the move. Someone who rushed may discover the stalemate the moment they are invited to name Black's reply. Someone who does not yet know what stalemate means needs the rule explained. Someone who was certain that Qf7 gave check may benefit from tracing the queen's lines on the board. All three might produce the very same move in a game. The score alone cannot choose among those accounts. A single technical question separates them more efficiently than speculation about a hidden motive.

## What the defender would like you to forget

Try playing the weaker side in a position where your partner has a material advantage. Your assignment is to give them decisions to make. Find repeatable checks, a passed pawn that needs watching, or a chance to trade into an ending their remaining material cannot win. After the first attempt, change sides without discussing the solution. When you get your extra material back, you may suddenly recognise a checking route you overlooked before. The exercise gives a concrete meaning to respecting counterplay. It does not require you to feel confident or to regard every opponent's threat as equally serious.

Now look at exchanges. Trading queens may end a series of checks against your exposed king. It may also leave a pawn ending in which your opponent reaches the critical square first. Freeze the board immediately after the proposed recapture and identify whose turn it is. Count the king moves that are actually legal; check whether either side has a spare pawn move. If this final position is too difficult to assess in the available time, that is a legitimate reason to prefer a different route that you understand. “Trade when ahead” cannot stand in for the missing assessment.

## Ask when the win became expected

During a postmortem a player might say that the outcome had felt settled from the moment the opponent lost a piece. A teammate watching the same game might point to a much later moment, after queens came off. Compare the two positions on a board. At the first, perhaps the attack against the king still made every quiet move difficult. At the second, a safe route for the king was finally available. The disagreement gives you a practical question: which defensive resource had to disappear before conversion became straightforward? A change in expectations can be examined alongside the actual change on the board.

If thoughts about a prize or another player's reaction occurred, record them where you remember them. “Before moving the rook I looked at the standings” is more informative than “I think I must have been thinking about winning.” It leaves room for the possibility that the standings distracted you, while keeping the evidence separate from a later explanation. If losing the position was especially painful, separate that reaction after the game from what you remember before choosing the move. They occurred at different times and may call for different conversations.

A draw offer introduces another decision. Suppose a stronger opponent offers a draw just after you have reached an ending with an extra pawn. The offer does not establish that you are winning, losing or about to be tricked. Spend the time available examining the current move, the defender's activity and the consequences of exchanging pawns. In a competition with rules about draw agreements, follow those rules. In ordinary training, you can even replay the ending after accepting the offer. The agreed result stands, while the replay teaches something about how tractable the position really was.

## Give the next practice session an ending of its own

Pick one game in which an advantage disappeared and label the task waiting at the critical move. It might be “remove the checks,” “keep the opponent out of the corner,” or “avoid the last pawn exchange.” Play from there twice. On the first run, let the defender choose the line that made you nervous in the original game. On the second, let them choose freely. Comparing the runs distinguishes studying the known threat from handling a different practical problem. Keep the clock settings if time pressure was part of what you wanted to understand.

Record the defence you now recognise, where your analysis stops, and which choice remains uncomfortable. It is possible to solve the technical problem and still dislike being watched while you try to win. You can work on the first with a board and the second with someone who understands the circumstances. Conversely, a difficult technique can continue to cost points even when you feel perfectly relaxed. The exercise earns its place if it leaves you able to explain a move you previously played on hope. Your next win need not serve as a test of your personality.

When teaching the queen position to someone else, let them show both endings from Black's side. The moment they can point to the missing check in the stalemate line, they have learned something observable, whatever emotion they felt before moving the queen.

With a partner, reverse the colours and put the defending king in the opposite corner. Trace the checking diagonal aloud once before moving. This small change helps distinguish understanding the escape squares from remembering the name of a square in the first diagram.
