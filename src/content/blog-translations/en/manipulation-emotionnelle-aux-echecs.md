A sigh, an insistent look, a draw offer at an awkward moment: what happens around the board can distract you. The challenge is responding to observable behaviour without claiming to read your opponent’s intentions.

## A gesture does not reveal an intention

A player may sigh because they are disappointed, tired or preoccupied. They may also be trying to unsettle you. The gesture alone does not settle it. You risk wasting time constructing a story the position does not confirm.

Return to questions you can answer: is there a threat? How much time remains? Is a specific behaviour disrupting the game? The other player’s apparent confidence does not replace calculation of a variation.

## What the rules prohibit

Article 11.5 of the FIDE rules effective since 2023 prohibits distracting or annoying the opponent. It mentions unreasonable claims, unreasonable draw offers and sources of noise. The arbiter assesses the situation and any penalties.

You do not need to prove a plan of manipulation to report repeated remarks or noises. Describe the facts to the arbiter: what happened, how often, and the disturbance caused. Avoid trying to resolve the conflict through provocation in return.

When requesting assistance, the rules specify the conditions under which you may pause the clock. The arbiter checks whether the reason was valid; it is not a free thinking break.

## A draw offer is not an evaluation

Under Article 9.1, an event’s regulations may restrict draw agreements. When an offer is allowed, it is normally made after moving and before pressing the clock. It remains valid until accepted, rejected or the game ends, under the conditions set out in the rules.

An offer does not prove that the position is equal. You can decline it. The general rules do not establish a universal number of moves to wait between offers: their unreasonable nature may fall under Article 11.5, alongside any event-specific provisions.

Evaluate the position and tournament situation. There is no compulsory three-minute period for considering an offer.

## Protecting your attention

If a behaviour draws your eye, return to a simple check: the latest change in the position, immediate threats, candidate moves. This routine is practical advice, not demonstrated protection against every distraction.

After the game, distinguish the incident from your decisions. A real disturbance may deserve reporting even if you also made a mistake. Conversely, a mistake does not demonstrate that an opponent’s gesture caused it.

Stories about hypnotic powers or diagnoses from afar add little to this approach. You can defend your playing conditions by relying on facts and the arbiter.
