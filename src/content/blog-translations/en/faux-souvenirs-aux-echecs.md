Post mortem. You reach move 27, the one where everything turned. The engine shows the refutation: Rxf7 was winning on the spot.

And you say, "oh yeah, I saw that, but I calculated that the queen was badly placed afterward."

Maybe. It's possible. But memory research suggests that in a fair share of cases, that sentence describes a memory that just formed, right then, under the influence of the information you've only now received.

## The Misinformation Effect

The field starts with an American psychologist, [Elizabeth Loftus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Loftus), and a 1974 experiment run with John Palmer.

Participants watch a film of a car accident. They're then asked a series of questions, only one of which varies: "how fast were the cars going when they **hit** each other?" or "when they **smashed** into each other?"

First result: speed estimates run higher with the stronger verb. You could see nothing there but a wording effect on an uncertain estimate.

The second result is of a different order. A week later, participants are asked again whether they saw broken glass at the scene. In the "smashed" condition, **32%** say they did. In the "hit" condition, **14%**.

There was no broken glass in the film.

The verb used in a question, a week earlier, added a visual detail to a memory. The participants aren't lying: they're reporting what they remember. This is the **misinformation effect**, and it's one of the best established results in the psychology of memory.

Its reach is considerable, because it implies that memory isn't a recording kept and then played back. It's a reconstruction, and a reconstruction accepts materials that didn't come from the event.

## The Shopping Mall, and Its Caveat

Loftus's most famous experiment goes further: it doesn't modify a memory, it creates one.

In 1995, with Jacqueline Pickrell, she recruited participants whose relatives supplied three genuine childhood memories. The researchers added a fourth, entirely invented: getting lost in a shopping mall around the age of five, crying, then being brought back by an elderly person.

All four accounts are presented as coming from the family, and the participant is asked to expand on what he remembers. Across several interviews.

Around **25%** end up reporting a memory, partial or complete, of the shopping mall episode. Some add details nobody gave them: the color of a shirt, the person's face.

The result had enormous impact, particularly on the law of testimony.

### What the Critique Contributed

Honesty is required here, because that 25% figure circulates widely and gets regularly rounded upward.

In 2017, Chris Brewin and Bernice Andrews published a systematic review of implantation studies in *Applied Cognitive Psychology*. Their work consists of examining what actually gets counted as a false memory in these protocols, which is no trivial question: the distance between "I might have a vague impression" and "I can picture the scene" is considerable.

Their result: on average, about **47%** of participants show some form of remembering experience for the suggested event, but only around **15%** produce something scorable as a **full memory**.

Their conclusion: implanting an autobiographical memory is neither easy nor common. The debate that followed remains lively, and a preregistered replication of the shopping mall protocol published in 2023 brought new elements to it.

The reasonable position is therefore in between, and that's the one to hold onto: **memory is distinctly more malleable than we used to think, and distinctly less malleable than the popular version claims**. Changing a detail is easy; manufacturing a whole episode is much less so.

And changing a detail is precisely what concerns us here.

## Why the Post Mortem Is an Implantation Protocol

Now look at what happens when you analyze a game, and compare it against the ingredients Loftus identified.

**New information about a past event.** The analysis teaches you what was really in the position. That information arrives after the fact, exactly like the question about the broken glass.

**An authoritative source.** The engine doesn't make mistakes. Neither does a stronger player, at your scale. There's no reason to resist what's being said, which is exactly the problem: memory has no defense to mount.

**Leading questions.** This is the most direct point. "Did you see Rxf7?" isn't a neutral question: it already contains the move, and it proposes an answer. Structurally it's the same thing as the choice between "hit" and "smashed."

**An interest in accepting the suggestion.** In Loftus's study, participants had nothing to gain from remembering the broken glass. You do. "I saw it" preserves the image you hold of your own level, whereas "I didn't see it" calls it into question. That's the mechanism described in our article on [cognitive dissonance in chess](/en/blog/dissonance-cognitive-aux-echecs/).

**Repetition.** You replay the position several times, you look at the variation, you comment on it. Each pass strengthens the trace, and a strengthened trace becomes indistinguishable from an original memory.

Put another way, the post mortem assembles **more favorable conditions than the lab protocol**, and chess players submit to it voluntarily after every serious game.

## What It Actually Costs

You could judge all this harmless. It isn't, for one precise reason.

Analysis has a purpose: identifying what you need to work on. And that purpose depends entirely on a question only your memory can answer: **did you not see the move, or did you see it and evaluate it badly?**

Those are two completely different diagnoses, calling for two different training regimes.

Not seeing Rxf7 is a **pattern recognition** deficit. The remedy is tactical exercise, in volume, to install the pattern.

Seeing it and evaluating it badly is a **calculation or evaluation** deficit. The remedy is something else entirely: work on positions calculated to the end, verification discipline, training on evaluating the final positions of a variation.

If your memory systematically tells you "I saw it," you always diagnose the second case. So you spend years working on a skill that isn't the one you're missing, on the basis of a memory the analysis itself manufactured.

And that error is invisible, since it shows up only as an absence of progress, which will be blamed on something else.

## The Protection Protocol

The solution has been known to lawyers for a long time, and it's surprisingly simple: **pin down the testimony before exposure to the information**.

### 1. Take Notes Before You Analyze

Right after the game, before any analysis, before the engine, before the conversation with your opponent. Three lines will do:

- the two or three moments where you hesitated;
- for each, the moves you actually considered;
- your evaluation of the position at that moment.

That note is a **record that lives outside your memory**. It's the only thing that will let you know, a week later, what you had seen, because by then your memory will already have absorbed the analysis.

It's the same rule as the one given in our article on [obedience to authority in chess](/en/blog/obeissance-a-l-autorite-aux-echecs/), and that's no coincidence: in both cases what matters is recording your own judgment before a more reliable source replaces it.

### 2. Ban Leading Questions

If you analyze with someone, the wording counts as much as the content.

Don't ask, "did you see Rxf7?" Ask, "what were you looking at here?"

The first contains the answer and triggers a recognition that may be false. The second opens up free recall, which we know produces fewer errors, even if it produces less material.

And if you're the one answering, the best sentence available is simply **"I don't remember."** It's almost always true, it costs nothing, and it's infinitely more useful than a reconstruction.

### 3. Distinguish the Three Possible Answers

For every error identified, one question, with three admissible answers:

- I didn't see it;
- I saw it and dismissed it for such and such a reason;
- **I don't remember**.

The third is legitimate and has to stay available. A system that doesn't allow it forces memory to choose between the other two, and it will choose the flattering one.

### 4. Count Your Categories

After thirty games analyzed this way, count. The ratio between "didn't see it" and "saw it but misjudged it" is your diagnosis, and it rests on notes taken before contamination.

It's probably the most useful information you can produce about your own play, and it costs three lines per game.

## Key Takeaways

One word in one question, a week earlier, is enough to make broken glass appear in the memory of a film that contained none. That's the misinformation effect, and it's solidly established. Implanting entire episodes, on the other hand, is harder than the popular version claims: the Brewin and Andrews review concludes at around 15% full memories, not 25.

But changing a detail is more than enough to cause trouble in chess, because the post mortem assembles every favorable condition: new information, an authoritative source, leading questions, repetition, and a subject with a direct interest in believing he saw it.

What's at stake isn't the accuracy of the memory for its own sake. It's the diagnosis. "I didn't see it" and "I saw it and misjudged it" point to two different weaknesses and two different training regimes. If your memory always answers with the second, you spend years working on the wrong one.

Three lines written before you turn on the engine are enough to preserve the answer.

**After reading:** in your next game, before any analysis, note the two moments where you hesitated and the moves you considered. Then analyze. Compare. The gap is what the analysis would have erased.
