Tournament hall, round 5. A hundred and forty players, eighty tables, and absolute silence. Nobody speaks. Nobody moves around. Nobody looks anywhere but at their own position.

It's a crowd. A hundred and forty people in one room, engaged in the same activity, at the same moment.

And it's the exact opposite of everything crowd psychology described for a century. No communication, no collective action, no shared objective, no contagious emotion permitted.

An anti-crowd. Which acts on you anyway, and measurably.

Three articles in this series have already explored aspects of it: [conformity](/en/blog/conformisme-aux-echecs/), [obedience to authority](/en/blog/obeissance-a-l-autorite-aux-echecs/) and [the bystander effect](/en/blog/effet-spectateur-aux-echecs/). What we're doing here is setting the general frame, and showing that the most famous frame is the wrong one.

## Le Bon, 1895

The founder of the field is a French physician and sociologist, [Gustave Le Bon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crowd:_A_Study_of_the_Popular_Mind), whose *Psychologie des foules* appeared in 1895.

His thesis is spectacular and easy to remember, which explains much of its success.

The individual who joins a crowd **loses what constitutes him**. His conscious personality fades, his judgment disappears, his will dissolves. He becomes, in Le Bon's terms, a kind of automaton who has ceased to be guided by his own will. What takes over is a "collective soul," governed by emotional contagion, suggestibility and impulsiveness.

The crowd, he writes, is always intellectually inferior to the isolated individual.

The book had an immense impact. It was read by sociologists, by military men, by political leaders. Its influence on twentieth-century theories of propaganda is considerable, and not in a good way.

### The Context, Which Explains a Lot

You need to know where Le Bon is speaking from, because it sheds light on what he sees.

He's writing in a France run through with workers' movements, still marked by the Paris Commune, inside a bourgeoisie frightened by the irruption of the masses into political life. His object isn't neutral: the crowd he describes is the one threatening an order he's attached to.

That isn't grounds enough to reject a theory, since a partisan observation can be accurate. But it does tell you where to look for the weaknesses: in what the thesis lets you **avoid explaining**. If the crowd is irrational by nature, there's no point asking what it's demanding.

## What the Research Established

A century of work kept Le Bon's observations and demolished his explanation.

The research carried out in particular by Stephen Reicher and Clifford Stott, based on field observation of riots, demonstrations and sporting gatherings, arrives at a very different model, known as the elaborated social identity model.

Its central claim: **members of a crowd don't lose their identity, they change it**.

The individual doesn't stop defining himself. He moves from a personal definition to a **collective** one, with the norms that come with it. He loses neither his judgment nor his self-control: he applies a judgment and a control informed by a different belonging.

### The Proof, and It's Decisive

The strongest argument for this model has a remarkable elegance, because it bears on a fact Le Bon's thesis can't explain.

**The targets crowds pick aren't arbitrary.**

If people genuinely lost their judgment, collective violence would have to be random. Yet observation consistently shows the opposite: during riots, some buildings get attacked and others don't, some people get targeted and others get protected, and those choices follow coherent lines matching the group's norms.

Selectivity that sharp implies judgment in working order. You can't sort without judging.

The same reasoning applies to online crowds, and it's directly useful here.

## The Deindividuation Model, Revisited

A word on a neighbouring notion, because it gets used constantly to explain behaviour on the internet, and usually wrongly.

The classic idea of deindividuation picks up Le Bon: anonymity and immersion in a group supposedly make you lose your sense of self, hence control over your behaviour, hence your inhibitions.

The model proposed by Reicher, Spears and Postmes turns that reading upside down. Anonymity, cohesion and immersion **don't suppress norms: they strengthen adherence to the group's norms**, by making collective belonging more salient than individual identity.

The consequence matters: an aggressive online chat doesn't prove people have lost their rules there. It proves **which rules apply there**. An anonymous space whose norm is mutual help will produce mutual help, through exactly the same mechanism.

Which shifts responsibility considerably. If anonymity mechanically disinhibited, there'd be nothing to be done. If anonymity strengthens local norms, then moderation, example and explicit rules become the relevant levers.

## The Mechanism That Actually Concerns Chess

Everything above describes acting crowds. But the chess player's crowd doesn't act: it watches. So the relevant mechanism is a different one, and it's better established.

### Zajonc, 1965

In 1965, Robert Zajonc published a theory in *Science* reconciling results on audience effects that had been contradictory until then.

His proposal comes in three steps.

**The mere presence of others raises physiological arousal.** No interaction required.

**Increased arousal favours dominant responses**, meaning the responses most firmly installed in the repertoire.

**So the effect depends entirely on the task.** If it's simple or well learned, dominant responses are correct, and the audience **improves** performance. If it's difficult or poorly mastered, dominant responses are wrong, and the audience **degrades** performance.

It's a rare kind of model: it doesn't say "audiences help" or "audiences hurt," it says exactly when each of those happens.

Worth flagging in passing that the founding experiment of the field, Norman Triplett's on cyclists in 1898, often presented as the first social psychology experiment, was reanalysed in 2005 by Michael Strube, who showed that the reported difference **wasn't statistically significant**. The field stands on the later work, not on its founding myth. That's a regularity this series has run into so often it deserves noting as such.

### The Prediction for a Chess Player

Apply Zajonc's model to a game and you get a precise, testable prediction.

**In well mastered phases**, a known opening, an endgame technique repeated a hundred times, a familiar tactical motif, your dominant responses are correct. The presence of an audience should **improve** your performance: sharper, more assured, faster.

**In novel positions**, the ones demanding original calculation and evaluation without bearings, your dominant responses aren't reliable. The audience should **degrade** your performance.

And the critical positions of a game are, by definition, the ones that don't belong to your automatic repertoire. So they're exactly the ones where the audience hurts.

That explains a commonplace observation that had been poorly understood: players often say the audience doesn't bother them, and yet they play worse in front of one. Both are true. It genuinely doesn't bother them in the eighty percent of the game that's automatic. It bothers them in the twenty percent that decides the result.

## The Chess Player's Three Crowds

### 1. The Tournament Hall, or the Anti-Crowd

Look at what the rules suppress.

**Communication is forbidden.** No speech, no signs, no coordination.

**Collective action is impossible.** Everyone plays their own game, and nothing connects the tables to each other.

**Emotion has to be contained.** Loud displays are punishable.

**Attention is directed inward.** Each player looks at their own board.

In other words, a tournament hall is a device designed, over a century, to **neutralize everything Le Bon described**. It's probably one of the most de-scripted human gatherings in existence.

And yet the effect is there, and every player knows it. The difference in sensation between playing at home and playing in that hall is enormous, even though the position is identical.

Zajonc's model explains it without needing any contagion at all: **mere presence is enough**. Not communication, not shared emotion, not collective identity. The physical presence of other people engaged in the same activity raises your arousal, and that arousal does the rest.

It's also why silence isn't a remedy. It removes the noise, not the presence.

### 2. The Chat, or the Synchronous Crowd

The exact opposite.

On a live broadcast, thousands of people comment in real time, in the relative anonymity of a username, with a heavily marked group identity: its own vocabulary, emotes, shared references, a sense of belonging to a community.

The lazy explanation invokes classic deindividuation: anonymous, therefore disinhibited, therefore aggressive.

The correct model says something else. Anonymity and immersion **strengthen the group's norms**. Which means a chat's behaviour is information about its norms, not about the disappearance of norms.

Two practical consequences. The first is that chats don't resemble one another: depending on the channel, the atmosphere is hostile or remarkably kind, with the same technology and the same anonymity. The second is that these norms get built, mainly through the example set by whoever's broadcasting and through moderation. Our article on [chess and streaming](/en/blog/echecs-et-streaming/) covers that economy.

And for the player broadcasting live, the Zajonc effect applies with particular intensity: the audience is large, its reactions are visible in real time, and it comments on the moves while they're being played.

### 3. The Community, or the Diffuse Crowd

The third one is never gathered in one place, and it may be the most influential.

It sets what's done and what isn't: which openings are respectable, which time controls are serious, what attitude is correct after a loss, from what level you're allowed to have an opinion.

That's the terrain of conformity, covered in a dedicated article in this series. And the social identity model applies directly to it: these norms don't impose themselves by contagion, they apply because you recognize yourself as a member of the group. It's also what makes the unity lever so commercially effective, as our article on [the psychology of persuasion in chess](/en/blog/psychologie-de-la-persuasion-aux-echecs/) shows.

## The Decisive Experiment: When a Crowd Really Played

There's one case, exactly one, where a crowd played chess. And it constitutes the most direct test imaginable of Le Bon's central claim.

In 1999, Garry Kasparov, then world champion, faced the rest of the world. He had white. Black was played by a **collective of more than 50,000 people from more than 75 countries**, with each move decided by plurality vote. The game took place on the internet, over four months.

Let's restate the thesis being tested. Le Bon wrote that the crowd is always intellectually inferior to the isolated individual, and that the collective lowers the level instead of raising it. Here we have fifty thousand people, the overwhelming majority of them amateurs, up against what was arguably the best player in history.

Kasparov won, on move 62. So the collective lost, which is hardly surprising.

But he then called that game **the greatest game in the history of chess**. That isn't politeness: black produced play of a quality nobody expected, with original ideas that took the world champion four months of work.

Which flatly contradicts Le Bon's mechanism. A crowd whose members had lost their judgment would have produced chaotic play, or at best mediocre play matching the average level of the voters. It produced the opposite.

The explanation is perfectly compatible with the social identity model. Those fifty thousand people didn't dissolve: they adopted a shared identity, that of the World Team, with norms of its own, one of them extremely strong, namely **respect for the chess argument**. Strong players, Irina Krush in particular, published detailed analyses that structured the discussion, and the vote followed the reasoning rather than the popularity.

Put another way: this collective didn't operate through emotional contagion but through **deliberation subject to a quality norm**. Which is exactly what Le Bon judged impossible.

The honest qualification has to be added: that game also showed the limits of the procedure. Coordination was difficult, serious disagreements set the analysts against each other, and some moves that won the vote were disputed by the very people who'd understood the position best. A deliberating crowd isn't a superior intelligence: it's a device whose quality depends entirely on its norms and its organization.

Which is, fundamentally, the general conclusion of this whole article.

## Three Mechanisms People Constantly Confuse

Here we need to lay down the distinctions the three other articles go into. They're usually lumped together under "social pressure," when they answer three different questions and call for three different remedies.

**Conformity answers: what are the others doing?** It operates horizontally, between peers. Its force comes not from numbers but from **unanimity**: a single dissenting voice is enough to collapse it. In chess, it governs theoretical fashions and silence in group analysis. So the remedy is to produce dissent, however weak, however wrong.

**Obedience answers: what does the one who knows say?** It operates vertically, and rests on attributed competence. It doesn't need unanimity: a single source is enough if it's legitimate. In chess, it concerns the coach, the titled player, and above all the engine, an authority of a new kind since it's almost infallible and structurally incapable of justifying its verdicts. The remedy isn't dissent but **producing your own judgment first**.

**The bystander effect answers: whose job is it to act?** It bears neither on information nor on authority, but on **responsibility**. In chess, it concerns reporting cheating and harassment, and it comes with a cost asymmetry specific to this world. The remedy is procedural: use a channel, which takes the decision off the terrain of personal appraisal.

The most frequent confusion consists of treating an obedience problem with a conformity remedy. Telling yourself you should "dare to think for yourself" in front of an engine gets you nowhere: it isn't a matter of courage, it's a matter of the order of operations.

## Four Practices

**Automate whatever has to hold up in front of an audience.** That's the direct consequence of Zajonc's model, and the most actionable one. Since the presence of others favours dominant responses, the only protection is to make sure your dominant responses are good. Endgame techniques, tactical motifs and the first moves of your repertoire have to be overlearned to the point of being automatic. What's automatic improves in front of an audience; what's fragile collapses.

**Train in performance conditions.** A player who only works online, alone, prepares his responses in a context with no arousal. Playing regularly in physical company isn't just a matter of enjoyment: it's what makes your repertoire available in the conditions where it'll be needed. The context-dependence phenomenon described in our article on [classical conditioning in chess](/en/blog/conditionnement-classique-aux-echecs/) points the same way.

**Deliberately slow down in novel positions.** Arousal speeds you up and pushes you toward the most available response. That's useful in familiar territory and dangerous in unfamiliar territory. A mechanical rule, along the lines of pausing the moment the position leaves familiar ground, counters this effect without demanding any composure.

**Cut the chat, choose your community.** Since a group's norms apply more strongly the more you identify with it, your choice of the spaces you spend time in isn't trivial. And during a game, no message you receive will help you play better.

## What to Take Away

Le Bon argued in 1895 that the crowd dissolves the individual, erases his judgment and makes him irrational. His thesis had considerable influence and its mechanism is false. Modern work shows that members of a crowd don't lose their identity but switch to a shared one, with norms of its own. The selectivity of targets, impossible without judgment, is the clearest demonstration of it.

The same correction holds for online spaces. Anonymity doesn't remove rules, it strengthens the group's. A hostile chat describes its norms, not their absence.

But the mechanism that actually concerns the chess player is neither contagion nor deindividuation: it's social facilitation. The mere presence of others raises arousal, which improves well learned responses and degrades the ones that aren't.

Hence the paradox of the tournament hall, a device designed to suppress every crowd effect, which produces an effect anyway, because presence is enough. And hence the only real defence: making the responses you want to see under pressure the dominant ones.

**After reading:** identify a technique you know but still have to reconstruct every time. That's exactly the one that'll fail you in front of an audience. Overlearn it until it no longer requires thought.
