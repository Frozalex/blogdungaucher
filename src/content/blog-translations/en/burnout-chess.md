You open your chess platform. The “Play” button is there, just as yesterday. You look at it, then close the tab.

Three months earlier, you read analysis for enjoyment. Now the thought of a game makes you tired. This scene is an example, not a diagnosis. Yet it deserves a question: what place has the game taken in your life?

## What kind of exhaustion are we talking about?

The WHO reserves the term burnout in ICD-11 for the occupational context and does not classify it as a medical condition. We therefore cannot apply that definition directly to every leisure activity.

Sport psychology research also uses the concept of athlete burnout. Gustafsson, DeFreese and Madigan (2017) describe three dimensions: physical and emotional exhaustion, a reduced sense of accomplishment, and devaluation of the activity. They highlight limitations in diagnostic criteria and a lack of intervention research.

These dimensions can help you discuss your experience. They do not allow the conclusion “three signs for two months = burnout”. A lack of motivation, a stagnant rating or a week of fatigue is not enough to identify the cause.

## When playing becomes an obligation

Ask what makes you start the next game. Do you want to? Are you trying to recover rating points? Are you afraid of disappointing your coach or losing your place on the team? The answers may overlap.

The review by Gustafsson and colleagues examines social pressures, motivation and perfectionistic concerns, among other factors. It does not establish a number of weekly hours beyond which a chess player is bound to become exhausted.

You can look at your actual schedule. What have you given up to study one more opening? Is that still a choice that suits you? The same volume can play a very different role depending on your commitments and circumstances.

## Observing without turning a list into a test

A few questions to prepare a conversation with someone you trust:

- When did your desire to play change?
- Does fatigue concern only chess or other activities too?
- What roles do ratings, other people’s expectations and competitions play?
- Can you still decline a session or tournament?
- What would you like to regain: rest, freedom, shared enjoyment?

You do not need to add up answers to obtain a score. If exhaustion persists, causes distress or also interferes with everyday life, a health professional can help investigate its causes. The game does not necessarily explain everything you feel.

## Reducing pressure at your own pace

No single protocol here requires three weeks, two months or six months off. The duration and form of a change depend on your situation. A break is one possibility; reducing commitments or changing the setting are others.

Choose a concrete, reversible measure: remove a competition from your calendar, suspend compulsory sessions, disable platform prompts or set aside time for an activity without a rating goal. These are options to discuss, not treatments validated by this article.

If you are under eighteen and the pressure comes from training or competitions, talk with a trusted adult. The organisation of your practice can change too: it does not all depend on your ability to tolerate more.

## Returning without recreating the same constraint

If you want to play again, begin with a format you choose: a friendly game, a study you find beautiful or a conversation at the club. You do not have to prove immediately that the break was useful by gaining Elo points.

Before resuming previous commitments, describe what you want to change. For example: being able to cancel a session, no longer studying late at night or choosing the next tournament yourself. Set a time to discuss it again with those around you rather than a compulsory date for returning to your best level.

You may also decide to continue your break. Loving a game does not oblige you to play it without interruption.
