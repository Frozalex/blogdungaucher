You want to build a repertoire with Black against 1.e4.

You can pick the Sicilian, the French, the Caro-Kann, the Scandinavian, the Pirc, the Alekhine, the Ruy Lopez, the Petroff, the Philidor. Inside the Sicilian alone you can play the Najdorf, Dragon, Sveshnikov, Taimanov, Kan, Classical, Accelerated Dragon, Kalashnikov.

Three weeks later, you haven't started. You've watched seven videos, bought two books and changed your mind four times.

There's a popular name for this, and it happens to designate an effect research hasn't confirmed. Which doesn't mean your problem is imaginary: it means you have to look for it somewhere else.

## The Jams

The experiment is probably the most cited in contemporary marketing.

In 2000, Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper published a field study in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, run in an upscale grocery store in Menlo Park, California.

A tasting booth offers jams. Depending on the moment, it displays **24** of them or only **6**.

The first result is intuitive: the big display draws far more people, roughly 150% more visitors.

The second one is spectacular. Among those who stopped, **30%** bought at the small display, against **3%** at the big one.

A ratio of ten to one. The result travelled the world, inspired Barry Schwartz's book on the paradox of choice, and became one of the most recycled arguments in design and marketing.

## Then the Meta-Analysis

In 2010, Benjamin Scheibehenne, Rainer Greifeneder and Peter Todd published a systematic review of the question in the *Journal of Consumer Research*.

Their corpus: **63 experimental conditions drawn from 50 published and unpublished experiments, covering 5,036 participants**.

Result: **the average effect is close to zero.**

This isn't an effect smaller than advertised. It's an effect whose average, across all the available data, can't be distinguished from zero. Several experiments even find the opposite: more options make the decision easier and increase satisfaction.

Including unpublished studies matters here, because it partly corrects publication bias: an experiment that finds nothing is less likely to appear in print, which artificially inflates effects in the visible literature. It's a form of [survivorship bias](/en/blog/biais-du-survivant-aux-echecs/) applied to science itself.

### The Useful Part of the Result

You could stop there and conclude the paradox of choice is a myth. That would be moving too fast, and it would miss the most usable piece of information.

Because the authors note something else: **very high variance between studies**.

A null average effect with high variance doesn't mean nothing is happening. It means the effect shows up under some conditions, disappears under others, and sometimes reverses. So the right question isn't "does choice overload exist" but **"under what conditions does it appear"**.

Later work proposed several favourable conditions, and as it happens they describe a club player's situation pretty precisely:

**When the options are hard to compare.** Comparing six jams is easy: taste, price, brand. Comparing the Najdorf and the Caro-Kann requires picturing thousands of positions you've never seen.

**When you have no prior preference.** A shopper who likes strawberry picks from 24 jars in two seconds. A player who doesn't yet know what kind of position suits them has no criterion at all.

**When you lack the expertise to evaluate the options.** This is the decisive point, and it's structural: **choosing a repertoire demands a skill you'll only have after playing the repertoire**. It's the same asymmetry described in our article on [the psychology of persuasion in chess](/en/blog/psychologie-de-la-persuasion-aux-echecs/).

Those three conditions line up almost perfectly in chess. Which explains why the difficulty is real even though the general effect isn't.

## The Real Cost Isn't the One You Think

Here's the point that, to my mind, is missing from every discussion of this subject.

The paradox of choice describes **paralysis at the moment of deciding**. That's what the jam experiment measures: people don't buy.

In chess, that's almost never the real problem. Players always end up picking something. The jam stand empties out.

The real cost lies elsewhere: in **the instability of the choice**.

A player who adopts the French, plays it three months, gets middling results, then moves to the Caro-Kann, then to the Scandinavian, isn't suffering from too many options at the moment of choosing. They're suffering from **resetting their experience** every three months.

And that loss runs far deeper than forgetting a few variations. What gets lost are the **pawn structures**, the typical plans, the characteristic endgames, the recognition of critical moments. In other words everything that makes up the real value of a repertoire, and that accumulates slowly.

That's also what makes the comparison with jams misleading. A jar of jam gets consumed and replaced. A repertoire is an investment whose return grows with how long you hold it.

Which suggests a reformulation: the problem isn't **how many** options, it's **how long** you keep the one you took.

### The Force That Makes Switching Attractive

Worth adding that a repertoire change is almost always triggered by a loss, and that it's then an attractive solution for a bad reason.

Switching openings is a concrete, visible, pleasant action that gives you the feeling of doing something. Working on your endgames offers nothing like that. And above all, switching openings supplies a **retrospective explanation** for the loss that doesn't implicate the player: it wasn't me, it was the opening.

You'll recognise the mechanism described in our article on [cognitive dissonance in chess](/en/blog/dissonance-cognitive-aux-echecs/). Except that here, the dissonance doesn't produce an excuse: it produces an expensive decision.

## The Protocol

### 1. Decide the Duration Before You Decide the Content

This is the main reversal of the article. Before you even choose an opening, fix **how long** you'll play it, and on what conditions you'll be allowed to give it up.

A sample criterion: I play this opening for a minimum of 100 rated games; I only reassess afterwards, and on my overall score, not on my memory of my losses.

Written cold, before any investment, that criterion holds. Written after a loss, it'll get negotiated. It's the direct remedy for effort justification.

### 2. Choose Fast and Badly Rather Than Slowly and Well

Since choosing demands a skill only practice supplies, initial optimisation is largely an illusion. Almost every respectable opening leads to playable positions at your level.

One criterion is enough: take the one whose positions you enjoy looking at. That's a serious criterion, because what you like looking at is what you'll actually work on.

### 3. Reduce the Dimensions Rather Than the Options

The block rarely comes from the raw number, but from the number of dimensions you're comparing at once: solidity, aggression, amount of theory, popularity, fit with your style.

Fix a single dimension and settle it on that. For instance: I want an opening with little theory. The list drops from twenty to three, and the choice becomes possible.

### 4. Separate Repertoire Choice From the Rest of Your Training

Time spent choosing is time not going to tactics or endgames, which are the best-documented items in terms of return. Our article on [the 40-40-20 rule](/en/blog/regle-40-40-20-echecs/) details that split, and [do you really need to study openings](/en/blog/faut-il-vraiment-etudier-les-ouvertures/) takes the problem from the other end.

## What to Take Away

The jam experiment is real and its result is spectacular. But it didn't survive aggregation: across 63 conditions and more than 5,000 participants, the average choice overload effect is close to zero, and sometimes reversed.

What survives, and what's more useful, is high variance: the effect depends on specific conditions. And those conditions are all present in chess, where options are hard to compare, where you have no prior preference, and where evaluating a repertoire demands a skill you'll only have after playing it.

But the main cost isn't the initial paralysis. It's **instability**: a repertoire changed every three months erases the structural experience that constitutes its entire value.

Hence a counterintuitive conclusion. The right question isn't which opening to choose, it's how long to keep it. And that answer, you can write down before you've even chosen.

**After reading:** write your abandonment criterion before you touch your repertoire. A number of games, a score threshold, a date. One sentence is enough, and it beats three weeks of comparison.
