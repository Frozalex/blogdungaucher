Tournament hall, round 2, under-twelve section. The door is closed. In the corridor, twenty or so adults are waiting, some for two hours, holding cold coffees.

A child comes out. He lost. He crosses ten metres of corridor towards his father.

What that father says in the next four seconds will have far more effect on this child's chess career than any opening lesson. And there's a psychological theory that describes precisely what's at stake in those four seconds.

## The Secure Base

[John Bowlby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bowlby), a British psychiatrist and psychoanalyst, formulated from the 1950s onward an idea that would restructure developmental psychology.

A child's need for the person who cares for him isn't only a need for food and protection. It's a need for a **base**. Bowlby describes a behavioural system whose function is to maintain proximity to a caregiver, and whose effect is paradoxical: **the more reliable the base, the further the child goes from it**.

That's the counterintuitive finding of the theory. A child who knows he can come back explores more. A child who isn't sure devotes part of his resources to monitoring the adult's availability, and those resources don't go into exploring.

Three functions define an attachment figure: she's sought out when difficulty rises, she serves as a **safe haven** in distress, and she serves as a **base** from which one dares.

Hold on to that last one, because chess is exactly an activity of exploration under risk.

## The Strange Situation, and Its Limits

In the 1960s and 1970s, [Mary Ainsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Ainsworth) turned the theory into measurable observation with a protocol that became famous: the Strange Situation.

A child of about one year, his mother, and a stranger, in a room with toys. Eight short episodes, including two separations and two reunions. What's observed isn't distress during the separation, contrary to common belief, but **behaviour at the moment of reunion**.

Three profiles emerge, with a fourth added later. The secure child protests at the departure, welcomes the return, is comforted and goes back to playing. The avoidant child seems indifferent to both departure and return, while showing physiological signs of stress. The ambivalent child is severely affected by the separation and hard to comfort on return, oscillating between demand and rejection.

### What the Protocol Also Measures

Here a caveat is needed that popular accounts almost always omit.

In Japan, researcher Keiko Takahashi showed that the Strange Situation worked badly, because it presupposes a norm of care that isn't universal. In a context where a child is very rarely separated from his mother, where they sleep and bathe together, a three-minute separation with a stranger isn't a slightly unusual situation: it's an event. Perfectly secure children ended up classified as ambivalent.

In Germany, Klaus Grossmann's team observed the reverse, with a high proportion of avoidant classifications, in an educational context that valued early autonomy and non-dependence.

The conclusion to keep: the protocol measures a mixture of quality of bond **and** cultural norms of care. The categories remain useful as a descriptive grid. They aren't diagnoses, and they don't predict a destiny.

## From Child to Adult

In 1987, Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver proposed that this system doesn't disappear in adulthood but transfers to close relationships. Their work opened a whole field, with its own controversies, notably about how stable an attachment style really is across a life.

Then the question reached sport. In 2010, Louise Davis and Sophia Jowett tested a simple hypothesis: **does a coach fulfil the functions of an attachment figure?** Their answer is yes. A coach is sought out in difficulty, serves as a haven after failure, and serves as a base from which the athlete dares to take risks. Athletes' attachment styles are associated with the quality of the relationship, particularly perceived social support, the depth of the bond, and the level of interpersonal conflict.

## Why Chess Is a Case Apart

Now for what's specific to our game. It fits in one sentence: **it's one of the rare children's competitions where the adult sees nothing.**

Take any other activity. Football, judo, swimming, dance, tennis: the parent is in the stands. He sees the effort, he sees the moment it turns, he sees his child fighting. When it's over, they have **a shared experience**. The conversation starts from something lived together.

In chess, the parent is in a corridor. The hall is closed, silence is mandatory, access is forbidden. For one to four hours, he knows nothing.

Three consequences follow.

**The child goes through the ordeal entirely alone.** No half-time, no coach at the touchline, no teammate. That's the subject of our article on [solitude in chess](/en/blog/echecs-et-la-solitude/), and it's worse when you're ten.

**The parent receives only a result.** No context, no nuance, no image of the effort made. Just a letter on a pairing sheet. Which mechanically concentrates all his attention on that letter.

**The meeting is abrupt and immediate.** The child goes from the table to a face in three seconds. And he reads that face before he even hears the sentence. In Bowlby's vocabulary, this moment is a **reunion**, exactly in the sense of the Strange Situation. That's where the reliability of the base gets checked.

## Three Configurations

### The Parent Whose Mood Tracks the Result

The most frequent, and rarely intentional.

A parent can be perfectly kind, never reproach a loss, and still communicate a very clear piece of information: his face changes with the result. Enthusiasm after a win, slightly emphatic gentleness after a loss.

The child, who is an excellent reader of faces because that's a survival skill, learns one thing: **the result changes the state of the person he depends on**. He doesn't learn that he'll be rejected. He learns that his performance has an effect, and that's enough to transform the next game.

From then on he no longer plays against his opponent. He plays against the corridor. And he'll start preferring draws, avoiding risky positions, no longer exploring. The base stops being a point of departure and becomes a tribunal.

### The Coach Who Became the Only One

Chess encourages long and exclusive teaching relationships. A child may work with the same coach for years, often one to one, on a subject that structures his entire identity.

It's a powerful configuration when it works: Davis and Jowett show that a coach properly fulfilling the secure-base function enables risk-taking, and therefore improvement. You dare a new opening, a sacrifice, a tournament above your level, because you know where to come back to.

And it's a risky configuration when it goes wrong. An attachment figure has a lever no ordinary teacher possesses: making approval conditional. That's what makes certain coaching relationships so hard to leave, even when they're doing harm. Our article on [gaslighting in chess](/en/blog/gaslighting-aux-echecs/) describes a neighbouring mechanism.

### The Adult Player With No Base Left

The theory doesn't only concern children.

An adult playing a tournament lives exactly the same structure: a solitary, long ordeal with a clear-cut outcome, followed by a return towards someone. That someone may be a partner, a friend, a club group, or nobody.

That last option is more common than people think, and it has a cost. With no recipient for the experience, the loss stays whole. It's one of the documented paths towards [burnout in chess](/en/blog/burnout-chess/): not too many games, but too many games with nobody to tell them to.

## Why People Quit

Federations know the phenomenon well: many promising young players stop abruptly, often around twelve or thirteen, with level having nothing to do with it.

Attachment theory offers a reading worth setting alongside the usual explanations. If playing the game has become the place where the solidity of a bond gets tested, then **quitting isn't giving up chess: it's ending a permanent evaluation**. It isn't abandonment, it's protection.

Which explains something parents often find incomprehensible: quitting frequently happens **after a good period**. From the standpoint of results, that's absurd. From the standpoint of load, it's logical: the better it goes, the more there is to lose.

## What to Do

### If You're a Parent

**Change the first question.** This is the most effective and least costly lever in the entire article. Don't ask the result first. Ask how long the game lasted, whether there was an interesting position, or whether he's hungry. The result will arrive anyway, within thirty seconds. What matters is what comes before it.

**Be steady rather than fair.** Your face transmits more than your words. A moderate reaction after a win costs a little shared pleasure and returns a great deal: it shows that your state doesn't depend on the result.

**Separate the analysis from the greeting.** The post-game isn't the moment for the lesson. The return in the corridor is a reunion, not a debrief. The game can be looked at tomorrow, and it's more effective that way anyway, as our guide on [how to analyze your games](/en/blog/analyser-ses-parties/) explains.

### If You're a Coach

**Accept the function, and its limit.** You're probably an attachment figure for your youngest students. That means your approval carries disproportionate weight, and that weight has to stay **unconditional about the person** while staying demanding about the work. The two are compatible, but only if the distinction is stated explicitly, and often.

**Encourage exploration, that's your main function.** A student who no longer dares play a risky move in your presence has stopped perceiving you as a base.

### If You're an Adult Player

**Find a recipient.** An analysis partner, a club group, a forum. The safe-haven function doesn't disappear with age, it simply changes address. A loss that gets told isn't the same as a loss kept.

## What to Take Away

Bowlby showed that exploration depends on security: you dare in proportion to knowing where to come back to. Chess is an activity of exploration under risk, practised in complete solitude, with an unambiguous result.

It also has a rare property: the adult witnesses nothing. The child crosses alone, then crosses ten metres of corridor towards a face that saw nothing and knows only one thing, the result. That moment is a reunion in the strict sense of the theory, and it's where the essential happens.

Ainsworth's categories have to be handled carefully: they also measure cultural norms, as the Japanese and German work showed. But the base mechanism is robust and directly actionable. Your first sentence in the corridor counts more than your evening analysis.

**After reading:** if you accompany a child to a tournament, prepare in advance the question you'll ask when he comes out, and make sure it isn't about the result. One sentence, decided beforehand, that doesn't depend on what happened.
