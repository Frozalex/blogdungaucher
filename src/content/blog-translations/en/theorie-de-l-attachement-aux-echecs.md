The round ends. A child leaves the playing hall and looks for their parent. They lost. You want to understand the game; they may mainly need a breather.

Attachment theory offers one way to reflect on that moment: can someone be relied on when things go wrong? It does not permit diagnosing a child from the way they leave a room or predicting their career from something their father says.

## A base for exploration

In Davis and Jowett’s work, a coach may be perceived as a secure base, a safe haven in difficulty and someone whose proximity is sought. This vocabulary describes a relationship; it does not mean a coach replaces a parent.

The 2010 survey concerns 309 student athletes aged 18 to 28. Responses associate certain attachment styles with satisfaction in the relationship and in sport. They are neither young children nor a sample of chess players. The study therefore cannot demonstrate that the response after a loss causes someone to quit chess.

## What the 2014 study adds

Davis and Jowett subsequently examine responses from 192 athletes. Attachment, perceived support, relationship depth, conflict and affect are associated in their analyses. The two publications are distinct: the 2014 findings should not be attributed to the 2010 paper.

These questionnaires do not, by themselves, demonstrate a causal chain from coach behaviour to well-being. Above all, they invite attention to relationship quality beyond sporting results.

## Welcoming a child without making the exit an examination

If you accompany a young player, you can start by asking whether they want to talk now. A question about hunger, tiredness or wanting to see friends leaves room for something other than the score. Then offer analysis rather than imposing it.

You can acknowledge disappointment without confirming every explanation. “I understand you are disappointed” does not imply that the clock, opponent or arbiter is responsible. Conversely, do not assume an excuse before hearing what happened.

Access for accompanying adults depends on tournament arrangements. Not every parent is excluded from every playing hall. If you did not see the game, say so: you have the child’s account, not a complete observation.

![Offering a conversation after the game](/images/en/attachement-apres-partie.svg)

## A reliable coach can also set boundaries

Availability does not require responding at every hour, avoiding disagreements or promising improvement. If you coach, clarify schedules, objectives and how to discuss a decision. An explicit framework avoids making every conversation depend on the mood after the last tournament.

If you play, you can request precise technical feedback without accepting humiliation. Distinguish criticism of a choice, such as a miscalculated variation or irregular training, from a judgement on your worth.

## A perspective, not a label

A quiet child may be tired, disappointed, absorbed or simply not talkative. An adult who analyses immediately may enjoy understanding their game. These isolated behaviours do not reveal an attachment style.

No established threshold at twelve or thirteen is presented here beyond which a relationship explains quitting. If discomfort persists, ask what is weighing on the player: tournament frequency, the coach relationship, family expectations or a desire to play differently. The answer deserves to be heard before reaching for a theory.
