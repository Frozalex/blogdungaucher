A club player defends their gambit with three beautiful games. Those games are real. But to know what they show, one question is missing: how many less successful games did they leave in the folder?

Survivorship bias appears when the filter making certain cases visible disappears from our reasoning. A success can be instructive without constituting a representative sample.

## The aircraft that returned from missions

In 1943, Abraham Wald worked at Columbia’s Statistical Research Group on estimating aircraft vulnerability from damage to survivors. His memoranda were reprinted in 1980 by the Center for Naval Analyses.

His problem involves missing data: aircraft that are shot down do not return to provide their damage records. The hits observed on returning aircraft therefore do not directly describe the vulnerability of the entire fleet. The calculations require assumptions about damage and survival.

The lesson goes beyond “reinforce where there are no holes”. An area with few hits among survivors does not, by itself, prove that every hit there would be fatal. We must examine the selection mechanism and the model’s assumptions.

## Advice from a player who succeeded

When a grandmaster describes their progress, their experience can teach you a great deal. It does not show how many players followed a comparable method without achieving the same result.

Ask what is being offered: an account, a technical explanation or an estimate of a programme’s effectiveness. An account need not be false to become misleading when turned into a guarantee.

The Polgár sisters’ success does not, by itself, constitute a controlled experiment separating every influence on playing strength. Sufficiently comparable situations would be needed. Imagining thousands of families that disappeared from the statistics does not replace those data either.

## Recorded games do not represent all play

A database depends on its collection rules: competitions covered, time controls, playing strength, publication and period. Before reading an opening percentage, examine those criteria and the number of games.

A high score does not prove that a variation offers a theoretical advantage. The players choosing it, their opponents and the circumstances may differ. Conversely, a line’s absence from a book demonstrates neither its refutation nor hidden excellence.

Opening theory may also incorporate analysis of positions never played. Reducing it to spectacular published games alone would be another generalisation.

## Your own gambit

Revisit your wins, draws and losses with this opening. Record the results, but also the opponent’s rating, your colour and the time control. If the sample is small, retain that uncertainty in your conclusion.

A gap between your memory and the recorded score can have several causes: selective memories, forgotten games, a recent run or simple imprecision. It is not an isolated, exact measurement of survivorship bias.

To go further, choose a few ordinary losses to [analyse](/en/blog/how-to-analyze-chess-games/). They may reveal a difficulty that never appears in your three best attacks.

## The tournament book nobody prints

Invent a club of thirty members and give all thirty the same offer: send in one game for the annual bulletin. Twelve members respond. Eight submit wins, three submit draws and one sends a remarkable defeat. The editor selects four attacking wins for the cover feature. An interested reader concludes that this is a remarkably aggressive club. Which population did that feature actually describe? There were thirty members, twelve contributors, eight wins among their submissions and four games chosen for a particular theme. At every step something happened that could change the sample.

The last selection is obvious because the cover says “Four great attacks”. The earlier selection is harder to see. A member who lost every game might feel they had nothing worth sending. A member whose most instructive game ended in a draw might prefer to keep it for a lesson. Equally, a busy player with five wins may simply miss the deadline. You cannot recover the missing games by declaring that every absent member was unsuccessful. You can recover some information by asking for all the games from one round, or by randomly assigning a round to each volunteer before anyone knows the results.

Now change the editorial aim. An anthology of attacking patterns is perfectly entitled to feature successful attacks. The reader wants to learn what an open file can look like when it works. The difficulty starts when the anthology is used to estimate how often opening that file succeeds. To answer that second question, collect comparable positions where someone had the option to open the file. Include those where they chose another plan and those where the attack fizzled into a worse ending. The same book can be a superb source of ideas and a poor estimator of their frequency.

## The invitation that changes the apparent success rate

Consider a voluntary training group. Twenty players start a difficult course, twelve complete it and six of the twelve gain rating points over the next season. An advertisement announces that half of those who finished improved. That is arithmetically correct under the terms of the example. It leaves open what happened to the eight who did not finish. If none gained points, six out of the original twenty improved. If all eight did, fourteen out of twenty improved. The completion statistic alone is compatible with both results. Neither result, even if known, establishes that the course caused the change.

What should a coach ask for? Start with the choice you want to make. If it is whether to take the course alongside a full-time job, the experiences of people who stopped for lack of time are central. Ask about the weekly workload, where people tended to fall behind and whether they could restart later. If it is whether the exercises teach rook endings, ask for positions attempted before and after the course, including errors. A promotion photograph of the best finisher answers neither question, though it may be a truthful and enjoyable account of that player's season.

This point can help when interviewing a grandmaster at a club evening. Ask which parts of a training routine were available to other juniors who did not continue. A coach who remembers an entire junior group may supply a perspective that complements the champion's first-person account. Avoid assuming either person has a complete record. Ask for contemporary tournament books, schedules or exercises and distinguish a remembered habit from a date that can be checked. The goal is to learn enough about the conditions to decide which part of the advice travels to your own situation.

## Rebuild your opening notebook

Choose one opening decision that you remember as a particular success. Specify the position before your decision rather than searching for a spectacular final pattern. Search your own games for that position, including transpositions if your archive permits it. Record which games are missing altogether: perhaps the club only kept the scores from the top two boards. An empty search result can mean that you never reached the position or simply that those games were never stored. Mark that distinction explicitly; it changes what you can learn from the exercise.

For each game you do find, hide the result and look at the next three decisions. Was the compensation for the pawn still visible after the opponent's most testing reply? Could you explain how to continue once the initial threats were covered? Now restore the results. A game you won on time may be a poor advertisement for the opening and an excellent reminder to play quickly in a difficult ending. A game you lost after achieving a strong position might preserve precisely the opening idea you were hoping to learn. Divergence between the position and the final result is evidence worth keeping.

Write two annotations to take to the next game. One should name a condition under which you would choose the gambit again. The other should name the defensive resource that would make you reconsider. Put both on the same page beside the move order. That arrangement forces the awkward example to remain available at the moment of decision. A folder containing nothing but brilliancies is more enjoyable to browse after dinner. It is less helpful when your opponent remembers the quiet move that nobody wanted to put in the bulletin.

## When a missing case stays missing

Sometimes you cannot find a reliable denominator at all. A retired player remembers giving up chess after an unpleasant event, but nobody kept a list of the other juniors who left. Preserve the account as an account. It can identify a concrete problem, such as the cost of travel to away matches, and suggest something the club can investigate now. It cannot establish how many players from that generation left for the same reason. Asking today's members how they managed the cost would select for people who found some way to continue. Ask former participants as well, if they want to be heard, and say clearly which voices you were able to reach.

The habits in these examples have a common practical payoff. Before trusting a percentage or copying a champion's move, finish the sentence “This conclusion would change if the missing cases included …”. Naming one plausible missing case tells you where to look next. One well-documented counterexample may teach you more about the limits of an idea than adding another ten glorious wins to the file.

Next time you show the archive to a teammate, leave the original selection rule at the top of the page. A future reader who knows why the games entered the collection can use what they contain without accidentally generalising beyond it.
