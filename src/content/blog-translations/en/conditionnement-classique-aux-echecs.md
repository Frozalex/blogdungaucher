Your opponent plays the move that leads to that structure. You know it. You've analyzed it. You checked with the engine that it's perfectly playable, and even slightly in your favor.

And still, something contracts. Your rhythm changes. You play the next three moves faster than you should, for no reason you can name.

You're not thinking anything negative about this position. You're **reacting** to it. And the difference between the two is the whole subject of this article.

## Pavlov, and What We Get Wrong About Him

The story is famous to the point of being a joke, which eventually erased its content.

Ivan Pavlov, a Russian physiologist, was studying digestion. He measured dogs' salivation in response to food. Then he noticed something that wasn't his object of study: the dogs started salivating **before** the food arrived, at the sound of the assistant's footsteps.

He formalized the mechanism. A stimulus that starts out neutral, presented regularly just before a stimulus that naturally triggers a response, ends up triggering that response on its own.

The point to remember, and the one we systematically forget, is that **the dog decides nothing**. It didn't conclude that the sound announced food. It didn't form a belief. An association got installed, and it produces a physiological response.

That's what radically separates this mechanism from [operant conditioning](/en/blog/conditionnement-operant-aux-echecs/), covered in the previous article in this series. Operant conditioning is about the consequences of your actions and shapes what you do. Classical conditioning is about involuntary associations and shapes what you feel.

## Little Albert, and Why He Should Be Cited Carefully

The extension to human emotion runs through a famous and deeply problematic experiment.

In 1920, John Watson and Rosalie Rayner showed an infant of about nine months, referred to as "Albert B.," a white laboratory rat. The child wasn't afraid of it. Then, on several occasions, the rat's appearance was accompanied by a loud noise made behind him.

After a few pairings, the child showed distress at the mere sight of the rat. The authors also reported generalization to other furry objects.

This study is in every textbook. It deserves to be cited with serious reservations.

**A single subject.** No statistical generalization is possible.

**Subjective measures.** The fear assessment rests on the experimenters' judgment, with no standardized criterion.

**Weak, unstable reactions.** Ben Harris, in a critical analysis published in 1979, notes that Albert's reactions were far less clear-cut than the legend has it, and that the pairings had to be renewed to keep them going.

**No desensitization procedure.** The child left the study with nothing undone.

**And even his identity is still disputed.** One team proposed in 2009 that he was a certain Douglas Merritte; another argued in 2014 that he was more likely William Albert Barger, whose age and residency dates fit the account better.

Put differently: the emotional conditioning mechanism is solidly established **elsewhere**, notably by modern work on fear conditioning. Little Albert is a historical illustration of it, not a proof.

## What Gets Conditioned at the Board

Here's where the mechanism operates in you, and it operates more than you'd think.

### A position structure

The most frequent case. You've taken three painful losses in a given structure, with real distress: the feeling of understanding nothing, the clock running down, the helplessness.

The association settles in between the visual configuration and the internal state. After that, the position alone is enough.

And this is where the mechanism gets expensive: **the state it triggers genuinely degrades your play**, which produces another bad result, which reinforces the association. The loop is the one described in our article on [the self-fulfilling prophecy in chess](/en/blog/prophetie-auto-realisatrice-aux-echecs/), with a physiological trigger on top.

### An opponent

The club player you never beat. His presence on the pairing sheet alone changes your state before the first move.

The objective record often doesn't justify the reaction: go check your actual score against him, it's rarely as catastrophic as your body suggests.

### Peripheral signals

These are the most surprising ones, and they're very real.

The sound of a digital flag. The smell of a tournament hall. The particular light of a municipal gym. The interface of a given platform.

None of these elements has anything to do with chess. All of them were present, dozens of times, at the moment you were feeling something intense. They became signals, and they trigger the response before the first piece even moves.

It's also why some players play distinctly worse over the board than online at equal strength: it isn't the time control, it's the context, loaded with associations your home doesn't contain.

## Why Knowing Isn't Enough

Here's the most useful point in this article.

You can check with the engine that the position is fine. You can repeat to yourself that your score against this opponent is three all. You can understand the mechanism described here perfectly.

None of that will remove the reaction.

Because an association **doesn't come undone by argument**. It installed itself through repeated pairing, and it comes undone through repeated pairing. Reasoning and conditioning don't take the same route.

That's good news disguised as bad. It means you don't have a willpower problem or a clear-headedness problem, and that beating yourself up for "knowing but not managing to" is wasted energy. It also means the solution exists, and that it's procedural.

## Extinction, and Its Limits

The undoing mechanism is called **extinction**: presenting the conditioned stimulus repeatedly **without** the unpleasant stimulus, until the response fades.

Three important qualifications.

**Avoidance blocks extinction.** This is the decisive point. Every time you avoid the structure you dread, you deny the association the chance to weaken. Avoidance relieves in the short term and guarantees persistence in the long term.

**Extinction doesn't erase, it covers over.** The original association stays in place; a new one is layered on top. That's why spontaneous recovery is well documented, along with context-dependent renewal: a reaction extinguished online can resurface in a tournament hall.

**It takes volume.** Two or three exposures won't do it. This is work counted in dozens of repetitions.

## The Protocol

**1. Identify the precise trigger.** Not "I play closed positions badly," but the exact configuration, the exact opponent, the exact moment. A vague trigger can't be exposed.

**2. Separate the reaction from the evaluation.** Go get the fact: the real evaluation of the structure, your real score against this player. That won't remove the reaction, and it isn't the goal. It keeps you from adding a false belief to a real reaction.

**3. Expose yourself at low stakes.** Deliberately play the position you dread, in training or unrated games. Reduced stakes are essential: if every exposure comes with intense stress, you're reinforcing the association instead of extinguishing it.

**4. Go for volume.** Twenty to thirty occurrences, not three. Write them down, because your sense of progress is unreliable here.

**5. Vary the contexts.** Since extinction is context-sensitive, work done only online will transfer poorly to tournaments. If your target is the tournament hall, part of the work has to happen there.

**6. Expect the comeback.** The partial return of the reaction is normal and documented. It doesn't mean the work failed, only that the original association wasn't erased. Knowing this keeps you from quitting right when it's working.

## Key Takeaways

Pavlov showed that an association between two stimuli is enough to produce a physiological response, with no decision and no belief involved. Watson claimed to extend that to human emotion with an experiment so methodologically weak that today it counts as historical illustration and not as proof.

In chess, this mechanism explains why certain positions, certain opponents and even certain sounds trigger a state that degrades your play, independently of any objective evaluation. And above all it explains why knowing the position is fine changes nothing: an association doesn't come undone by reasoning.

What undoes it is repeated exposure without an unpleasant consequence. What preserves it is avoidance, which relieves every time and costs every time.

**After reading:** identify a position you avoid, and deliberately play it ten times in unrated games. Note what you feel on the first one and on the tenth. It's the only protocol that acts on this mechanism.
