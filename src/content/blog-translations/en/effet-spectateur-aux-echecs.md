A player strings together fifteen games with engine-grade accuracy, in an open tournament, in front of two hundred spectators. The chat comments. Some laugh. A few write that it's obvious.

Nobody reports it.

The explanation that springs to mind is called the bystander effect, and it's been taught everywhere for fifty years. In this particular case it's also, probably, wrong. Which makes the whole thing more interesting.

## The Parable, and What It Hides

It starts with a story, and the story has to be told because the story is what created the field.

On 13 March 1964, in New York, Kitty Genovese was murdered near her building in Queens. A few days later the *New York Times* ran a piece explaining that **38 witnesses** had seen or heard the attack from their windows, and that not one of them called the police.

The article travelled the world. It set off a national debate about urban indifference, it inspired research, and it became the obligatory illustration in every social psychology textbook.

In 2007, Rachel Manning, Mark Levine and Alan Collins published a fact-check in *American Psychologist*. They went back to the court archives, the police files and the press coverage of the period to test the three claims in the story.

All three collapse.

**The figure of 38 appears nowhere in the police files.** It comes from no verifiable source.

**Nobody watched the murder.** The attack happened in two stages, roughly ten minutes apart and in two different locations, the second one out of sight of the windows.

**People did intervene.** At least two neighbours called the police. One man shouted from his window and drove the attacker away, which broke off the first assault. And a friend came downstairs to hold Kitty Genovese in her arms while she died.

The authors conclude that the story works better as a modern parable than as a piece of history.

This series has run into the same situation before, with the Dunning-Kruger curve and Abraham Wald's aircraft: **the most famous illustration of a phenomenon is often the one that best survived being simplified**, which is a criterion for spreading, not for being true.

## What the Research Actually Established

None of which makes the phenomenon itself an invention. It's been studied seriously, independently of the story that launched it.

From 1968 onwards, John Darley and Bibb Latané built laboratory situations where a participant faces an apparent emergency, either alone or with other people present. Their finding is robust: **the more witnesses there are, the less likely any given individual is to intervene, and the longer he takes**.

They identify three mechanisms, and they need to be kept apart because they don't have the same remedies.

**Diffusion of responsibility.** With twenty witnesses, each one carries a twentieth of the obligation to act.

**Pluralistic ignorance.** Faced with an ambiguous situation, you watch the others to work out whether it's an emergency. If nobody reacts, everybody concludes it isn't one. Everyone reassures everyone else, while all of them are worried.

**Evaluation apprehension.** Stepping in means risking being wrong in public.

### What the Recent Data Changed

Two results have seriously resized the phenomenon.

In 2011, Peter Fischer and his colleagues published a meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin* covering fifty years of work. The effect exists, but it **weakens markedly when the situation is perceived as dangerous**, and it can even reverse. Put another way: the more serious the situation, the less the bystander effect operates.

In 2020, Richard Philpot and his team went one better than the lab: they analysed CCTV recordings of **219 real public conflicts** in Amsterdam, Cape Town and Lancaster.

Result: **someone intervened in 91% of cases**.

Nine times out of ten. So the reasonable conclusion is that the bystander effect is a real phenomenon, measurable in the lab, and **much weaker under real conditions than the parable would have you believe**.

## So Why Does Nothing Move in Chess?

This is where the article becomes useful, because the obvious diagnosis is probably the wrong one.

If people intervene in 91% of public conflicts, then the chess community's silence in the face of blatant cheating or online harassment needs a different explanation. Three reasons, and the main one has nothing to do with diffusion of responsibility.

### 1. The Asymmetry of Costs, and It's Overwhelming

Here's the dominant factor, and it's specific to this field.

Reporting cheating means making an accusation. And the chess community now has explicit case law on this point: in July 2026, the FIDE Ethics Commission sanctioned a former world champion for, among other things, unfounded public accusations and psychological abuse, establishing that naming identifiable players publicly without institutional verification causes them unwarranted harm. Our article on [gaslighting in chess](/en/blog/gaslighting-aux-echecs/) goes through that decision in detail.

That position is right. It protects players against a genuinely destructive mechanism.

It also has a mechanical consequence: **it makes reporting costly and silence free**.

Look at the matrix from the point of view of a player who has a doubt. If he says nothing and he was right, nothing happens to him. If he says nothing and he was wrong, nothing happens to him. If he speaks up and he was right, he gains nothing. If he speaks up and he was wrong, he's exposed to a sanction and to a reputation as an accuser.

Three boxes out of four reward silence. That isn't cowardice, it's arithmetic.

This is the real dilemma in the chess world, and it has no simple solution: the two protections at stake, the honest player wrongly accused and the competition itself against cheating, pull in opposite directions.

### 2. The Ambiguity Is Total

Pluralistic ignorance requires an ambiguous situation. In chess, ambiguity is the norm.

How do you tell a cheat from a player improving fast, from an underrated player, or simply from a player in form? The honest answer is: not with the naked eye. Serious detection rests on statistical analysis that platforms and federations run with tools no spectator has access to.

So a spectator who has "an impression" holds, literally, no usable information. And he knows it.

### 3. The Channel Is Invisible

In the street, calling the police is an action everyone knows about. Online, the report function nearly always exists, but it's buried in a menu, its effect is invisible, and nobody has ever received any feedback after using it.

A channel you don't know exists and whose effect you never see isn't a channel.

## The Three Concrete Situations

**Suspected cheating.** The useful rule fits into one distinction, and it's decisive: **reporting isn't accusing**. Passing an observation to the platform or the arbiter means supplying information to a body that has the means to investigate and the obligation to do it discreetly. Posting a public message means delivering a verdict without the means to reach one. The first action is useful and carries no risk; the second is the one that got sanctioned.

**Online harassment.** Here the ambiguity disappears, and with it the main excuse. An insulting message is visible and requires no expertise to read. This is the case where the classic bystander effect really does operate, and where a single report changes the situation, if only by breaking pluralistic ignorance for the other witnesses.

**Problem behaviour at the club.** The hardest setup, because it adds a social cost: everyone knows everyone and everyone will be back next Tuesday. It's also the one where Philpot's result is most encouraging: in real life, someone almost always ends up acting. Often all it takes is being that someone first, because the first person to speak transforms the situation for everybody else.

## Four Principles

**Report through the channel, never in public.** It's the only action that's both useful and risk-free. It hands the information to whoever can act on it, and it takes you out of the matrix described above.

**Don't wait for certainty before reporting.** A report isn't a verdict, it's a data point. Deciding is precisely the job of the governing bodies, and they have tools for it that you don't.

**Be first, the effect is disproportionate.** Pluralistic ignorance works by having everyone take their cue from everyone else's inaction. One visible reaction breaks the loop for the whole group.

**Don't confuse the two problems.** Defending a wrongly accused player and reporting probable cheating aren't opposite positions. They're two applications of the same principle: facts get handled by the people who can establish them.

## What to Take Away

The founding story of the bystander effect is false. There were no 38 passive witnesses, the attack wasn't visible, and several people did intervene. The 2007 fact-check is conclusive.

The phenomenon does exist in the lab, but it's far weaker in real life than people believe: CCTV footage of 219 public conflicts shows intervention in 91% of cases, and the effect weakens when the situation is dangerous.

Which means the chess community's silence needs another explanation. It's arithmetic. An unfounded accusation is punishable, rightly so; failing to report isn't. Three boxes out of four reward silence, and the doubt is nearly always there because cheating can't be detected with the naked eye.

The only way out is to stop treating a report as an accusation. It isn't one, and that's exactly why the procedures exist.

**After reading:** go and find where the report function lives on the platform you play on. If you don't know where it is, you've just identified the real reason you've never used it.
