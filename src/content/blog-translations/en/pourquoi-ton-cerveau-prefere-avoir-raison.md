An engine displays an evaluation that contradicts yours. You have two seconds to decide what you're going to do with that information.

The first route is fast. It consists of working out why the engine says that, absorbing the variation, telling yourself you'd seen the idea but misjudged the follow-up, then moving on to the next game. It costs thirty seconds and it changes nothing in you.

The second route is slow. It consists of admitting your evaluation was wrong, hunting for what you can't see, accepting that this hole probably exists in hundreds of your games, and rebuilding your training plan accordingly. It costs weeks, and it hurts.

You almost always take the first one. That isn't laziness, and this article exists to explain why.

It closes a series of forty-one texts on the psychology of chess. What follows isn't a summary: it's what became visible once all the others had been written.

## A Badly Framed Question

We usually state the problem in terms of character. What you'd need is humility, courage, a capacity for self-questioning.

That framing leads nowhere, for a simple reason: it turns a mechanism into a moral failing, and a moral failing can't be corrected with a procedure.

The useful framing is economic. **Being right and learning don't carry the same price.**

Keeping a coherent self-image is fast, automatic, free, and brings immediate relief. Revising that image demands time, attention, a tolerance for discomfort, and pays out nothing for several weeks.

Faced with a cost gap that wide, there's nothing surprising about a system that picks the first option every single time. It isn't malfunctioning. **It's optimising for something other than accuracy.**

What it's optimising is the stability of the model you hold of yourself. And that stability isn't a luxury: it's what lets you decide, commit, and start over after a loss. A player whose self-image got fully rewritten after every defeat would be incapable of playing the next game.

So the problem isn't that this system exists. It's that it has no off switch, and that it keeps running in exactly the cases where revision would be the right thing to do.

## The Chain

Going back over the forty preceding articles, an order emerges. These mechanisms aren't a collection of independent biases: they fire in sequence, each one covering the moment the previous one can't reach.

### Before: the structural blind spot

Everything starts with an impossibility.

The effect Kruger and Dunning described isn't that weak performers think they're strong. It's that **the skills required to do something well are the same skills required to judge whether you're doing it well**. When they're missing, they're missing twice.

That's the mandatory starting point, because it determines everything downstream: you can't see what you don't know how to see. And the 2025 study of 3,388 tournament players shows that even accurate, precise, public and permanent feedback isn't enough to correct it. On average they placed themselves 89 Elo points above their actual rating, after nearly twenty years of practice.

That's the subject of our article on [the Dunning-Kruger effect in chess](/en/blog/effet-dunning-kruger-aux-echecs/).

### During: the directed search

The second mechanism doesn't wait for the result. It works while you're calculating.

[Confirmation bias](/en/blog/biais-de-confirmation-aux-echecs/) doesn't make you calculate less: it makes you calculate **in one direction only**. Eight minutes on the side that proves you right, a few seconds on the side that would prove you wrong. The tree isn't too short, it's lopsided.

Wason demonstrated this with three numbers: participants tested their hypothesis using examples incapable of disproving it, then declared themselves certain at the end of an empty demonstration.

And while the search runs in one direction, [projection](/en/blog/projection-aux-echecs/) fills in the blanks. Modelling your opponent's mind is expensive; absent explicit effort, the system fills it with your own content. You assume he saw what you saw, and didn't see what you didn't see.

### At the moment of the result: the discomfort

The game is lost. Two incompatible cognitions turn up: I'm a decent player, I've just lost a winning position.

That's [cognitive dissonance](/en/blog/dissonance-cognitive-aux-echecs/), and Festinger showed it carries a motivational force of its own. It doesn't ask to be understood: it asks to stop.

Three exits are available. Changing the first cognition is expensive, it touches who you are. Changing the second is impossible, the result is on record. Which leaves the third route, the one that consists of adding a cognition that reconciles the other two.

### Three minutes later: the explanation

That third cognition arrives fast, and it's produced by a remarkably efficient system.

[Rationalisation](/en/blog/rationalisation-aux-echecs/) supplies an explanation that's acceptable, coherent, and often accurate. You were tired. It was blitz. Your preparation was poor.

The most unsettling experiment in the field shows that this system works even when it has access to nothing: in a 2005 protocol, the photograph chosen by participants was secretly swapped, and 87% of them explained with conviction why they'd preferred a face they had actually rejected.

They weren't lying. The system that produces justifications is separate from the system that produces decisions, and it justifies whatever it's handed.

Which gives us the decisive test: **an explanation produced in three minutes, without consulting the facts, isn't the result of an investigation. It's an output.** Its accuracy is a happy accident.

And what that explanation costs isn't dishonesty. It's the premature end of the search, accompanied by the feeling of having understood.

### What's left: the defences

Whatever rationalisation doesn't absorb gets handled by a broader repertoire, catalogued in our article on [defence mechanisms in chess](/en/blog/mecanismes-de-defense-aux-echecs/).

Denial doesn't consist of denying the result, but of not looking at the game. Displacement spends thirty minutes on the noise in the playing hall and zero on move 22. Intellectualisation, the hardest one to spot, analyses competently while never once mentioning what was felt, because the board doesn't hurt.

It's worth restating here that this model is fragile: the DSM-5 dropped the defensive functioning scale that appeared in the DSM-IV appendix, partly for lack of empirical support. What remains is a useful descriptive typology, not a validated causal system.

### Over years: the normalisation

The last mechanism is the slowest and probably the most decisive.

[Reconstructive memory](/en/blog/memoire-reconstructive-aux-echecs/) doesn't replay a recording: it rebuilds. And Bartlett showed that this rebuilding isn't random: it normalises toward the schema of the person doing the remembering.

Your schema, here, is the idea you have of your own play. Every time you recall a game, the elements that don't fit fade a little. After a few years you're holding a remarkably coherent body of memories describing a player who doesn't exist.

And since you build your training around that image, the loop closes: the schema distorts the memories, the memories confirm the schema.

## Why Chess Is What Exposes All This

One question deserves asking: why devote an entire series on this subject to a game?

Because chess is probably **the best detector of these mechanisms that exists**, for a reason that comes down to the nature of the result it produces.

A chess loss has five properties that rarely come together. It's **unambiguous**: the king is mated or he isn't. It's **attributable to nobody else**: no teammate, no referee. It contains **no element of luck**: complete information on both sides. It's **public and numerical**. And it's **frequent**.

Put another way, this game produces a continuous stream of evidence capable of contradicting the image you hold of yourself, under conditions that close off almost every ordinary escape route.

That's what makes it a hard activity. It's also what makes it an instrument. There are very few places in a life where you receive information about yourself this clean, this often.

And that's where the real reason to play sits, the one this series ended up establishing without setting out to: chess doesn't make you smarter, as our article on [whether chess makes you more intelligent](/en/blog/les-echecs-rendent-ils-plus-intelligent/) shows. It does something else, something rarer. It makes visible the way you handle what contradicts you.

## What Showed Up On Its Own

Here's the part I wasn't expecting when I started this series.

Every article was written independently, with its own sources and its own ground. And at the end of nearly every one, the same practical recommendation appeared, phrased differently each time.

**Write it down first.**

Your evaluation of the position before you switch on the engine, against [anchoring](/en/blog/biais-d-ancrage-aux-echecs/) and against [obedience to authority](/en/blog/obeissance-a-l-autorite-aux-echecs/).

The moves you actually considered, before the analysis, against [false memories](/en/blog/faux-souvenirs-aux-echecs/).

Your first explanation for the loss before you examine the facts, against rationalisation.

Your abandonment criterion before you invest in a repertoire, against effort justification and the [paradox of choice](/en/blog/paradoxe-du-choix-aux-echecs/).

Your stopping rule before you start playing, against the variable-ratio reinforcement of [operant conditioning](/en/blog/conditionnement-operant-aux-echecs/).

What you believe your weakness to be, before you look at your statistics, against the [self-fulfilling prophecy](/en/blog/prophetie-auto-realisatrice-aux-echecs/) and against the [Barnum effect](/en/blog/effet-barnum-aux-echecs/).

The rating you expect before you pay for a course, against the seven levers of [persuasion](/en/blog/psychologie-de-la-persuasion-aux-echecs/).

Seven different mechanisms, seven different literatures, one countermeasure.

### Why it works

The reason is structural, and it's the most useful thing this article has to offer.

**All these mechanisms operate after the fact.**

Rationalisation produces the explanation after the loss. Reconstruction normalises the memory after the event. Anchoring shifts your estimate after the number appears. Memory adjusts what you thought you'd been thinking, after the analysis.

They all rewrite **the same thing**: what you thought beforehand.

And none of them can alter a written note. A dated text sits outside the system. It doesn't normalise, it doesn't justify itself, it doesn't remember things differently.

That's why one sentence written beforehand is worth more than an hour of sincere introspection afterward. It isn't about being more honest. It's about **producing a trace the mechanism can't reach**.

And the cost is trivial: ten seconds, one line, up front. Set against everything this series has described, that's the best deal on the table.

## When Being Right Is the Correct Strategy

This series has made a habit of laying out the case against whatever it's arguing. It would be dishonest to skip that at the point of wrapping up.

The title of this article implies you should always prefer learning. That's false, and there are at least three situations where defending your model is the correct call.

**When the signal is noise.** A loss isn't information about your level, it's a sample of size one. Revising your self-image after every defeat isn't clear-sightedness, it's overfitting. Normal rating fluctuation comfortably exceeds the effect of a month of serious work, as our article on [social comparison in chess](/en/blog/comparaison-sociale-aux-echecs/) points out. A player who questions everything every week builds nothing.

So the criterion isn't "always revise," it's **revise on aggregates**. Thirty games say something. One says nothing.

**When the revision comes at the wrong moment.** During a tournament, between rounds, "am I really the player I think I am" is the worst possible question. It consumes exactly the resource the next round needs. Deliberate suppression, meaning conscious and dated postponement, ranks among the most adaptive defences in our article on [defence mechanisms in chess](/en/blog/mecanismes-de-defense-aux-echecs/). Deferring isn't fleeing, provided you come back.

**When the cost exceeds the benefit.** Not every revision is worth it. Discovering a gap in a type of endgame you meet once a year is accurate information with no practical value. Clear-sightedness isn't an end in itself: it has a price, and that price should be paid where it pays back.

Which gives us the correct statement of this article's thesis, narrower than its title.

The problem isn't that your brain prefers being right. It's that it prefers it **indiscriminately**, including in the cases where revision would pay, and that it has no mechanism for telling the cases apart.

That's precisely what an earlier written trace lets you do. It doesn't force you to revise: it gives you the means to know whether you should have.

## A Note on the Sources

Something has to be said about what emerged, article after article, while checking the studies cited.

A striking share of the classics presented in this series **has not survived**.

The viral Dunning-Kruger curve appears in none of its authors' publications. The dissonance paradigm failed a 2024 replication run across 39 laboratories. Kitty Genovese's 38 witnesses are a journalistic invention. The paradox of choice has a mean effect of essentially zero across 63 conditions. The Pygmalion effect sits at 0.11. The marshmallow test loses two thirds of its effect once family background is accounted for. Seligman published in 2016 that his own 1967 theory had the mechanism backwards. And social psychology's founding experiment, Triplett's in 1898, wasn't statistically significant.

That isn't a reason to throw out psychology. It's a reason to read it the way you read everything else.

What held up best across the whole series shows an interesting regularity: **preregistered** protocols, measures **independent of the subject's judgement**, and studies where the verification criterion is **external** to the experimenter.

Put another way, research solved its problem the same way the one proposed here does: by writing down what it expected **before** looking.

That isn't a coincidence. It's the same problem at two scales.

## So Why Prefer Learning?

That leaves the question that gives this article its title, and it deserves an answer that isn't a moral one.

Being right is pleasant, free and immediate. Learning is unpleasant, expensive and deferred. On those criteria, being right wins every time.

So the argument for learning isn't that it's more virtuous. It's that **its effects accumulate and the effects of being right don't**.

Every loss explained in three minutes is a loss that cost you nothing and returned you nothing. Net zero. Every loss actually examined costs an unpleasant afternoon and leaves behind something that will still be there in five years.

Over one game, the first option is clearly better. Over a thousand games, it produces a player who has played a great deal and hasn't moved, which every club knows.

That's also what makes this game interesting. It doesn't reward sincerity, it doesn't reward effort, it doesn't reward good intentions. It rewards exactly one thing: having corrected something. And it keeps the books.

## Key Takeaways

Your brain doesn't prefer being right out of pride. It prefers it because keeping a coherent self-image is fast and free, while revising it is slow and painful. The system isn't malfunctioning: it optimises for stability rather than accuracy, and that stability is what lets you play again tomorrow.

Six mechanisms handle the job, in a regular order. A blind spot that stops you seeing what you lack. A directed search during calculation. A projection that fills the gaps with your own content. Discomfort at the moment of the result. An explanation in under three minutes. And a slow normalisation of the memory toward what you already believe.

They all share one thing: they act **afterward**, and they all rewrite what you thought beforehand.

Hence the one countermeasure that showed up in nearly every article of this series, without being sought. Write it down first. Your evaluation, your calculation, your explanation, your criterion, your rule. One line, ten seconds.

A dated text is the only thing in this whole business your brain can't edit.

**After reading:** open a file, today, and write one sentence: what you believe your main weakness at chess to be. Date it. Reopen it in a year. That's the entire protocol of this series, in one line.
