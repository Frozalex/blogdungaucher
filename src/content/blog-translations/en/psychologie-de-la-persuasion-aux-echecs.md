"From 1,200 to 2,000 in six months."

The ad has everything it needs. The coach is an international master, and his photo shows him at a tournament table. Three thousand students trained. Testimonials with first names and rating gains. A discount valid until Sunday. And the first lesson is free: you can watch it right now.

Nothing in that ad is necessarily a lie. The course may well be excellent. What's certain, though, is that **every single element you just read corresponds to a documented lever**, and their appearing together is never an accident.

This article maps out the seven levers and shows why the chess market is so exposed to them. Three of them, reciprocity, social proof, and scarcity, will each get their own article later in this series.

## Cialdini's Investigation

The field starts with a personal admission. [Robert Cialdini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Cialdini), professor of social psychology at Arizona State University, described himself as an easy mark. He bought subscriptions he didn't want, gave money to charities he'd never heard of, walked out with options he hadn't planned on.

His method was to go and look. For roughly three years, he trained in the persuasion trades from the inside: car sales, telemarketing, fundraising, advertising. Not to observe students in a lab, but to identify what professionals had discovered empirically because it worked.

The result appeared in 1984 under the title *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*. Six principles. A seventh would be added in 2016.

The guiding idea is interesting and often misread. These levers aren't bugs in the brain. They're **shortcuts that normally work**. Trusting an expert is an excellent strategy most of the time; returning a favor is the foundation of cooperation. They only turn into vulnerabilities when someone triggers them artificially.

## The Seven Levers, Seen From a Chessboard

### 1. Reciprocity

**The principle:** receiving something creates a debt, and that debt is felt even when you never asked for anything.

The landmark demonstration dates from 1971. Dennis Regan put participants in a fake study on evaluating paintings, alongside a confederate. During a break, the confederate steps out and comes back with two bottles of Coke, one of them for the participant. Nobody asked for anything. Later, the confederate asks the participant to buy raffle tickets from him.

Those who'd received the drink bought about twice as many. And the result has held up well in later replications, which is not true of everything in this field.

**In chess:** the free lesson, the first week on the house, the repertoire PDF in exchange for an email address, the trial session. None of these gestures is dishonest. But none of them is neutral either: when the moment to decide arrives, you're not starting from zero, you're starting from a debt.

### 2. Commitment and Consistency

**The principle:** once you've committed to something, especially in public, you feel strong pressure to stay consistent with that commitment.

This is the foot-in-the-door mechanism: get a small agreement first, which makes the big agreement far more likely afterward. It's only fair to note that this sub-principle is one of the most contested in replication terms.

**In chess:** it works mostly on you, and that's where it costs the most. Announcing your rating goal publicly. Signing up for a year-long program. Buying a complete repertoire. Each of those acts creates a commitment that becomes psychologically expensive to disown, regardless of its actual value.

That's exactly the structure of effort justification described in our article on [cognitive dissonance in chess](/en/blog/dissonance-cognitive-aux-echecs/): the more you've invested, the less you can afford to conclude it was a mistake.

### 3. Social Proof

**The principle:** in uncertain situations, we work out the correct behavior by watching what everyone else does.

**In chess:** "3 million players use this method." Course rankings by popularity. Traffic statistics on display. And, more subtly, the popularity of openings themselves: a variation looks better because people play it, when its actual scoring rate is three clicks away.

*A dedicated article on this lever comes later in the series.*

### 4. Authority

**The principle:** we follow instructions from figures perceived as legitimate, often without re-examining the content of the instruction.

**In chess:** this lever is probably more powerful here than in any other field, because authority comes with **a number attached**. A title and a rating are objective, public markers, and that objectivity gives them enormous weight.

Which produces a very specific, very costly slippage. The title certifies a **level of play**. It certifies nothing about **teaching ability**, which is an entirely different thing. A grandmaster can be a superb player and a poor teacher, partly because he's forgotten how you think at 1,400 Elo, and partly because he no longer sees the difficulties that have become invisible to him. It's the blind-spot mechanism described in our article on [the Dunning-Kruger effect in chess](/en/blog/effet-dunning-kruger-aux-echecs/), turned upside down.

So the useful question isn't "what's his rating," it's **"what progress did his students make starting from my level."** That's rarely on display.

*Obedience to authority is the subject of the next article in this series.*

### 5. Liking

**The principle:** we're more willing to be influenced by someone we like. And liking depends on factors largely unrelated to competence: similarity, familiarity, compliments, physical attractiveness.

**In chess:** the streaming era has turned this lever into a business model. You watch someone play for hundreds of hours, you know their reactions, their expressions, the way they comment. They feel familiar to you. That familiarity is real and completely one-directional.

When they recommend a product, your decision is shaped by hundreds of hours of perceived relationship, and not at all by an evaluation of the product. Our article on [chess and streaming](/en/blog/echecs-et-streaming/) covers that economy.

### 6. Scarcity

**The principle:** the perceived value of something rises as its availability falls. We react more strongly to the prospect of losing a possibility than to gaining an equivalent advantage.

That last point ties this lever directly to loss aversion, which gets its own article in this series.

**In chess:** limited spots in a training camp, offer valid until Sunday, discount on an annual subscription, closed group of ten students. The scarcity is almost always **manufactured**: a video course has no inventory, and a countdown timer can be reset.

*A dedicated article on this lever comes later in the series.*

### 7. Unity

**The principle:** the newest one, added in 2016 in *Pre-Suasion*. It's no longer about liking someone, it's about **sharing an identity** with them. Cialdini puts the distinction this way: it's not "he's like me," it's "he's one of us."

**In chess:** this may be the most effective lever in the whole field, because the chess community has a strong identity. The vocabulary, the shared references, the awareness of being a minority practicing something the people around you don't get.

"Between chess players," "we all know what that's like," "anyone who's done Tuesday nights at the club": these phrases don't describe a product, they install a sense of belonging. And a member of the group gets believed more readily than a salesperson.

## Why Chess Is Such Fertile Ground

There's a structural reason, and it's worth spelling out.

The chess training market sells an **experience good with maximum asymmetry**. Which is to say: you can't judge the quality of what you're buying before consuming it, and often not much better afterward.

That's the heart of the problem. A 1,200-rated player, by construction, **has no way to evaluate content designed to take them to 1,800**. If they had the knowledge needed to judge that content, they wouldn't need it. That's the structure of all learning, but it's unusually stark here because the skill gap is measurable.

What does a buyer do when they can't evaluate the product? They evaluate **signals**. The coach's title, the student count, the testimonials, the production quality of the video, the urgency of the offer.

And those signals are precisely the seven levers. They aren't marketing garnish laid on top of the product: in a market with strong asymmetry, **they're the only information available**.

## The Exception That Should Change Everything

And yet chess has something almost no other self-improvement market has.

Compare. A productivity coach promises to make you more efficient: you'll never know whether it worked, because there's no measure of efficiency. A coach for confidence, leadership, stress management: same story. The assessment rests on your impression, and your impression is shaped by the price you paid, the effort you put in, and how badly you want it to have worked.

In chess, there's **a number**. Objective, calculated by a third party, independent of both the seller and you, updated continuously, and predictive.

So you can do what's impossible everywhere else: write down your rating on the day you buy, define in advance what would count as success, and check six months later.

That's a remarkable amount of consumer power. It's barely used, for a reason this series has already run into: when the result disappoints, it's cheaper to conclude that you didn't work hard enough than to conclude that the product was worthless. The rating doesn't lie, but you can decide not to look at it, which amounts to the same thing.

## Teardown: The Opening Ad, Line by Line

Let's go back to the ad that opens this article. It runs five lines, and it contains six of the seven levers. That's no coincidence: it's the standard format, and once you've seen it, you can't unsee it.

> "**From 1,200 to 2,000 in six months.** Taught by an international master. **Over 3,000 students trained.** *"I went from 1,350 to 1,720 in four months, thanks Alexandre!" Thomas, 34.* **First lesson free.** **40% off until Sunday midnight.** Join the community of people who stopped stagnating."

**"Taught by an international master"**: authority. The title is true and verifiable. What it doesn't tell you is whether he knows how to teach at 1,200 Elo. Notice that the relevant information, namely his method and his teaching experience, would take two more lines and isn't there.

**"Over 3,000 students trained"**: social proof. An impressive number with no useful denominator. Three thousand students trained says nothing about how many improved, or how many quit. It's a volume, presented as a result.

**"I went from 1,350 to 1,720"**: social proof and unity combined. The testimonial gives you a first name, an age, and a trajectory to identify with. It's also survivorship bias in its purest form: Thomas is visible because he succeeded.

**"First lesson free"**: reciprocity. The gift precedes the request, exactly like the bottle of Coke in Regan's experiment. Once you've consumed the lesson, you're no longer deciding from zero.

**"40% off until Sunday midnight"**: scarcity. A video course has no inventory. The deadline is a commercial decision, not a constraint. It's also the only element you can test for free: wait until Monday.

**"Join the community of people who stopped stagnating"**: unity, and it's the most carefully written of the six lines. It doesn't describe a product. It offers membership, and it quietly splits the world into two groups, only one of which is respectable.

As for commitment and consistency, the seventh lever missing from the ad, it doesn't need to be there: it'll switch itself on after the purchase, when the expense needs justifying.

**What's absent, on the other hand, deserves attention.** No mention of the syllabus. No indication of the weekly workload expected. No number on the share of students who hit the target. No refund policy. Those are exactly the four pieces of information you'd need in order to decide, and their absence tells you more than everything else put together.

## What the Replication Crisis Did to This Field

Honesty requires saying that social psychology went through a severe crisis starting in 2011: many classic results failed to survive rigorous replication. Cialdini's territory wasn't spared, and the picture is mixed.

**What holds up reasonably well.** Reciprocity between two individuals, with converging replications since Regan. The door-in-the-face technique, whose preregistered replication published in 2021 recovered the original effect forty-six years later. Authority, corroborated extensively elsewhere.

**What's shakier.** The most telling example concerns social proof, and it's instructive because the result isn't a clean failure.

The famous study, published in 2008 by Goldstein, Cialdini, and Griskevicius, dealt with hotel towels. A message stating that nearly 75% of guests reused their towel produced more reuse than a standard appeal to environmental conscience.

In 2014, Gerd Bohner and Lena Schlüter published a replication in *PLoS ONE* across two German hotels, with 724 rooms and then 204. The result: both messages did increase reuse compared to no message at all, but **the descriptive-norm message was no more effective than the standard message**. The specific effect attributed to social proof didn't show up.

This isn't "social proof doesn't exist." It's more awkward than that: the effect is **context-dependent**, less robust and less general than its reputation suggests, and it becomes hard to know in advance which conditions it operates under.

**So how should you read the seven principles?** As a **useful observation grid**, derived from commercial practices that work, and not as seven quantified laws. The fact that a sales professional systematically uses a lever is information about that lever, even when the lab protocol meant to measure it fails to replicate. But the level of certainty isn't the one marketing books lead you to believe.

## Five Defenses

**Write the number down before you buy.** This is the most powerful defense in this article, and it's specific to chess. Before you pay, note today's rating, the target you expect, and the date you'll check. One sentence, in a file. That simple act neutralizes most of the seven levers on its own, because none of them survives a measurement taken after the fact.

**Separate the signal from the content.** Title, student count, testimonials, discount: those are signals. Ask yourself what you actually know about the **content**. Often the answer is: nothing. A detailed course syllabus is more informative than its author's rating.

**Apply the twenty-four-hour rule.** Manufactured scarcity can't survive waiting, and that's its one weakness. If the offer is gone the next day, you've dodged a seller who needed you not to think. In the overwhelming majority of cases, it'll still be there.

**Look for the denominator.** Three enthusiastic testimonials tell you nothing until you know how many students went through the program. That's survivorship bias applied to the training market, described in our article on [survivorship bias in chess](/en/blog/biais-du-survivant-aux-echecs/).

**Be suspicious of comfort.** The most effective lever isn't the strongest argument, it's the feeling of being understood by one of your own. When an offer makes you feel especially at ease, that's the moment to go look at the content.

## Key Takeaways

Cialdini spent three years learning the persuasion trades from the inside, and came back with six levers, later seven. They work because they exploit shortcuts that normally serve us well: trusting an expert, returning a favor, following the group. They only become traps when triggered artificially.

The chess market is more exposed than most, for a structural reason: it sells a skill the buyer has no means of evaluating, which forces them to judge on signals. Those signals are precisely the levers.

But this market also has a unique protection, and that's the thing to take away. Chess is just about the only area of self-improvement where the promise is **measurable by a third party**. Your rating six months from now will answer the question, whether the seller likes it or not, and whether you like it or not.

All you have to do is write the number down before you pay.

**After reading:** if you've bought a course or a subscription in the last six months, go look up your rating on the date of purchase and compare it with today's. You already have the answer, you were just missing the part where you look at it.
