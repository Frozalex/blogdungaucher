The game is over. You stayed seated, yet feel drained. Your heart may have sped up, your shoulders tensed, and you spent time holding variations in mind.

That feeling deserves to be taken seriously. It does not, however, tell you how many calories you burned. This is precisely where spectacular stories about chess often take a shortcut.

## What researchers measure

Troubat, Fargeas-Gluck, Tulppo and Dugué (2009) follow 20 men playing against a computer program. They record measures including heart rate and respiratory gas exchange, used to estimate energy expenditure through indirect calorimetry.

Heart rate increases during play. Reported mean total energy expenditure is 138 kcal over the whole game, with values between 102 and 198 kcal. This total includes basal metabolism; these are not extra calories attributable solely to calculation. The authors find no significant variation in energy expenditure between the periods compared.

This protocol provides a measurement in a particular setting. It describes neither every player nor an entire day of a world championship. It therefore cannot be directly extrapolated to a week-long tournament.

## Heart rate and calories: two different kinds of information

A heart-rate monitor primarily measures the rhythm of the heart. A calorie estimate calculated from that signal also depends on the model used. It should not automatically be treated as equivalent to a gas-exchange measurement.

You can therefore observe an increase in heart rate without being able to infer energy expenditure comparable to running. The presence of stress is not enough to validate any calorie figure.

## The brain already uses energy at rest

Raichle and Gusnard (2002) note that the brain uses a substantial share of the body’s energy even at rest. Local changes associated with a task occur within that background activity. A feeling of intense effort does not imply an equivalent surge in its overall consumption.

The mistake would be to reason: I am exhausted after the game, so my brain must have used thousands of extra calories. Demonstrating such expenditure would require an appropriate measurement method and a clear statement of duration, conditions and the comparison baseline.

## The 6,000-calorie figure

It is not established by the Troubat study. An unverified estimate cannot be rescued by subsequently explaining it through heart rate, cortisol or sleep. Those mechanisms do not replace measurement.

Likewise, a reported weight change during a competition does not isolate its cause: it does not, by itself, provide an energy balance or a measure of mental effort. Chess should not be presented as a weight-loss method.

## Fatigue remains a real experience

Removing spectacular numbers does not make your experience imaginary. You may struggle to stay attentive, feel tense or want to leave the board. Note when it happens and what accompanies it: time remaining, a difficult position, a lack of breaks, discomfort.

This personal record does not measure a hormone. It can, however, help you organise the playing conditions within your control.

## Preparing your body without an Elo promise

For your next tournament, consider the setup too: a suitable chair, equipment ready and a schedule that leaves room for recovery. Between games, you can change position and walk a little if that suits you.

You do not need to copy a champion’s fitness programme. The value of activity away from the board does not depend on a number of Elo points gained. Chess can play an important part in your life without replacing other forms of movement.
