Sometimes you recognise a position before you can explain what it contains. A pawn structure or piece arrangement reminds you of a plan. This familiarity is a useful starting point for understanding memory and chess expertise.

## Specialised memory

Chase and Simon (1973) study how players of different strengths perceive and reconstruct positions. Their work highlights organising information into meaningful groupings, or “chunks”.

The finding should not become a law that every grandmaster replaces 28 pieces and every beginner four, or that experts lose precisely all advantage on random positions. Protocol, participants and position structure matter.

The study concerns domain expertise. It does not predict how many words Karpov would recall from a list, or attribute general photographic memory to champions.

## Working memory and long-term knowledge

Holding a variation in mind requires retaining and updating information. Existing knowledge can help organise the task: you do not need to rediscover every piece relationship as though seeing the game for the first time.

The old account “seven units for everyone, so four pieces per unit makes 28 pieces” turned an illustration into a universal measurement. Capacities and strategies depend on the task; that multiplication cannot calculate every player’s recall.

Gobet and Simon (1996) develop the template model to explain recall of several boards. These structures combine stable elements and variable slots. They are memory models, not a directly measured inventory of thousands of independent positions in every brain.

## Recognition does not remove the need to calculate

Seeing a familiar pattern may suggest a candidate. You still need to check differences: an extra piece, a king on another square or castling rights may change the conclusion.

To work on the transition from recognition to checking, revisit a known position and change a legal condition. Ask whether the same idea still works. This is an exercise suggestion; the cited sources do not measure its return for all players.

During an official game, follow notation rules: taking analysis notes or drawing variations to ease memory demands is not allowed. Keep those tools for training and post-game analysis.

## Consolidating an idea rather than collecting moves

You can learn both an exact opening sequence and its accompanying plan. They are not mutually exclusive. What matters is knowing what you will do if the opponent deviates.

After a session, try recalling a position or explaining a decision without looking. Then check. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) show a benefit of retrieval for delayed text retention in their experiments; they do not test an optimal chess dose or every type of memory.

Adapt reviews to what you forget. Thirty to sixty minutes or three to five sessions per week may be an organisational choice, not a scientific prescription guaranteeing an effect.

## Broader transfer still needs demonstration

Sala and Gobet’s review (2017) urges caution about far transfer to school or general cognitive skills. Using a function during play does not prove it improves in every other activity.

Likewise, puzzle performance is not medical screening. For ageing and associations between leisure and dementia, see [the Alzheimer’s article](/en/blog/chess-and-alzheimer-prevention/).

You can follow a precise improvement: recalling a pattern more reliably, forgetting fewer defences or explaining an endgame better. These goals are already useful without promising superior memory across everyday life.
