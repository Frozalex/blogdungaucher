Sometimes you recognise a position before you can explain what it contains. A pawn structure or piece arrangement reminds you of a plan. This familiarity is a useful starting point for understanding memory and chess expertise.

## Specialised memory

Chase and Simon (1973) study how players of different strengths perceive and reconstruct positions. Their work highlights organising information into meaningful groupings, or “chunks”.

The finding should not become a law that every grandmaster replaces 28 pieces and every beginner four, or that experts lose precisely all advantage on random positions. Protocol, participants and position structure matter.

The study concerns domain expertise. It does not predict how many words Karpov would recall from a list, or attribute general photographic memory to champions.

## Working memory and long-term knowledge

Holding a variation in mind requires retaining and updating information. Existing knowledge can help organise the task: you do not need to rediscover every piece relationship as though seeing the game for the first time.

The old account “seven units for everyone, so four pieces per unit makes 28 pieces” turned an illustration into a universal measurement. Capacities and strategies depend on the task; that multiplication cannot calculate every player’s recall.

Gobet and Simon (1996) develop the template model to explain recall of several boards. These structures combine stable elements and variable slots. They are memory models, not a directly measured inventory of thousands of independent positions in every brain.

## Recognition does not remove the need to calculate

Seeing a familiar pattern may suggest a candidate. You still need to check differences: an extra piece, a king on another square or castling rights may change the conclusion.

To work on the transition from recognition to checking, revisit a known position and change a legal condition. Ask whether the same idea still works. This is an exercise suggestion; the cited sources do not measure its return for all players.

During an official game, follow notation rules: taking analysis notes or drawing variations to ease memory demands is not allowed. Keep those tools for training and post-game analysis.

## Consolidating an idea rather than collecting moves

You can learn both an exact opening sequence and its accompanying plan. They are not mutually exclusive. What matters is knowing what you will do if the opponent deviates.

After a session, try recalling a position or explaining a decision without looking. Then check. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) show a benefit of retrieval for delayed text retention in their experiments; they do not test an optimal chess dose or every type of memory.

Adapt reviews to what you forget. Thirty to sixty minutes or three to five sessions per week may be an organisational choice, not a scientific prescription guaranteeing an effect.

## Broader transfer still needs demonstration

Sala and Gobet’s review (2017) urges caution about far transfer to school or general cognitive skills. Using a function during play does not prove it improves in every other activity.

Likewise, puzzle performance is not medical screening. For ageing and associations between leisure and dementia, see [the Alzheimer’s article](/en/blog/chess-and-alzheimer-prevention/).

You can follow a precise improvement: recalling a pattern more reliably, forgetting fewer defences or explaining an endgame better. These goals are already useful without promising superior memory across everyday life.

## Three diagrams, one detail that changes the answer

Start on a completely empty analysis board and place a white rook on a1, a white king on g1 and a black king on g8. Put a black rook on a8 and a white pawn on a4. It is White to move. Before testing chess moves, give yourself a memory task: look at the board for a moment, conceal it and write down the five occupied squares. Then answer a relational question without looking back: can White's rook capture Black's rook immediately? The pawn on a4 blocks the a-file. You might remember that both rooks stood in corners yet overlook the blocker. The list of squares and the answer about the capture test two distinguishable things.

On the second board move the same white pawn from a4 to b4. Leave the other pieces exactly where they were. White now has a clear line from a1 to a8 and can capture the rook with check along the eighth rank. Look away and tell a friend what changed. The number of men, the two king positions and both rook positions are identical to those on the first board. You need to update both the pawn's location and the effect of the now open file. A player who recalls the second board as “two opposing rooks on the a-file” has retained a useful summary but not the whole reason the capture works.

For the third board keep the pawn on b4 but put the black rook on b8 and a black bishop on a8. The white rook on a1 can take the bishop, but the rook on b8 now blocks the check along the rank, and Black can answer ...Rxa8. You have preserved the picture of a long open file and a capture on the eighth rank while changing the identity of the target and adding a defender. If somebody automatically recommends the same capture because it worked on the preceding diagram, ask what happens on the next move. A motif is a prompt to investigate; it is not a licence to apply the old verdict to a different position.

These are invented teaching positions. Their purpose is not to compare scores between people. Change the order when using them a second time, and ask for a legal line as well as a list of pieces. If a player puts a pawn back on a4 in the last diagram, the preceding board has intruded on their recall. If they place every piece correctly but miss the reply ...Rxa8, their immediate problem is checking a defensive resource. Copying the diagrams faster would not correct that second error.

## What kind of memory does a game collection need?

Imagine saving an entire game in a notebook. On the first page write its move sequence. On the second page show a single diagram taken just before a forced exchange and ask why the exchange favours one side. On the third page omit the players' names and present a new position with the same attacking pattern but a different defender. Reading those pages in order feels coherent. Testing them in reverse asks three separate questions: can you apply the pattern, recognise the original decision and finally reproduce the historical sequence? A reader can do well on one without succeeding on all three.

Choose the version you want before grading yourself. For an opening rehearsal the move order may matter because changing it gives the opponent a useful move in between. For an endgame lesson you might care more about reaching a particular arrangement of kings and pawns, even if two safe move orders reach it. For a club talk you may want to tell the story of a game and name the point where the plan changed. There is no universal percentage of pieces or moves recalled that summarises all these goals. Recording the type of error makes it possible to select an exercise that addresses the actual gap.

Return to a card a few days later before turning it over. Say what you expect the answer to be and why. When you check, correct both the destination and the relationship: “the rook is on b8, which lets it recapture on a8.” Then make a fresh card where that relationship matters but the pieces have been rearranged. A correct answer on the original card shows that some trace of the lesson has survived; a correct answer on the new card gives additional evidence about what you can recognise outside the memorised picture.

## Keep your study notes and your competition notes separate

At home you can draw arrows, circle an empty square, write an alternative line and physically return pieces to the starting diagram. Those are useful tools for finding what went wrong while studying. A tournament is a different setting with rules on notes and outside information. Do your detailed reconstruction after the game, with the moves you actually played clearly separated from the lines you remember considering. The question “what was on the board?” and the question “what crossed my mind?” need different evidence.

A useful final page in the notebook has just two diagrams. On the left put the original pattern, on the right the near miss where the same solution fails. Do not write the difference in the title. Ask a partner to point out the changed defender before choosing the move, then swap roles with a new pair from their games. The discussion will reveal whether the memorable part was merely the appearance of an open file or the concrete conditions that made a capture safe. That distinction is one of the rewards of building your own collection rather than amassing hundreds of positions you have only ever seen once.

Leave both cards undated on the front and put a date on the back. A partner can then shuffle them without announcing which position you have already revised.
