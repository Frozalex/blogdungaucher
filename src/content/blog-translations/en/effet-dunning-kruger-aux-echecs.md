You know your rating but may feel it does not quite reflect your strength. That impression can have concrete reasons. It also deserves to be checked against something other than your best games.

A 2025 study lets us discuss overconfidence in chess using data. It does not turn every player who feels underrated into an example of a psychological flaw.

## The 3,388 players are real

Heck, Benjamin, Simons and Chabris publish two preregistered studies in *Psychological Science*. Their combined sample comprises 3,388 rated players from 22 countries, aged 5 to 88, with an average of 18.8 years of tournament experience.

Participants assign themselves a playing level averaging 89 Elo points above their observed rating. Overestimation is greater among lower-rated players; higher-rated players are better calibrated in this sample.

One year later, 11.3% of players classified as overconfident had reached their claimed rating. This result describes that group and follow-up. It does not mean that you, today, have exactly an 88.7% chance of being wrong.

## An average is not an individual portrait

The average 89-point gap does not say everyone overestimates themselves by that amount. A player may be improving, have few recent games or vary across time controls. The study remains informative without making ratings a perfect, permanent measure of every skill.

Likewise, an expected score calculated from an Elo difference is not a guaranteed number of wins. Draws contribute to the score, and short series vary considerably.

The study’s title refers to persistent overconfidence. That does not, by itself, demonstrate that every proposed mechanism explaining the Dunning-Kruger effect is confirmed.

## Why a graph’s form matters

Nuhfer and colleagues (2016) examine the influence of noise and graphical conventions on self-assessment. Their simulations show, among other things, that certain representations encourage misleading interpretations.

Relating measured ability to a difference containing that same measurement can create mathematical dependence. A slope on a graph is therefore not enough to identify a psychological inability to recognise one’s level.

This work does not make all overconfidence disappear. It reminds us that assessment errors and their explanation are separate questions. The publication used here is from 2016; no second 2017 publication is needed for the reasoning retained.

## Giving yourself more useful feedback

If you think you are underrated, write what you expect from your next games and under which conditions. Then compare a sufficiently documented series with that expectation. Keep losses, draws and wins without changing criteria afterwards.

You can also ask someone to comment on a game and explain their objections. A concrete variation teaches more than a judgement such as “you lack humility”.

[Game analysis](/en/blog/how-to-analyze-chess-games/) and [rationalisation](/en/blog/rationalization-in-chess/) extend this work. If your difficulty is mainly not feeling legitimate despite your results, read [the article on impostor feelings](/en/blog/impostor-syndrome-in-chess/) instead. Both questions can coexist; they do not allow a diagnosis from afar.

## Four predictions and a graph that draws itself

Here is a small example you can reproduce with pencil and paper. Four members of a club sit the same test of four chess positions. Before opening the booklet, each predicts a score of two correct solutions. The actual results are one, two, two and three. Sort the players by their actual results. At the bottom of the list is a player who overestimated the score by one. At the top is a player who underestimated the score by one. The two people in the middle were exactly right. It would be easy to label the two ends of the graph as different kinds of personality. Yet all four made precisely the same prediction.

This invented example cannot refute or establish any published effect. It illustrates a feature of the calculation: sorting people by one variable that is also used to calculate their error. Because the forecasts in our example are all two, the smallest observed score must sit below the forecast and the largest score above it. To explain why the players all said two, you would need other information. Did they know the difficulty of the positions? Had they already tried a similar booklet? Did a coach tell them that a typical participant usually solves half?

Now ask for a range instead of a single prediction. Each player expects to solve between one and three problems. Everyone's observed score falls inside their range. Does that mean everyone knew their level with exceptional accuracy? The range includes three of the five scores that are possible on the test. It is informative enough to rule out both extremes but tells a coach relatively little about the difficulty to choose for individual players. A forecast can be more or less accurate and also more or less precise. Writing down only whether the final result fell within the range conceals the second question.

## The comparison group can move under your feet

Two members both describe themselves as belonging in the top half. One is thinking of the adults who attended the Thursday training session. The other is thinking of the entire membership, including the children who first learnt to move the pieces last week. Their statements use identical language and refer to different lists of people. A ranking of sixth among eighteen present that evening serves a different purpose from a ranking of sixth among eighty names on the membership register. You cannot calculate either player's error until you know the reference group they meant.

This matters when a player compares an online blitz rating with an expected performance in a local classical tournament. Both interfaces display a number beside the player's name. The time controls, opponents, rating systems and periods of observation may all be different. A numerical disagreement between the two need not arise from an inability to judge skill. Specify the question before comparing the answers. How many points does the player expect against these particular four opponents at this particular time control? Once the games are complete you have one comparison, albeit an extremely small one.

A range can lead to a more revealing discussion. Suppose the player expects between one and three points. What information would move the prediction towards the middle? They might have played two of the opponents in the same opening before and feel uncertain about an ending that often arises. Or they may expect to play tired after a late journey for the last round. One uncertainty concerns a skill, another the circumstances of play. They do not suggest the same preparation, although either could lead to an identical prediction.

## Practising calibration on the board

Some of the most useful chess forecasts have nothing to do with rating points. Go back to a position where you judged that a pawn was poisoned. Hide the continuation and write a statement you can check. For example, you may think that after capturing with the bishop, the opponent has a check which wins your queen. Start with that one claim. Is the checking move legal? Does the king have a square which still guards the queen? Is there a capture of the checking piece? Decide how confident you are before you start moving pieces. Once you know the answer, changing the original confidence label will deprive you of the comparison.

Write down the cases where you were right as well. A notebook containing only the eight things you got wrong is an excellent collection of exercises. It cannot show how frequently these judgements are wrong in the positions you normally encounter. To ask that question in a limited setting, agree to record the next ten diagrams your partner chooses before you see any of them. Keep the first judgements for all ten, including the obvious ones. At the end, compare the positions where you felt certain with the ones you found uncomfortable. If one group contains a single example, wait for more material before making anything of a percentage.

A pattern you find might be quite local. Perhaps you recognise the endings you can defend reliably but are optimistic about tactics when both kings are exposed. In that case, spend a training session looking for counterchecks in double-edged positions. Practising a specific search is more actionable than issuing yourself a general instruction to be more modest. Next time, record whether you actually looked for a countercheck and what difference it made.

Repeat the original test with near neighbours of the first four diagrams. Decide in advance whether finding the first move is enough or whether the best defence must be followed all the way to a stable position. A player who saw the idea but missed the final recapture needs to know which part earned credit. An improvement produced simply by a more generous rule for marking answers says little about learning.

Finally, give two anonymised answer sheets with equal scores to a training partner. Ask which skill they would practise with each author. One may need to look for a second candidate; the other may need to check the final position more carefully. If the prescriptions differ, the detail in the answers has already provided information unavailable from either the ranking or the confidence score alone. That is a good use of a small chess test even when it cannot support a broad claim about anyone's character.
