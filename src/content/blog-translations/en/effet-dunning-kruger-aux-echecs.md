You know your rating but may feel it does not quite reflect your strength. That impression can have concrete reasons. It also deserves to be checked against something other than your best games.

A 2025 study lets us discuss overconfidence in chess using data. It does not turn every player who feels underrated into an example of a psychological flaw.

## The 3,388 players are real

Heck, Benjamin, Simons and Chabris publish two preregistered studies in *Psychological Science*. Their combined sample comprises 3,388 rated players from 22 countries, aged 5 to 88, with an average of 18.8 years of tournament experience.

Participants assign themselves a playing level averaging 89 Elo points above their observed rating. Overestimation is greater among lower-rated players; higher-rated players are better calibrated in this sample.

One year later, 11.3% of players classified as overconfident had reached their claimed rating. This result describes that group and follow-up. It does not mean that you, today, have exactly an 88.7% chance of being wrong.

## An average is not an individual portrait

The average 89-point gap does not say everyone overestimates themselves by that amount. A player may be improving, have few recent games or vary across time controls. The study remains informative without making ratings a perfect, permanent measure of every skill.

Likewise, an expected score calculated from an Elo difference is not a guaranteed number of wins. Draws contribute to the score, and short series vary considerably.

The study’s title refers to persistent overconfidence. That does not, by itself, demonstrate that every proposed mechanism explaining the Dunning-Kruger effect is confirmed.

## Why a graph’s form matters

Nuhfer and colleagues (2016) examine the influence of noise and graphical conventions on self-assessment. Their simulations show, among other things, that certain representations encourage misleading interpretations.

Relating measured ability to a difference containing that same measurement can create mathematical dependence. A slope on a graph is therefore not enough to identify a psychological inability to recognise one’s level.

This work does not make all overconfidence disappear. It reminds us that assessment errors and their explanation are separate questions. The publication used here is from 2016; no second 2017 publication is needed for the reasoning retained.

![From observation to explanation](/images/en/dunning-interpreter.svg)

## Giving yourself more useful feedback

If you think you are underrated, write what you expect from your next games and under which conditions. Then compare a sufficiently documented series with that expectation. Keep losses, draws and wins without changing criteria afterwards.

You can also ask someone to comment on a game and explain their objections. A concrete variation teaches more than a judgement such as “you lack humility”.

[Game analysis](/en/blog/how-to-analyze-chess-games/) and [rationalisation](/en/blog/rationalization-in-chess/) extend this work. If your difficulty is mainly not feeling legitimate despite your results, read [the article on impostor feelings](/en/blog/impostor-syndrome-in-chess/) instead. Both questions can coexist; they do not allow a diagnosis from afar.
