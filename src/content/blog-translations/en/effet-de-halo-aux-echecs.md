Complicated position, middlegame. Your opponent plays a move you weren't expecting. A strange move: he puts his rook on a square that seems to have no future.

If you see 2,480 written next to his name, you'll spend six minutes working out his plan. There must be something there. A three-stage manoeuvre, a weak square you haven't spotted.

If you see 1,380, you'll spend forty seconds looking for how to punish it.

It's the same rook, on the same square, in the same position. What changed is a number displayed next to a name.

## Thorndike, 1920

The phenomenon was identified over a century ago, and its first description is already remarkably sharp.

[Edward Thorndike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halo_effect), an American psychologist, published a short paper in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* in 1920 under a modest title: "A Constant Error in Psychological Ratings." In it he examined the evaluations of 137 aviation cadets by their commanding officers.

The officers had to rate each man on distinct and theoretically independent qualities: intelligence, physical aptitude, leadership, character.

The result was that these scores were **strongly correlated with each other**. Far too strongly for the qualities themselves to be. A cadet judged physically good was almost systematically judged intelligent and a good leader.

Thorndike's conclusion was that the officers were **incapable of separating the dimensions**. They formed an overall impression first, favourable or unfavourable, then deduced each specific score from it. The word *halo* describes exactly that: a global light that colours everything it illuminates.

## Nisbett and Wilson, or the Real Problem

Thorndike's result is already awkward. The 1977 one is far more so.

Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson designed a very clean protocol. They filmed **the same teacher**, a university lecturer speaking English with a European accent, in two different interviews. In the first he's warm and open. In the second, cold and distant. Same man, same accent, same physical appearance, same gestures.

118 students watched one version or the other, then evaluated the teacher on attributes that were **physical and objectively identical in both videos**: his appearance, his mannerisms, his accent.

Result: those who saw the warm version judged his appearance pleasant, his mannerisms agreeable and his accent charming. Those who saw the cold version judged exactly the same attributes irritating.

So much for the effect. But that isn't the important part.

### The Participants Deny It, and Get the Direction Wrong

Nisbett and Wilson then questioned the students about what had determined their judgments.

**They denied any influence of their global impression.** They claimed to have evaluated the accent and the mannerisms independently.

And for some, stranger still: **they reversed the causality**. They explained that it was because the accent was pleasant that they found the man likeable. When the experimental manipulation concerned only the warmth of his behaviour, and the accent was strictly identical.

That's the decisive result, and it's what makes this article useful. The halo effect isn't merely an error of judgment. **It's an error of judgment accompanied by a sincere conviction of having escaped it**, doubled with an explanation manufactured afterwards to justify the judgment.

You'll recognize a mechanism this series has already met: the explanation produced after the decision and mistaken for its cause. That's the subject of an upcoming article on rationalization.

## Chess's Numerical Halo

Almost every field produces vague global impressions: he seems competent, he inspires confidence. Chess does something far more effective.

**It supplies the halo as a number, before the first move.**

Objective, public, calculated by a third party, updated, and predictive. It isn't an impression: it's exact information. Which makes its contaminating effect much harder to spot, since taking it into account is perfectly legitimate.

Here's where it operates.

### Evaluating the Move

This is the central case, described at the top. A surprising move gets two opposite treatments depending on the displayed number. From a higher-rated player, the default hypothesis is "he saw something." From a lower-rated player, the default hypothesis is "he blundered."

The cost isn't theoretical: it's paid in **allocation of thinking time**. You allocate six minutes or forty seconds according to a number, not according to the position.

### Preparation

The halo acts before the game. Facing a much higher-rated player, many players change their opening choice, take a more solid line, try to "survive." Facing a lower-rated player, they take risks they wouldn't otherwise take.

Neither of these adaptations is absurd in itself. But they rest on a global prediction rather than on the position, and they're applied before any move.

### Annotations

Open a book of annotated games and look at how the moves are marked.

A difficult move played by a world champion gets an exclamation mark and three paragraphs of explanation. A comparable move played by an unknown amateur passes without comment, when it isn't flagged as imprecise.

So the corpus of annotations you learned to judge positions from was produced by annotators who **knew who played the moves**. It's a halo built into the literature itself.

### The Reverse Halo

There's also a version that concerns you directly: the one that applies to your own position when you play someone stronger.

You're actually slightly better. But the rating gap produces an unfavourable global impression, and that impression contaminates your evaluation. You look for what's wrong with your position instead of looking for how to exploit it. Your objective judgment gets rewritten by a number.

This mechanism is close to the one described in our article on [the placebo effect in chess](/en/blog/effet-placebo-aux-echecs/), under the nocebo heading: negative expectation does bite for real.

## The Test You Can Run Tonight

This is chess's great advantage over other fields where psychology gets applied: here, the bias is **directly testable on yourself**, free, with no laboratory.

Most platforms let you hide opponents' ratings. Here's the protocol.

**1.** Note your current rating.

**2.** Hide opponent ratings and play thirty games at a long time control. Thirty is a minimum: below that, noise dominates.

**3.** At the end, look up each opponent's actual rating and calculate your score by band: against weaker, against equal, against stronger.

**4.** Compare those scores to the ones you normally achieve in the same bands with ratings visible.

If your score against higher-rated players is better blind, you've measured your own halo effect, on yourself, with data. That's information three psychology articles will never give you.

And be careful with the interpretation: the result may also be nil. That's a result too, and it's just as interesting.

## Four Practices

**Separate the moment of prediction from the moment of evaluation.** Using the rating to prepare is legitimate, and even recommended. Using it to evaluate a position isn't. The rule fits in one sentence: the rating may decide **what you play before the game**, never **what a position is worth during it**.

**Handle the move, not the author.** Faced with a surprising move, the question isn't "why did he play that" but "what does this move threaten." The first calls for psychology, the second calls for calculation. Only the second produces information about the board.

**Distrust your own explanations.** This is the specific lesson from Nisbett and Wilson. If you tell yourself "it isn't his rating influencing me, it's that the move really is strange," note that this is exactly what the participants said. The sincerity of the denial is no indication of its accuracy.

**Analyse blind.** When you work on masters' games, hide the names and the results. Evaluate the position, choose your move, then reveal. The halo attached to a famous name makes you accept moves you wouldn't have accepted otherwise, which removes the entire point of the exercise.

## What to Take Away

Thorndike showed in 1920 that professional evaluators are incapable of judging distinct qualities separately: they form an overall impression and deduce the details from it. Nisbett and Wilson added the genuinely awkward part in 1977: people deny the effect, and go as far as reversing the direction of causality to explain a judgment they didn't produce the way they think they did.

In chess this halo is particularly powerful because it's numerical. A rating is exact, public, and available before the first move. It changes the default hypothesis about every opposing move, and therefore how you allocate your calculation time.

But chess also offers something social psychology almost never has: the possibility of **measuring the bias on yourself**, by hiding ratings for thirty games and comparing the scores.

That's a little effort for a piece of data nobody else can give you.

**After reading:** hide opponent ratings on your platform and play your next thirty long games that way. Note your starting rating today, you'll need the point of comparison.
