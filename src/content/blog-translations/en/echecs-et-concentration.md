You reread the same variation and no longer know what you were looking for. Then you check the clock: several minutes have passed. The difficulty is not always calculating more. Sometimes it is finding a clear question again.

Concentration depends on the task, fatigue and distractions. The muscle metaphor can help organise training, but it does not describe a brain reservoir that empties at a precise time.

## Noticing when you lose focus

You can look for concrete signs: repeatedly revisiting the same line, forgetting the opponent’s threat, looking at ratings instead of the position. None is enough to diagnose an attention disorder. They mainly indicate that a change of method is worth trying.

There is no universal threshold of 90 to 120 minutes after which the cortex runs out of glucose or neurotransmitters. A long game can be demanding without every mistake coming from fatigue.

## Returning to a useful question

When you notice a distraction, reformulate the decision you need to make: which threat needs addressing? Which candidate moves still need comparing? What has changed since the last move? [Mind-wandering at the board](/en/blog/reseau-mode-defaut-aux-echecs/) is easy to notice in hindsight, even when its cause is hard to pinpoint.

A short question can provide a starting point for resuming. If you have already calculated a line, recall its conclusion before starting over. The aim is to avoid a loop that produces no new information.

You can test this routine in training on a few positions, then note after the session whether it helped. This review of your practice does not measure a general gain in attention, but it can show where you lose the thread.

## Taking a break without expecting a miracle

Albulescu and colleagues (2022) bring together 22 samples concerning micro-breaks. Their meta-analysis finds favourable effects on perceived vigour and fatigue. The overall performance effect is not statistically significant. It therefore does not establish that taking a break every twenty or thirty minutes prevents chess mistakes.

In a long game, you can use an appropriate moment to relax your shoulders or briefly look away. If you leave the board, follow tournament rules and keep the clock in mind. A badly timed break can also cost time.

For organising rest between sessions, see [the benefits of breaks](/en/blog/benefits-of-breaks-in-chess/). Here, the main issue is resuming the reasoning where it was interrupted.

## Building a session that lets you observe your attention

Choose an identifiable task: analysing an endgame, solving an exercise, reviewing a game. Remove notifications and tabs that do not serve it. Plan an end to the session rather than extending it through inertia.

Duration can vary. Start with a block you can complete, then adjust it based on your experience. There is no validated progression requiring twenty-five, then thirty-five, then fifty minutes to train concentration in chess.

Record an observable result: did you find candidates, check defences or understand why your solution fails? Spending a long time at the board does not answer those questions.

## Do not confuse engagement with general benefit

A deeply absorbing session does not prove that your attention is improving in every activity. Likewise, playing blitz does not, by itself, establish deterioration in deep thinking.

If your difficulties persist across several contexts, do not explain them only as a lack of training. You can adapt your practice without expecting it to replace an assessment of those difficulties.

The best routine for you makes your work clearer and remains practical. Start with one change, observe what it adds, then adjust.

## One exercise, three ways of losing the thread

Set up the starting position and play **1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Nd4**. Stop. Imagine that White is studying the apparently undefended pawn on e5. The knight on f3 can certainly capture it. The issue is what Black's last move made possible. Moving the black knight away from c6 did more than stop defending e5. With the e-pawn on e5 and the knight already out of the way on d4, Black has plans that an inventory of attacked pawns will miss. Before moving, name one active reply to the capture. Do this even if you remember the opening trap. Recognising its name does little to explain how it works.

Continue **4.Nxe5 Qg5**. The new question is what the queen attacks. It reaches the knight along the fifth rank and the pawn on g2 along the g-file. The queen also vacated d8. Imagine **5.Nxf7**. There is a familiar pattern in which a knight on f7 forks queen and rook. Name each target on the actual board. Here the knight attacks the rook on h8, but the square d8 is empty. If you pictured a double attack, you may have concentrated quite hard on a pattern that belonged to the previous diagram. The repair is to update the position, not simply to spend more time looking at the knight.

Continue **5...Qxg2 6.Rf1 Qxe4+ 7.Be2**. Remove both captured white pawns as you play. The rook that started on h1 is on f1. The bishop between the black queen and the white king is the bishop that started on f1. What is Black's strongest move? Decide before playing it. Then ask whether White's bishop could capture the piece that gives check. Answering the second question is part of solving the exercise. Several quiet-looking pieces determine which moves count as legal replies.

The answer is **7...Nf3#**. A knight's check cannot be blocked. The king on e1 cannot capture a knight on f3, and its neighbours provide no escape. The bishop on e2 appears to reach f3, but moving it opens the e-file between queen and king. The pawn that could have taken the knight from g2 has already been captured. If you found the right move because it looked like a famous smothered mate, test the explanation anyway. Reaching the right square with a wrong account of the bishop's freedom is an error worth finding at home.

There are at least three different training needs hidden in an incorrect answer. If you left the queen on d8 after it moved to g5, rehearse updating the square a piece vacates. If you kept the white g-pawn on the board, replay the captures and state which defender disappears. If you remembered every piece but thought the bishop could move, work on legality and pins. A longer puzzle session may simply ask you to repeat the wrong exercise. Pick the earliest error you can actually reproduce and design the next diagram around it.

## Staying with a variation and switching away from it

A player can lose a line halfway through and still be excellent at finding alternatives. Another can follow a line accurately for six moves and never wonder whether the first move was forced. The two difficulties call for different questions. For the first, stop after each capture and reconstruct the position. For the second, put the pieces back at the starting point and ask what else you might do. In the example above, no rule required White to chase the pawn on e5 or continue with the knight on f7. A game score can make choices look inevitable simply by listing only the moves that were played.

Try this in a quiet position from your own games. Cover the continuation and name your preferred move. Give a concrete reason for it. Then name a move with a different purpose. If your first idea improves a rook, your second might defend a weak square or offer an exchange. The point is to compare the questions facing your opponent after each move. Generating three equivalent rook moves may teach less than looking seriously at a single defensive alternative. Once you find a decisive refutation, stop analysing that branch and say where your attention belongs next.

## A record that catches the next mistake

Instead of giving a whole evening a concentration score, record one interruption and its consequence. Suppose a notification arrives while you are looking at a pawn ending. When you come back, you remember the promotion race but forget whose move it was in the starting position. At the next practice session, leave a marker beside the board showing which colour moves before answering the interruption. See whether that small aid saves you from rebuilding the line. Keep training notes like this at home. During a tournament, follow the rules governing notes, devices and assistance.

For another example, you may find yourself staring at the clock during your opponent's turn. Recall the threat you expected and ask which move would invalidate it. When your opponent actually moves, compare that prediction with the board. This gives you a job even if the other player spends a long time thinking. It also gives you permission to abandon the prediction immediately when it no longer fits. Your attention has served its purpose when you return to the position in front of you.

After a week, see whether the same kind of error recurred. If the marker fixed your problem with the side to move but you still missed a pawn breakthrough, study the breakthrough on its own terms. An unfamiliar idea can be hard to find even on a quiet afternoon. Describing what you missed precisely leaves room for a technical explanation as well as an attentional one.

For a coaching session, give each player a different stopping point in the same opening sequence. Have them describe the board to one another before revealing the moves. A disagreement about an empty square is often more instructive than a disagreement about which side is better.
