You open the app. Before you've played a single move, you've received four pieces of information: your rating, how it's changed since yesterday, where you sit among your friends, and the rating of the opponent you've been offered.

Compare that with someone who picks up a guitar, or goes for a run, or cooks. That person can spend ten years in their activity without ever knowing precisely where they stand.

Chess did something no other hobby has done. It didn't just make comparison possible: it **removed the work of comparing**, by delivering the result directly.

## Festinger, 1954

Six years before the dollar experiment that made him famous, which we covered in our article on [cognitive dissonance in chess](/en/blog/dissonance-cognitive-aux-echecs/), [Leon Festinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_comparison_theory) published a quieter and probably more universal theory in *Human Relations*.

His proposal comes down to three ideas.

**First**, humans have a fundamental need to evaluate their opinions and abilities. This isn't vanity: without that evaluation, you can't decide what to take on.

**Second**, when an objective yardstick exists, we use it. To know whether you run fast, a stopwatch is enough, and nobody needs anyone else.

**Third**, in the absence of an objective yardstick, we compare ourselves **to other people**. And not to just anyone: Festinger insists that comparison targets people **close to our own level**. Comparing yourself to a world champion supplies no usable information.

The theory was enriched later, notably by Thomas Wills, who formalised **downward comparison** in 1981: comparing yourself to someone worse off protects self-esteem, which explains why people reach for it when they're feeling bad.

So there are two directions, with two opposite functions. **Upward** comparison supplies information and a model, at an emotional cost. **Downward** comparison supplies comfort, and no information.

## The Chess Twist

Here's why chess is a limiting case for this theory.

Festinger says: in the absence of an objective yardstick, we compare. It follows logically that **the presence of an objective yardstick should remove the need for social comparison**.

And chess has the most fully developed objective yardstick of any hobby. The Elo rating isn't an opinion: it's a calculation.

So you'd expect chess players, holding an exact measurement, to stop comparing themselves to others. Obviously the opposite happens, and the reason is simple.

**The Elo rating isn't an absolute measure. It's a relative one.**

A stopwatch measures your speed regardless of who's running next to you. Elo measures nothing except your results **against other players**. Its value means nothing outside the population that produces it.

Put another way, the rating isn't an objective yardstick that would replace social comparison. **It's industrialised social comparison, calculated for you, and published continuously.**

It's a remarkable object, and as far as I know it has no equivalent in leisure activities. It turns a diffuse, occasional, approximate psychological process into a permanent and exact data point.

## What That Produces

### Comparison Becomes Impossible to Avoid

In most activities, you can choose not to compare. It takes effort, but it's possible: you just don't ask.

In chess, the information comes to you. It's displayed before the game and updated after it. You can't not know.

And that point carries more weight than it looks, because Festinger's theory describes an **activity**: you go looking for a comparison when you need one. Here the activity is removed and replaced by a notification.

### The Rating Becomes a Measure of You

This is the central drift, and it's dealt with in depth in our article on [Elo and self-esteem](/en/blog/echecs-estime-de-soi-elo/).

The slide is easy to describe. The rating measures results. Since it's the only number available and it's exact, it ends up measuring the player. Then the person.

### Noise Gets Mistaken for Signal

A more technical point, and badly underrated.

The normal fluctuations of a rating, at ordinary game volume, are far larger than the effect of a month of serious work. A thirty-point swing contains almost no information.

But the daily display invites you to interpret every swing. A player who checks his rating after every game spends most of his time interpreting noise, with the emotional consequences that come with it.

That's also what makes the rating unusable as a learning signal, for the reasons developed in our article on [learned helplessness in chess](/en/blog/impuissance-apprise-aux-echecs/): too slow, too noisy, not attributable.

### Lateral Comparison, the Most Painful Kind

Festinger was right about this, and players' experience confirms it: what hurts isn't the gap to the world champion, it's the gap to **the player at your club who started at the same time as you**.

Two thousand points of distance from Carlsen provokes nothing. Eighty points of distance from someone who started the same month provokes plenty, because that comparison is **informative**: it isolates a difference that can't be blamed on circumstances.

That's also what makes ranked friend lists so effective emotionally. They select exactly the population that maximises the effect.

## What Makes a Comparison Useful

They're not all equal, and the criterion is clean.

**A useful comparison delivers information about what to do.**

Knowing that a player at your club is 200 points above you teaches you nothing. Knowing that he spends most of his study time on endgames while you spend yours on openings is usable information.

The difference is between comparing **results** and comparing **methods**. Results are visible and useless. Methods are invisible and useful, which explains why nearly everybody compares the first kind.

That's also the limit of the downward side: reassuring yourself by looking at someone weaker really does work on your mood, and produces strictly no information.

## Four Practices

**Cut the frequency, not the number.** The rating carries real information, but it's only readable over several dozen games. Look at it once a month. In between, it says nothing you can use.

**Compare yourself to yourself, over a long stretch.** The only comparison that controls every variable is you a year ago. Pull up one of last year's games and analyse it today: what you see now and didn't see then is a measure of progress no rating will ever give you.

**Pick your upward target at the right distance.** A player 800 points above you teaches you nothing you can apply. A player 150 to 250 points above, whose choices you can watch, sits in the zone where comparison stays informative. That is exactly what Festinger described.

**Ask for the method, not the result.** When someone improves faster than you, the useful question isn't by how much, it's how. The rating is public, the method isn't, and yet the method is the only one of the two that transfers.

## What to Take Away

Festinger proposed in 1954 that we evaluate ourselves by comparison to others, for want of an objective yardstick, and preferentially against people close to us.

Chess produced an object his theory didn't anticipate: an exact, public, permanent number that isn't an absolute yardstick but **the calculated result of social comparison itself**. So the need Festinger described isn't satisfied by Elo, it's industrialised by it.

Hence three effects: comparison becomes unavoidable, the number slides from measuring results to measuring you, and checking it often delivers nothing but noise.

The way out isn't to ignore the rating, which would mean giving up real information. It's to change what you compare: results are visible and sterile, methods are hidden and fertile.

**After reading:** pull up one of your games from a year ago and analyse it now. What you see today and hadn't seen then is your real progress, measured on the only population that controls every variable: you.
