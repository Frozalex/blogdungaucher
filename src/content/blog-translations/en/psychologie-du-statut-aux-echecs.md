A club, on a Tuesday evening. Nobody's wearing a uniform, nobody has a job title on display, nobody knows who earns what.

And yet everyone knows everyone else's exact rating. To the point.

There are few social spaces where the hierarchy is this explicit, this precise and this public. Which makes it a remarkable object for understanding what status actually does to people.

## The Whitehall Civil Servants

The most important study on status wasn't run on dominants and subordinates, but on office workers.

From 1967 onwards, Michael Marmot and his team followed British civil servants in the Whitehall administrative district. A second cohort, launched between 1985 and 1988, covered **10,314 people** aged 35 to 55.

The founding result is brutal. There's a **steady gradient** between employment grade and mortality from all causes. Men in the lowest grade, messengers and service staff, show a mortality rate roughly **three times higher** than the administrators in the top grade.

Three things make that result decisive.

**Nobody was poor.** All of them were civil servants: housed, medically covered, on a stable income. So this isn't material deprivation.

**It isn't a threshold, it's a gradient.** Every rung below the previous one has higher mortality. Including between the top grade and the one just below it, two comfortable positions.

**And it isn't income.** This is where the most important result sits. Among the factors examined in Whitehall II, the largest contribution to the coronary heart disease gradient came from **low control over one's own work**.

Put another way: being at the bottom isn't what kills you. It's **having no grip on your situation**, which happens to be strongly correlated with being at the bottom.

## The Baboons, and the Nuance Everyone Forgets

Robert Sapolsky spent more than twenty years following a troop of baboons in the Serengeti, measuring stress hormones against rank.

The basic result points the same way: subordinate males show higher baseline cortisol levels, less efficient stress responses, and a slower return to normal after an event.

But the popular version stops there, and it misses the point.

**That link depends on how stable the hierarchy is.** When rank is stable, being dominant comes with low stress, because the position supplies control and predictability. When the hierarchy turns unstable, during periods when rank is being contested, **it's the dominant males who pay the heaviest price**.

And Sapolsky observed something else: males with strong social ties, who weren't carrying the load of permanently defending a position, often lived longer with lower stress hormone levels.

So the common factor with Whitehall isn't rank. It's **control and predictability**.

## The Chess Case

Now apply that grid to the Elo rating, and the result is more nuanced than you'd expect.

### What Makes This Hierarchy Harsh

**It's explicit and numerical.** In almost every human group, status is fuzzy, contestable, multidimensional. Here it's reduced to a four-digit number that everybody knows.

**It's one-dimensional.** In a workplace you can be lower-ranked but better liked, more technically competent, more senior. In chess there's only one axis.

**It's permanent.** No off-season, as our article on [social comparison in chess](/en/blog/comparaison-sociale-aux-echecs/) describes.

**And it's unstable in Sapolsky's sense.** Rank moves every week, which is precisely the condition under which hierarchical stress runs highest, including for the people at the top.

### What Makes It Bearable

But here's the decisive point, and it flips the conclusion.

The main factor identified in Whitehall is **control**. And on that score, a chess player's situation has nothing in common with a civil servant's or a baboon's.

A civil servant doesn't choose his grade, or his tasks, or his pace. A baboon doesn't choose to enter the hierarchy, and he can't leave it.

A chess player, on the other hand, **controls nearly everything**: whether he plays, when, against whom, at what time control, in which format. He can stop at any moment, with no consequences for his housing, his income or his health. He can play unrated.

Put another way, the chess hierarchy combines **maximum visibility** with **minimum constraint**. That's a rare configuration, and it explains why a rating can be experienced so differently from one person to the next: the variable that counts isn't the number, it's how far you feel obliged to be in there at all.

## The Particular Burden of Legitimacy

One difficulty remains, and it belongs to this hierarchy specifically.

Most social hierarchies offer an important psychological resource: the possibility of **contesting their validity**. You can decide your grade doesn't reflect your worth, that the promotion turned on unfair criteria, that the system favours certain profiles. Those attributions aren't always accurate, but they protect you.

The Elo rating shuts that exit. It's **calculated, transparent, earned and revisable**. It doesn't depend on anyone's judgment, on favouritism, on connections. If you're worth more, you can demonstrate it, and the system will record the demonstration.

It's a remarkably fair hierarchy. And that's exactly why it weighs on you.

An unfair rank points at the system. A fair rank points entirely back at you. That's what makes the slide from number to identity so easy, a mechanism covered in our article on [Elo and self-esteem](/en/blog/echecs-estime-de-soi-elo/).

## Status in the Real Chess World

Three observations this grid clarifies.

**A title is acquired status, a rating is current status.** An international master stays an international master even if his rating drops. That dissociation is rare and valuable: it supplies a stable component inside an otherwise unstable hierarchy.

**Status doesn't convert.** Unlike professional sport, being strong at chess brings almost nothing outside the chess world. Our article on [chess and money](/en/blog/echecs-et-argent/) shows that even the best players in the world get little out of it. Which has an interesting consequence: the chess hierarchy is **self-contained**, and therefore easier to leave than most.

**Club status runs in parallel.** In a real club, other axes are at work: seniority, willingness to arbitrate, ability to teach, reliability in team competition. Those axes aren't numerical and don't depend on rating, which makes them, in Deci and Ryan's terms, a source of relatedness that online play doesn't provide.

## Four Practices

**Look at the control, not the rank.** If your rating weighs on you, the useful question isn't whether it's low, but whether you feel you're choosing to be there. The answer is nearly always yes, and spelling that out to yourself does change something.

**Use your exit option.** It's the one thing neither the Whitehall civil servant nor the baboon has. Playing unrated for a while isn't running away: it's exercising a control that's genuinely there.

**Grow a second axis.** Arbitrate, coach, organise, run events. Those roles produce a status that doesn't depend on the number, which cuts your dependence on a single unstable axis.

**Be careful during unstable periods.** Sapolsky's result deserves to be taken seriously: it's when rank is on the line that the load peaks, at the top as much as anywhere else. A stretch of heavy rating swings is a good moment to reduce the competitive load, as our article on [chronic stress in chess](/en/blog/stress-chronique-aux-echecs/) sets out.

## What to Take Away

The Whitehall studies established a steady gradient between hierarchical position and mortality, on people none of whom was poor, with a ratio of one to three between bottom and top. And above all, they identified the factor carrying most of that effect: not income, but **control over one's own work**.

Sapolsky's work adds stability: in a hierarchy that moves, it's the dominants who pay the most.

Chess offers a hierarchy of extreme visibility, one-dimensional, permanent and unstable. But it also offers a degree of control that neither a job nor a baboon troop allows: you choose to be in it, and you can leave tomorrow without losing anything essential.

What remains is a burden of its own, and it's the price of the hierarchy's quality. This rating is fair, so it can't be blamed. What it says, it says to you.

**After reading:** ask yourself whether you play rated because you choose to or because you never considered doing otherwise. It's the one question the Whitehall civil servants couldn't ask themselves.
