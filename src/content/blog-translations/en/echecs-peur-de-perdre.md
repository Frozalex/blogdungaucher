You have not lost anything yet, but are already playing to avoid the feeling of defeat. You postpone entering an event, check the same variation or look at your rating more often than the position.

These situations are possible examples, not compulsory stages of a universal phenomenon. Fear of losing can take different forms, and a cautious decision may be entirely justified.

## What exactly are you afraid of?

Try specifying the imagined consequence: disappointment, a rating drop, someone else’s judgement, the feeling that you prepared badly. These concerns are not interchangeable.

Write down what depends on you and what remains uncertain. You can choose your preparation and examine a threat; you do not control the result alone. Even a good method can lead to defeat against someone who plays better that day.

There is no need to claim a prevalence among club players or particular brain activation to recognise that this fear can trouble you.

## Loss aversion: an analogy with limits

Kahneman and Tversky (1979) propose a theory of risky decisions in which outcomes are evaluated relative to a reference point. Losses and gains do not occupy symmetrical positions in it.

This may suggest a question: do you experience a rating drop as a loss relative to a number you have become attached to? That application does not demonstrate that fear of losing a game is entirely explained by loss aversion. The publication does not measure this mechanism in chess players.

The multiplier “twice as painful” is not a constant applying to every person, stake and game.

## Caution or avoidance?

An exchange, solid defence or draw offer can be a good choice. To discuss its quality, examine the position and tournament situation. Visible style does not reveal a player’s courage.

After the game, compare a decision you considered cautious with its alternatives. Had you seen a real threat? Rejected a favourable line you did not yet understand? Run short of time? This precise analysis helps more than dividing players into those who want to win and those who merely want not to lose.

## Regaining room to choose

Before playing, propose a practical goal: check forcing replies, compare two candidates when they exist, keep time in reserve. The goal need not become an obligation to be perfect on every move.

If tournaments weigh on you, you can return in a less demanding setting, with a trusted partner or in a format that suits you. Accumulating twenty games in a weekend is not a demonstrated anxiety treatment and can also add fatigue.

You can discuss this difficulty with your coach without having it reduced to a lack of willpower. If it persists and extends beyond chess, appropriate support may be preferable to another training prescription.

For your relationship with the displayed number, read [Elo and self-esteem](/en/blog/elo-and-self-esteem/). [Fear of winning](/en/blog/fear-of-winning-in-chess/) separately addresses difficulty finishing a favourable position.

## Identify the loss you are trying to prevent

Imagine a club player paired with a visitor whose rating is much lower. Before the first move the player says that losing would be embarrassing. Ask what the embarrassment would consist of. Would the published result bother them? Is a teammate likely to make an unkind comment? Do they dislike the thought of spending three hours on a game only to discover that one missed check decided it? Each answer points to a different part of the experience. The pairing cannot be changed, but a conversation with a teammate, a plan for analysing the deciding move, or a decision about checking standings can still change what the afternoon feels like.

Now imagine the same player facing the top seed. Suddenly a loss seems acceptable. The consequences for the tournament table are still concrete, but the story attached to them has changed. That observation need not become a permanent label. The same person may play freely in league competitions and cautiously in private games against a sibling. On another day a time commitment or an unfinished deadline can make any lengthy game feel expensive. A practical account begins with the event, the people involved and what the player believes is on the line today.

During analysis, mark the point where the reference result changed. Perhaps the player began hoping to hold a draw, then won a pawn and started thinking that anything short of victory would be a disaster. On the scoresheet the opponent regained the pawn ten moves later. An observation such as “I stopped looking for active play after giving the pawn back” can be tested on the board. A general claim such as “I become cowardly against weaker opponents” leaves you with no particular move to examine. It also obscures games where the same player defended accurately under pressure.

## A quiet move can be excellent

Suppose your opponent has placed a rook on an open file and can invade your second rank next move. You can attack a wing pawn, or you can challenge the rook. If the invasion creates two simultaneous threats, challenging the rook may be the most ambitious practical decision. It keeps your pieces active and prevents you from spending the next four moves defending. Calling it fear because it anticipates danger does the move a disservice. Show the continuation after the pawn grab before deciding which move was timid. Sometimes a flashy attack is the way a player avoids the uncomfortable work of calculating a defence.

Reverse the example. The invading rook would have no target and could be challenged after it arrived. Meanwhile an unguarded central pawn gives you a way to open a file against the enemy king. Your defensive move might still be playable, but the reason “I cannot let the rook in” deserves closer examination. Put the rook on the square you fear and give the other side its strongest follow-up. What, exactly, does the invasion accomplish? If you cannot find anything substantial, you have learned why that particular precaution may cost too much. The test is on the board and does not depend on working up the courage to attack.

## Compare a draw you chose with one you drifted into

Choose two games ending in draws. In the first, find a position in which you could repeat moves and deliberately chose to secure the result. Write down the alternative, your clock times if available, and one concrete risk you saw. In the second, find a position where the pieces gradually came off and neither side could make progress. Identify when your last active possibility disappeared. Perhaps a pawn exchange removed the only possible breakthrough. Perhaps trading a knight stopped you from challenging a blockading piece. Although the scores are identical, the two draws may have been reached through completely different decisions.

Give the positions to a training partner without saying which result disappointed you. Ask them to play the alternatives you rejected. If the active try loses to a straightforward reply, your caution may have been well judged. If it gives balanced play and presents the opponent with real difficulties, you have a variation to learn. If neither of you can settle it, keep the uncertainty in your notes. It is more honest and more helpful to write “I could not assess this ending before the clock ran low” than to turn a hard decision into a verdict on your appetite for risk.

A partner can help most by avoiding hindsight. Once everyone knows a winning continuation, it is easy to talk as if playing it were merely a matter of will. Restore the original time limit and ask the partner to choose without seeing the next move. Ask which reply they worried about and what convinced them it was safe. If they spotted a tactical resource you did not know, keep that resource. If they could not explain their preference either, their confidence is of limited use as a reason to change your decision.

## Return on terms you can describe

If rated games have become unpleasant, decide what you want from the next one. Would you like to finish a complete game, practise an opening you understand, or spend a sensible amount of time on the critical position? Those are different goals. An unrated training game with the same starting position can be a manageable first step if the purpose is to examine an ending. A rated game with someone who agrees to analyse afterward might suit a different question. Avoid interpreting every adjustment as permanent avoidance. Its value depends on what it lets you do and whether you can change it when your needs change.

After playing, record one prediction and what actually followed. Perhaps you expected weeks of teasing and nobody commented. Perhaps someone did make an unwelcome remark; that is information about the social setting, not a reason to suppress how it affected you. You can ask the person to stop, choose a kinder training partner or speak with the organiser. If what you feared was misunderstanding the ending, spend the next practice session on that ending while you remember why it matters. Dealing with a real external difficulty is at least as legitimate as changing the way you think about it.

Over several games, look for occasions when you recognised the risk and still found an acceptable move. Include games you lost. Losing after a carefully checked decision supplies different evidence from winning because an opponent missed the refutation. A review that keeps only victories would reward exactly the habit you are trying to examine, judging a choice by the final score alone. Give yourself credit for identifying a genuine threat and for discarding a threat you tested and found empty. Both are chess skills worth using in the very next round.

For a closing question, ask what evidence would make you prefer the quieter move again. Perhaps a single tempo in the rook ending would reverse the assessment. Naming that tempo turns a debate about bravery into a distinction you can carry to another board.
