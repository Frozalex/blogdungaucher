“How could I miss that?” A mistake may come from incomplete calculation, a misunderstood position, fatigue or an idea that monopolised your attention. No verified figure here allows us to attribute most mistakes to five biases.

These five approaches help examine your reasoning. They do not replace analysis of the position.

## 1. Einstellung: a familiar solution taking up too much space

Bilalić, McLeod and Gobet (2008) study problems in which a known solution may divert attention from a better one. Eye movements show that experts continue to look at elements associated with the first idea while saying they are looking for another. This is eye tracking, not an MRI demonstration.

In training, after finding a satisfactory first idea, try looking for another way to approach the position. Asking this question does not guarantee that the effect disappears.

## 2. Confirmation: examine the defences too

Cowley and Byrne (2004) observe that masters more readily generate continuations refuting their plans than beginners do in the tasks studied. That does not make every master immune or every beginner incapable of critical examination.

When calculating an attack, look at what happens if your opponent does not reply as expected. An intermediate check or a refusal to recapture may change the line. The article on [confirmation bias](/en/blog/confirmation-bias-in-chess/) explores this research.

## 3. Anchoring: a first estimate that persists

Tversky and Kahneman (1974) show that initial values can influence subsequent estimates, even when those values are arbitrary. Applying this to an engine evaluation requires distinguishing biased influence from reasonable use of useful information.

You can record your judgement before consulting the analysis. Then compare the reasons and variations, without reducing the entire session to the gap between two numbers. See [anchoring bias](/en/blog/anchoring-bias-in-chess/).

## 4. Overconfidence: an expectation to check

Heck and colleagues (2025) find an average gap between claimed ability and observed rating among the players surveyed. This study does not demonstrate that a sequence of ten good moves automatically causes a drop in vigilance.

After winning material, re-examine the threats that remain. Having an advantage does not answer whether your next move is safe.

## 5. Availability: the memory that comes easily

In Tversky and Kahneman’s framework, the ease of recalling examples can influence estimates of frequency. A tactic you recently fell victim to may therefore occupy your attention; that remains a hypothesis to check in your own play. When [a blunder seems to come at the worst possible moment](/en/blog/murphys-law-in-chess/), counting the games in which it did not happen helps test that impression.

Compare your memories with a set of games, including the less memorable ones. Then choose a specific difficulty to work on. Knowing a psychology term is not enough to explain a mistake or correct it.

## One diagram, several explanations, one testable answer

Set up an invented endgame with White's king on g1 and knight on e5. Black has a king on h8, a rook on d8 and a bishop on c4. White to move. Try Nf7+. A knight on f7 attacks both the king on h8 and the rook on d8. Your study partner announces that White will win the rook. Can Black answer the check while keeping the rook? Play the answer on the board before reading further.

Black can play ...Bxf7. Follow the diagonal from c4 through d5 and e6. All three features of the attractive fork were real: the knight could reach f7, the king was in check and the rook was attacked. None of those statements tested whether the knight could survive on its destination. A position with very few pieces is enough to expose the missing question. That is why this example makes a good first exercise for a group with mixed ratings.

Why might two players announce the fork so confidently? One may recognise the shape from a puzzle book where every exercise has a winning answer. Another may trust a partner's announcement and check only the two knight attacks. A third may have followed the bishop's diagonal correctly until an interruption. The game score would preserve the same Nf7+ in all three cases. You can settle whether the bishop can capture it by moving the pieces. A claim about a person's reasoning requires different evidence, and an explanation given afterwards may miss part of the process.

Move the black bishop from c4 to a6. This time it cannot take the knight on f7. Look for all legal replies again rather than assuming the first diagram has told you the entire story of the second. For example, the king can go to g8. If White now takes the rook on d8, where can the bishop go? The work of analysis continues after a fork has landed. Giving a player both versions lets a coach hear exactly which features they used, even when they chose the same first move twice.

## Ask a different question for each suspected error

For the familiar-solution problem, give a player a little time after they have found a satisfactory continuation. Ask them to name a second candidate played by a different piece. Do not announce that a better solution exists unless that is genuinely part of the puzzle. If every exercise rewards rejecting the first candidate, the player will soon learn the teacher's habit rather than learn how to compare moves. Keep positions where the first line survives as well as those where it fails. The observable achievement is producing a competing line worth evaluating.

For confirmation, let the player choose a move and then change seats. Their new task is to defend against the move they just recommended. They should find the reply that makes the original justification hardest to sustain. In the fork diagram that reply is a bishop capture rather than a king move. In a quieter position it could be a timely exchange that removes the only attacker. Write down the reply before asking whether the initial idea still works. Otherwise a partner can unintentionally reward a long explanation that never encounters the hardest defence.

For anchoring, withhold the engine display during a first look at a completed game. Record your judgement in simple words and give a reason. On a second look, reveal the evaluation and investigate one concrete disagreement. It is reasonable to change your mind when an engine provides a line that you can verify. It is harder to learn from moving a sentence in your notebook from “Black has active pieces” to “White is slightly better” without finding the move that separates the two evaluations. The line, not the colour of the bar, gives you something to carry into another position.

For overconfidence, make the forecast before checking the answer. Choose a small set of training positions and write down which ones you think you can explain to a partner tomorrow. Actually run the explanation the next day, including the opponent's strongest reasonable reply. Recalling the winning move and explaining the defence are two different standards; agree on one in advance. A surprisingly good result is as informative as a disappointing one. Either way, the comparison concerns your stated expectations for this task. It does not give your coach a licence to diagnose your character.

For availability, look at which games you used to form an opinion. A spectacular mating attack is easier to retell than six ordinary rook endings. If you think a position always leads to attacks, choose one whole event from your archive, hide the results and count the positions where that attack was actually available. A tiny sample cannot yield a reliable universal rate. It can reveal that the most memorable game is the only one in which the key pawn had moved. That observation gives you a reason to study pawn structure rather than spend the next week hunting for the same sacrifice.

## Take one intervention into the next event

Select a single prompt to practise over your next few games. For example, just before a forcing sequence ask, “What can take my checking piece?” That question would have caught the bishop on c4. It is short enough to rehearse and concrete enough to evaluate afterwards. If your most common problem is a premature exchange in quiet positions, choose a different prompt. Applying five psychological labels to every move is an excellent way to exhaust your clock without doing much analysis.

After a game, record one position in which you used your prompt and one where it would have been irrelevant. In the first, say what you discovered. In the second, say what the real question was. A routine earns its place by helping you see something you could act on with the time available. If your prompt regularly points to a threat already checked, revise it. If it repeatedly uncovers a legal resource for the opponent, keep it. The improvement you are after is a change in the moves you consider, and the board lets both you and a partner see whether that change occurred.

When you share the exercise, ask the partner who gives the position to read both colours and all five occupied squares aloud. That small check prevents a copied diagram with a missing bishop from turning an attention exercise into an unsolvable disagreement. The disagreement worth studying is about the legal position both people were actually given.
