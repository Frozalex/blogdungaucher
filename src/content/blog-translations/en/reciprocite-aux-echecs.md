You lose your game at the club. A stronger player sits down across from you and offers to go over it together.

Twenty-five minutes later, you've understood three things you'd never have found on your own. He asked for nothing, he expects nothing, and he wasn't thinking about anything in particular while doing it.

And yet you owe him something, and you both know it without either of you saying so.

This article extends our piece on [the psychology of persuasion in chess](/en/blog/psychologie-de-la-persuasion-aux-echecs/), which lays out the seven levers. It covers the oldest one, the most experimentally solid, and the only one whose use is as often beneficial as manipulative.

## Regan's Can of Coke

The landmark experiment dates from 1971 and was run by Dennis Regan at Cornell.

A participant shows up for what's presented to him as a study on aesthetic judgment. He's paired with another participant, who is in fact a confederate of the experimenter. Together they rate paintings.

During a short break, the confederate leaves the room and comes back with two bottles of Coca-Cola. He hands one to the participant, saying he grabbed one for him too.

**Nobody asked for anything.** The gift is tiny, unsolicited, and comes from a stranger.

At the end of the session, the confederate mentions that he's selling raffle tickets for a charity and asks the participant whether he'd like to buy some.

The result: those who'd received the drink bought about **twice as many** as those who'd received nothing.

One more detail is worth noting, and it's instructive: the effect worked even when the participant **didn't particularly like** the confederate. Debt and liking are two separate levers, and debt doesn't need liking.

### One of the Rare Results That Holds

This series has spent a lot of time flagging fragile classics. This one isn't among them.

The reciprocity effect between two individuals has been recovered in several later replications, across several decades. It's one of the best-established principles in the persuasion field, which isn't surprising: the norm of reciprocity is probably one of the most universal foundations of human cooperation.

So this isn't a weakness in the brain. It's a mechanism that makes social life possible, and that becomes exploitable the moment someone triggers it artificially.

## The Commercial Side

Let's start with the most visible one, because it's also the easiest to deal with.

The chess training market is built around the opening gift. Free trial lesson, first week on the house, repertoire PDF for an email address, an introductory video series in open access, a free analysis of one of your games.

Three honest clarifications.

**It isn't dishonest.** A coach who gives you a free lesson is handing you exactly what you need in order to judge: a real sample of his teaching.

**It works regardless of quality.** The mechanism operates whether the content is good or bad, since it has nothing to do with the content.

**And it takes you out of neutrality.** When it's time to decide, you're not starting from zero. You're starting from a position where something has already been received, and where saying no costs more than the product is objectively worth.

The remedy is simple: **separate your evaluation of the gift from your evaluation of the product**. The free lesson tells you about the coach's teaching, which is genuinely useful. It tells you nothing about the ratio between price and content, and that's where the decision lives.

## The Chess Side, Which Is More Interesting

Marketing is ordinary ground. What's specific to chess is that reciprocity **shapes the social life of the game itself**.

### The Post-Mortem Is a Gift

Post-game analysis is an institution almost unique in sport. Two opponents who've just fought each other for three hours sit down together and reconstruct the game.

But that moment is very rarely symmetrical.

More often than not, the stronger player gives more: he sees more, he explains, he corrects. The weaker player receives time and expertise, and has no equivalent to give back.

This creates a **vague obligation**, never stated out loud, that produces real effects in clubs.

**Some players never ask for analysis.** Not out of shyness, but because they sense the debt and can't see how to repay it. That's a considerable loss, since analysis by a stronger player is by far the richest feedback available, as our article on [analyzing your games](/en/blog/analyser-ses-parties/) points out.

**Others repay in social currency.** Admiration, deference, automatic acceptance of every opinion. Which creates a problem: the debt then gets repaid by **giving up the right to disagree**, which drains part of the value out of the analysis and sets the stage for the mechanisms described in our article on [obedience to authority in chess](/en/blog/obeissance-a-l-autorite-aux-echecs/).

**And the fix is structural.** A club that makes analysis a normal, systematic, expected practice removes the debt, because a collective custom commits nobody individually. It's probably the most useful arrangement a club can put in place, and it costs nothing.

### The Micro-Reciprocities of a Game

They exist, and they're real.

Letting an opponent take back a move played by mistake when the rules don't require it. Accepting a draw out of courtesy after being granted one in the same circumstances. Overlooking a clock that wasn't pressed properly.

These gestures create an expectation of return. Which is perfectly healthy between players acting in good faith, and manipulable when they aren't, as our article on [emotional manipulation in chess](/en/blog/manipulation-emotionnelle-aux-echecs/) describes.

The clearest case is still the draw offer: accepting a draw you shouldn't accept because your opponent was accommodating about something else is debt repayment dressed up as a chess decision.

### Reciprocal Concessions

One variant of the principle deserves a mention, because it's in play in any negotiation.

The technique consists of making an excessive request first, one designed to be refused, then presenting a more modest one. The retreat looks like a concession, and the norm of reciprocity pushes you to concede in turn.

In chess, you find it in organization: entry conditions, schedules, time controls, arrangements between clubs. Knowing that the first request was made to be refused changes the negotiation completely.

## How to Use It Properly

This lever has a peculiarity: it's the only one on Cialdini's list whose **sincere use is flatly good**. So it isn't just a matter of protecting yourself from it.

**Give first, without waiting.** In a club, offering an analysis to a weaker player is a real gift, and it has a real effect. It's what builds a community, and it also answers the need for relatedness described in our article on [self-determination theory in chess](/en/blog/theorie-auto-determination-aux-echecs/).

**Make the debt explicitly cancellable.** If you're the giver, say so: "you'll show me one of your games sometime." That turns a fuzzy obligation into a named exchange, which is far more comfortable for the person receiving.

**Ask, even if you can't repay.** If you're the recipient, note that most strong players genuinely enjoy explaining. The debt you feel is usually overestimated, and the cost of not asking is certain.

**And for purchases, stay on the question of content.** What you received for free is in the past. The only question that matters is whether what's on offer is worth its price.

## Key Takeaways

A can of Coke handed over without being asked for doubles the number of tickets bought, and the effect works even when the giver isn't liked. It's one of the best-replicated results in the psychology of persuasion, because it rests on a norm that makes cooperation possible.

In the chess training market, it structures the whole acquisition phase, and the defense fits in one distinction: the gift tells you about the coach, not about the value of the product.

But its most interesting ground is club life, where post-game analysis works as an asymmetric gift economy. That asymmetry explains two common behaviors: the players who never dare ask, and the players who repay by no longer pushing back.

The remedy isn't individual, it's collective. An established practice commits nobody.

**After reading:** if you've never asked a stronger player at your club to look at one of your games, do it this week. The debt you're dreading is almost always smaller than what you're about to learn.
