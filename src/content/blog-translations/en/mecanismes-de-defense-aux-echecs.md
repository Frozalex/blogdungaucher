Five players lose a game. One blames noise, another jokes, a third wants to analyse immediately. Those reactions invite questions. They are not enough to place five people in a hierarchy of maturity.

The vocabulary of defence mechanisms can help you reflect on experiencing defeat. It becomes much less useful when every behaviour serves to diagnose the opponent.

## A defence in what sense?

In this psychological framework, a defence means a way of managing a conflict or difficult feeling, sometimes without awareness. The idea is not equivalent to a deliberate lie. But you also cannot conclude that an explanation is unconscious merely because it annoys you.

Noise or an incorrectly set clock can genuinely be a problem. Before treating it as an excuse, distinguish a verifiable fact from an interpretation.

## What Vaillant and colleagues studied

Vaillant, Bond and Vaillant (1986) examine the defences of 307 men followed for forty years. Assessments based on interviews at age 47 correlate with independent measures of mental health. A questionnaire later administered to part of the group finds comparable styles.

This supports the value of studying defensive styles. It does not validate instantly classifying players from their exit from a room, or show that a particular gesture has an “identifiable neurology”. The study does not concern chess.

Maturity here belongs to a psychological assessment framework. It measures neither a person’s worth nor chess strength.

## Examples for examining your own account

[Rationalisation](/en/blog/rationalization-in-chess/) can be a possibility to examine when you construct a later justification that does not match your thoughts during the game. Check it by comparing your account with your memories and the variations; a feeling of clarity is not enough.

[Projection](/en/blog/projection-in-chess/) invites you to examine what you attribute to someone else. If you ascribe contempt without an interaction or clear evidence, you may mainly have a hypothesis about their intentions.

Intellectualisation is another possible term when technical explanation avoids any contact with disappointment. Yet analysing a variation may also be a pleasure or a legitimate need. Visible behaviour does not, by itself, determine its function.

Humour can help create distance, but may also hurt or conceal a difficulty. It is not automatically evidence of greater maturity.

## Returning to facts after defeat

You can try a brief written review after the game: what happened, what you felt and what you conclude. Separate those three elements. “I lost after missing an intermediate check” is a technical observation; “I can never calculate” is a generalisation.

If you are still too agitated to analyse, postpone the exercise. One hour or twenty-four hours is not a universal prescription: choose a moment when you can examine the game without restarting the conflict.

## Using the framework without confining people

You can ask a coach to challenge your explanation with a variation. That is more useful than a personality label. If analysing with a friend, start with what they want to understand rather than what you think you have exposed.

This framework remains an invitation to examine your account. It turns neither a game notebook into a diagnostic tool nor a post-tournament routine into psychological treatment.

## Two soundtracks to the same recording

In a friendly game you lose a piece just after a spectator walks behind your chair. Outside the room you tell a friend, “I lost when he came past.” That short sentence contains an event that is easy to describe, a moment in the game and a possible explanation. Imagine that the scoresheet provides one soundtrack and your memory provides another. On the first you can recover the pieces present, the square occupied by your knight and the opponent's reply. On the second you can report that the movement behind you made you turn your head. Neither soundtrack can play the alternative game which would have occurred in an empty room.

Go back to the first move at which the piece could have been taken. Perhaps it was already in danger two moves earlier. Perhaps the opponent missed a first opportunity and spotted the second. The file would then contain an interesting extra observation: you had difficulty seeing the same motif twice, with and without the passing spectator. The annoyance need not have been imaginary for that to be true. You have found something worth practising even if the tournament director can offer you a perfectly quiet room next time.

Reverse the situation. A teammate says that a reflection in his glasses made it difficult to distinguish two squares by the window. You remember that at the previous round he blamed tiredness. The earlier conversation might make you expect another excuse as soon as he starts speaking. After the round, try looking at the squares from his chair. If the lighting is poor, the organisers have a useful practical question to settle regardless of what else he said about his game. A psychological explanation can hide a problem with the equipment just as an explanation about the equipment can leave a chess mistake unexamined.

## What a captain needs to learn on a difficult evening

A captain talking to a team after a heavy defeat may have two jobs. One is to identify what the players would benefit from practising. Another is to keep the door open for those same people to come back next week. Putting the players' explanations on trial can make the reports less informative for both purposes. Try a different invitation: each player may bring one position where help would have been useful. They can also name a question they would prefer to discuss another evening. Nobody has to interpret a neighbour's silence.

The first player shows a game lost on time and jokes that the club should buy a slower clock. This might be humour, a way of putting the others at ease, a wish to move on or simply a decent joke. For Thursday's practice programme the captain needs a different piece of information. When did the player start spending a lot of time and which decision was difficult? The answer might suggest an exercise in comparing two long variations. It can do that just as well following a joke as after a solemn declaration of responsibility.

A second player declines to show the game in front of the group. There is no need to make the player agree that the loss was upsetting before offering another role for the evening. They could take Black in someone else's analysis, or sit a little further back and listen. It remains possible to offer an individual session another day. Keeping that option open preserves a practical condition for learning: a player may return with their own question rather than an account they felt obliged to give.

These are choices for an ordinary teaching evening, not a way of testing which member has a particular defence style. The study cited above evaluated people using information from interviews collected in a research setting. A captain with one loss, one joke and two scoresheets has quite different information and a different task. Attributing a motive from the meeting alone can actually obscure the information the captain is in a position to obtain, such as where a variation went wrong or what kind of session the player will attend.

## Replace a label with a prediction you can try

Write three captions to the same moment on a board. The first reads, “My opponent played too quickly for me to see the threat.” The second reads, “When the rook moved, I was still picturing the previous position.” The third reads, “After the rook move, I calculated my check before looking at the squares the rook controlled.” The first directs attention to the opponent's pace, the second to a potentially incomplete memory, and the third to an order of search you can recreate. All three statements could contain some truth. They do not give you the same starting point for practice.

Use the third caption to construct an exercise. A partner prepares two positions which differ in the location of the opposing rook. In one your check works and leads to a favourable exchange. In the other the rook covers a square needed by your king or queen. Your task is to identify the difference before choosing a move. If the two cases are obvious when you know the theme, ask for another pair later without the announcement about the rook. You may discover something about noticing the cue, independently of why you lost the original game.

The first caption can lead to a small practice experiment too. If the opponent's tempo bothers you, choose one action to mark the start of your turn in a training game: look at the departure square and the arrival square of the piece that just moved. Try it for a single game. Afterwards, ask whether it helped you see the changed squares or just got in the way. You have altered your own routine without asking the opponent to think at a pace that happens to suit you.

Both exercises can fail in informative ways. The two rook positions might be too easy. Looking at the departure square may be unnecessary in an ending with three pieces. You can adjust the task or abandon it. By contrast, accusing someone of being defensive whenever they explain a mistake leaves little room for correction: even an objection can be interpreted as yet another defence. A claim to which nothing at the board can count as contrary evidence is a poor tool for a chess lesson.

Leave a blank line under today's explanation. Another week, you can fill it with a technical detail you recovered, a material condition you verified, or an honest admission that there is still too little information to distinguish two plausible accounts. The open line acknowledges both your experience on the day and the possibility of learning something you could not have known when you first told the story.
