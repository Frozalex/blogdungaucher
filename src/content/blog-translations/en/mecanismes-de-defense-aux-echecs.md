End of round. Five players walk out of the hall, all of them beaten in the hour that just passed.

The first hurls his piece onto the table and slams the door on his way out. The second explains that the clock was set wrong and someone was coughing at the back. The third sits down at the board and holds forth for twenty minutes on the theoretical subtleties of the variation, without ever mentioning that he lost. The fourth tells the story of his defeat as a joke, and it's funny. The fifth writes three lines in a notebook and goes to eat.

Same event. Five treatments. And twentieth-century psychiatry spent a great deal of time establishing that these five treatments aren't equivalent, that they form a hierarchy, and that where a person sits in that hierarchy predicts things about their life that reach far beyond chess.

This article is an inventory. Several of these mechanisms will get their own article, rationalization and projection in particular. Here, we draw the map.

## Where the Idea Comes From

The concept starts with [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud), who talks about defense as early as the 1890s, but it's his daughter who turns it into a system.

In 1936, [Anna Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Freud) published *Das Ich und die Abwehrmechanismen* (*The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence*). Her thesis: the mind has automatic procedures for reducing anxiety, and those procedures operate **outside the awareness of the person using them**.

That last point is the most important one, and it's the one everybody drops the moment defenses come up in ordinary conversation.

A defense isn't an excuse. An excuse is deliberate: I know I'm in the wrong and I manufacture a justification. A defense comes **before** any awareness of the problem. The player explaining that the clock was set wrong isn't trying to fool you. He doesn't feel like he's fooling himself either. He's reporting what he sincerely perceives as the cause of his defeat. The work already happened, upstream, without him.

That's what makes the subject both hard and interesting. You can't decide to stop using a mechanism whose use you're unaware of at the moment it operates.

## What Vaillant Added: The Hierarchy

For forty years, defenses stay a theoretical object, listed differently depending on the author, with no order and no evidence.

Then [George Vaillant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Eman_Vaillant) arrives, a Harvard psychiatrist who inherits the direction of an exceptional study: the Grant Study, launched in 1937, which follows Harvard undergraduates across their entire lives, with regular interviews plus medical and professional data.

So Vaillant had something nobody else had: decades of prospective observation on the same individuals.

His contribution comes down to two ideas.

The first: **defenses aren't all equivalent**, and you can order them by cost. Some protect you by massively distorting reality, which settles the immediate discomfort and creates lasting problems. Others protect you while leaving reality intact.

The second: that ordering **holds up empirically**. In 1986, with Michael Bond and Caroline Vaillant, he published a study in the *Archives of General Psychiatry* covering 307 men followed for forty years. The maturity of defensive style, assessed during a two-hour interview around age 47, was strongly correlated with independently established measures of mental health and adaptation.

Defensive style is therefore, on their account, an autonomous dimension of psychological health. Not a symptom: a way of handling difficulty that carries consequences of its own.

Vaillant distinguishes four levels. We'll go through the last three, which cover the bulk of what you see at a chessboard.

## Why Chess Puts It All on Display

Before the inventory, you need to understand why this game makes such a clean observation ground. It's a matter of the properties of the result.

In most human activities, failure is **negotiable**. A botched job interview can come down to a hundred unverifiable factors. A failed project involves a team. A team sport match is lost eleven-a-side, with a referee, weather, a pitch.

A chess loss, by contrast, has five rare properties stacked on top of each other:

**It's unambiguous.** The king is mated or he isn't. No interpretation of the result itself is possible.

**It's attributable to nobody else.** No teammate, no injury, no refereeing decision. That's the subject of our article on [solitude in chess](/en/blog/echecs-et-la-solitude/): by the rules, you're alone.

**It contains no element of luck.** No dice, no card deal, no share of chance you can honestly invoke. Information is complete on both sides.

**It's public and numerical.** The result is recorded, the rating moves, anyone can look it up.

**It's frequent.** An active player loses dozens of times a year. Even the best lose.

Put together, those five traits produce a psychologically brutal situation: **raw, accurate information, invariably pointed at you, delivered continuously**. Few domains do that.

So read what follows carefully: defenses aren't a character flaw peculiar to chess players. They're a normal response to a setup that would be hard to bear without them. The question isn't whether you use them. You use them. The question is which ones.

## Level 2: The Immature Defenses

These distort reality massively. They're effective in the short term and expensive afterward.

### Denial

The refusal to register information that's plainly available.

In chess it rarely takes the crude form of denying the result. It bears on what the result implies: the player who never checks his rating, never replays his losses, "doesn't have time" to properly analyze the games that would sting. Denial doesn't consist of saying the game never happened: it consists of not looking at it.

Its most effective version is avoiding the ground itself: quitting tournaments, only playing unrated, no longer facing the opponents who cause trouble. The information stops arriving, so the problem stops existing.

### Acting out

The emotion is discharged into behavior rather than felt and thought through.

That's the flying piece, the pounded keyboard, the abrupt disconnection in the middle of a lost position. The ragequit is its modern form, and it has an identifiable neurology, described in our article on [chess and anger](/en/blog/echecs-et-colere-ragequit/).

What defines acting out is that it **short-circuits** the emotion. The player who slams the door hasn't felt his defeat: he flushed it out before feeling it. That's why he gets nothing from it, and why he'll do it again.

### Projection

Attributing to someone else what you can't acknowledge in yourself.

In chess it takes a specific form that's very expensive at the calculation level: crediting your opponent with your own intentions. You see an idea, you like it, you assume he's seen it too and is playing accordingly. Or the reverse: you don't see a threat, so you assume he isn't preparing one.

Its social form is more familiar: the player convinced his opponent is cheating, sneering, laughing at him. A dedicated article follows later in this series.

### Passive aggression

Hostility expressed without being owned. The draw offer made purely to irritate, the theatrically slow resignation, the falsely admiring comment after the game, the game played without effort so the loss won't count.

That last form deserves attention because it disguises itself very well as nonchalance: not trying protects you from the verdict, since the result then measures nothing.

### Fantasy

Replacing action with representation. The player who studies world champions' games for hours without ever playing, who accumulates courses without applying them, who prepares a repertoire for a tournament he won't enter.

Identifying with the strong player replaces the work that would bring him closer. It's pleasant, and that's what makes it dangerous: it looks a lot like training.

## Level 3: The Neurotic Defenses

Subtler, socially acceptable, often invisible. These are the most common among club players.

### Rationalization

The after-the-fact manufacture of an acceptable explanation.

It's the chess player's reigning defense, so much so that it gets its own article in this series. The principle: produce a plausible reason, often a true one, that makes the loss compatible with the image you hold of yourself. Fatigue, time control, preparation, form on the day.

Its effectiveness comes from its accuracy. You may genuinely have been tired. The problem isn't whether the explanation is true, it's **when it arrives** and the question it lets you avoid asking. The mechanism is detailed in our article on [cognitive dissonance in chess](/en/blog/dissonance-cognitive-aux-echecs/).

### Intellectualization

Handling emotional content in a purely technical register.

This is the third player from our opening scene. He doesn't deny the loss, doesn't look for an excuse, analyzes competently. He just talks about the pawn structure and never about what he felt at the moment he understood he was lost.

It's the hardest defense to spot, because it **looks exactly like what you're supposed to do**. Technical analysis is recommended everywhere, including on this blog. The difference is thin but real: healthy analysis includes the player's state, what he saw, what he feared, the moment his concentration gave out. Defensive analysis talks only about the board, because the board doesn't hurt.

A practical tell: if your analyses never mention fatigue, or fear, or rushing, or the fact that you moved fast because you'd had enough, they're probably defensive in part.

### Displacement

The emotion redirected onto a harmless target. The clock, the noise, the lighting, the tournament organization, the uncomfortable chair, the coffee.

None of that is necessarily false. Playing conditions genuinely matter. You recognize displacement by the **disproportion**: thirty minutes on the noise in the hall, none on move 22.

### Reaction formation

Adopting the feeling opposite to the one you're experiencing. The player who's excessively warm toward the opponent who just humiliated him, the insistence on repeating that he couldn't care less, the spectacular sportsmanship that protects him from having to feel the defeat.

### Isolation of affect

The fact, severed from the emotion that should accompany it. "I lost." Flat tone, on to the next thing, no visible trace.

Careful here, because this defense has a mature lookalike we'll get to below. The distinction shows up when the emotion returns: isolated, it resurfaces somewhere else, as irritability, unexplained tiredness, a gradual loss of interest in the game. [Chess burnout](/en/blog/burnout-chess/) often follows that path.

## Level 4: The Mature Defenses

Here's the part popular psychology almost always leaves out, even though it's the heart of Vaillant's contribution.

Mature defenses **are defenses**. They don't consist of facing reality with no protection. They protect you too, just without demanding payment in clear-sightedness.

### Humor

Our fourth player. He tells the story of his loss, it's funny, everyone laughs, including him.

Vaillant places humor very high, for a precise reason: it **suppresses no information**. The player joking about his blunder has registered it perfectly. He knows its nature, its timing, its absurdity. He's simply made that information bearable without altering it.

That's the difference with sarcasm, which is disguised aggression, and with systematic self-mockery, which is a way of making the game insignificant so it can no longer wound. Mature humor is about the situation. The counterfeits are about yourself or about other people.

### Suppression

Deliberate, conscious postponement. "I'm not looking at this game now, I'm too angry. Tomorrow morning."

It's the mature lookalike of isolation, and the difference comes down to one word: **deliberate**. The player knows he's postponing, knows why, and comes back. Vaillant considers suppression one of the most adaptive defenses there is, and it's directly teachable, which is rare in this field.

It's also, precisely, the practical advice given in several articles on this blog: analyze cold, not hot. See [analyzing your games](/en/blog/analyser-ses-parties/).

### Anticipation

Realistic preparation for a future difficulty, accepting that you'll feel part of it now.

The player who, before a tournament, tells himself he'll probably lose two games, asks himself how he'll react, and plans how he'll handle the following round. He isn't reassuring himself and isn't discouraging himself. He's spreading the load.

### Sublimation

Converting the energy of an affect into socially productive activity.

The textbook example: the anger from a loss converted into methodical work on the weakness it exposed. The energy is neither discharged, nor repressed, nor displaced. It's redirected at the problem itself.

### Altruism

Getting something out of helping others through what you're going through. The player who coaches the club's beginners, who arbitrates, who organizes. He isn't running from his own difficulty, he's working on it via a detour that's useful to other people.

## Now, the Limits

We have to be honest about the scientific standing of everything above, because it's notably shakier than the precision of the classification suggests.

**The central problem is measurement.** A defense mechanism is by definition unconscious. So you can't ask about it directly: the person doesn't know. The two available routes are the clinical interview scored by a trained observer, which is expensive and sensitive to the rater's judgment, and the self-administered questionnaire, which means quizzing someone about processes they have no access to.

The results reflect that. Validity analyses of the field's main questionnaire, the Defense Style Questionnaire, don't reliably recover the expected factor structure: the levels that are supposed to exist don't cleanly separate in the data.

**The institutional signal is sharper still.** The DSM-IV, in 1994, included a defensive functioning scale in its appendix, offered as a provisional assessment axis. The DSM-5, in 2013, dropped it. The stated reasons combine the cost of training and scoring with the thinness of the empirical results supporting the theory.

**What holds up.** The 1986 study remains solid in what it claims: across 307 men followed for forty years, defensive maturity predicts independent measures of adaptation. A long-run prospective design is rare and valuable. You just have to note that the sample consisted of men, white, Harvard graduates, born in the 1920s. That isn't humanity.

**So how should you read this inventory?** As a **useful descriptive typology**, not as a validated causal system. These categories name behaviors you can watch for at your club tonight. They don't prove the existence of an underlying psychic architecture organized into four levels.

That's already plenty. Naming a behavior makes it visible, and a visible behavior can be worked on.

## How to Use It

Four approaches, in order of feasibility.

**Spot it afterward, not during.** Structurally that's the only possible moment: while it's operating, a defense is invisible to the person using it. Keep a post-game journal with a single line, the first explanation that comes to you. Reread it after thirty games. The pattern that repeats is your defensive style, and you'll never see it any other way.

**Use suppression, it can be learned.** It's the one mature defense you can install by decision. Simple rule: no analysis in the hour after a loss. Record the position and the facts, close it, come back tomorrow. You're not trying to react better in the moment, you're moving the moment.

**Occupy the space.** A defense fills a void. An explicit post-game ritual, always the same one, leaves less space available: put the pieces away, write three lines, say one sentence to your opponent, leave. The ritual doesn't need to be profound, it needs to exist.

**Aim one level up, not for the summit.** Nobody goes from acting out to sublimation. But from acting out to displacement, then from displacement to humor, yes. Vaillant observed that defensive style shifts with age and experience. It shifts by degrees.

## Key Takeaways

A defense mechanism isn't a moral weakness, it's an automatic operation that makes painful information bearable. In chess, that information arrives with a sharpness few human activities produce: unambiguous, with no external culprit, no luck, public, and repeated every week.

You use them. Everyone uses them. What varies is the price paid. Denial and acting out settle the immediate discomfort by suppressing the information, and therefore the learning. Rationalization and intellectualization keep the information but wrap it up. Humor, suppression and sublimation keep it intact and make it usable.

The theory organizing all this is shakier than it looks, and the DSM-5 drew the obvious conclusion. It remains, as of today, the best grid for naming what happens in the corridor five minutes after the round.

**After reading:** think back to your last loss and recover your first reaction, the one from the first three minutes. Place it in the inventory above. Then look at the level just above it: that's the only realistic move.
