You calculate a variation without moving the pieces, then forget where a knight is. Visualisation requires keeping a position up to date while exploring its transformations. It may draw on images, relationships between pieces and chess knowledge; those descriptions are not mutually exclusive.

## Memory organised by knowledge

Chase and Simon (1973) study position reconstruction and describe its organisation into meaningful groups, often called *chunks*. Their participants should not be turned into two large, representative groups: the experiment examines a small number of players of different strengths.

Experienced players’ advantage is much greater for game positions than for random arrangements. This highlights the role of knowledge without proving the absence of every difference on random material. Gobet and Simon (1996) specifically re-examine expert advantage for such positions.

These findings also do not establish whether a player “sees” an inner image. A recall task and a subjective description do not answer exactly the same question.

## Relationships that provide reference points

A familiar pawn structure may help you recall several pieces together. But you still need to check their exact locations: a knight shifted one square can change an entire tactic.

Knowledge therefore does not replace updating the position. It provides reference points to check throughout calculation. No fixed number of five to seven groups describes every position of every grandmaster.

## Progressive exercises

Start with a position containing few pieces. Choose a short variation, calculate it without touching the board, then check where the pieces are. If you lose the thread, shorten the variation rather than accumulate uncertain moves.

You can then hide the board after observing it and answer precise questions: where are the kings? Which piece defends this pawn? After this capture, which file is open?

These suggestions are practical exercises. They do not guarantee measurable improvement in three to six months with a specified weekly duration.

## Blindfold play is not compulsory

You can work on calculation with the board visible. If you try a blindfold endgame, use a simple position and a partner willing to check the moves. Stop if the exercise becomes unpleasant; accumulating representation errors does not necessarily provide useful training.

In official games, remember the touch-move rules and the prohibition on analysis aids. Exercises that allow freely moving the pieces belong in training.

Keep a concrete reference point: can you reconstruct your variation’s final position more reliably? That criterion is more useful than comparison with a simultaneous blindfold record whose conditions differ from your exercise.

## The knight's route and the squares it left behind

Try this first with just a white knight. Start it on b1, move to c3, then e4, then g5. Before touching the board again, say which of those four squares are empty now. The knight has visited all four and occupies only g5. Name a square it attacks from g5, such as f7, and one it attacked from c3 but no longer attacks, such as b5. A description that lists four attractive knight squares without saying where the knight currently is would not let a partner reconstruct the position. That is the difference between remembering a path and updating a board.

Now stop halfway along the route, at e4. Your partner asks whether a white knight attacks d6. From e4 the answer is yes. Continue to g5 and ask the same question: now the knight attacks e6 and f7, among other squares, but not d6. If the old answer persists, restart just that transition and say what changed. Finishing an even longer route with the wrong set of attacks would give you a bigger number to boast about and a less useful piece of practice. Nothing requires seeing the knight as a sharply lit picture. Coordinates, words, imagined movement or a combination may be enough to keep the state correct.

## A capture has two effects

Set up the starting position and play through this short line once: **1.e4 d5 2.exd5 Qxd5 3.Nc3 Qa5**. Return the pieces to their initial squares and try again without moving them. After White's second move, where is the white e-pawn? It has arrived on d5, the black d-pawn has been removed, and e4 has been vacated. After Black's reply, the same white pawn also disappears. The final position has the black queen on a5, the white queen on d1 and the knight from b1 on c3. The white d-pawn and the black e-pawn are still on their starting squares. They belonged to neither capture.

Ask a partner to check your diagram by counts as well as squares. Each side has seven pawns. There is only one black queen, and it cannot be left on d5 as well as a5. The move to a5 was legal because the queen travels horizontally along the fifth rank from d5 through c5 and b5 to a5, with both intermediate squares clear. Saying “the queen moved across the fifth rank” is more precise than a vague picture of it going left. Orient the board from Black's side and the coordinates stay the same even though the apparent left and right change.

Compare two White choices from this final position, **4.Nf3** and **4.d4**. They are siblings, not successive moves. In the first the g1 knight has moved and the d2 pawn has not. In the second the d2 pawn has advanced and the knight is still at home. If you compare the plans using a board with a knight on f3 and a pawn on d4, you have accidentally granted White a free move. Write the branching position once on a page, put each candidate below on its own line and reset mentally to the parent before entering the other branch. A short accurate comparison is worth more than a long line constructed from both alternatives at once.

## The pawn that vanishes beside the landing square

Here is a more demanding update. From the initial array play **1.e4 a6 2.e5 d5**. White to move. Name the occupants of d7, d6, d5 and e5 in that order. The first two squares are empty, d5 contains Black's pawn that has just advanced two squares, and e5 holds White's e-pawn. White can now play **3.exd6 en passant**. Which squares change? The white pawn leaves e5 and arrives on d6. The black pawn disappears from d5 even though White never lands there. Anyone who checks only where the moving pawn finished will leave an extra black pawn on the diagram.

Continue **3...cxd6**. It is the black c-pawn that now stands on d6; the original black d-pawn cannot make this capture because it was removed one half-move earlier. Both players have lost one pawn. If this is hard to hold in your head, name the history of the survivor: from c7, diagonally to d6, capturing White's e-pawn. Reset to the point after **2...d5** and choose **3.d4** instead. This time the black d-pawn remains where it was. If Black replies **3...h6**, the earlier opportunity to capture en passant has expired. A complete description of a chess position can depend on whose turn it is and what the last move was, even when a particular cluster of pieces looks familiar.

## Set the level by the kind of error

One evening, count errors in three separate columns. A missing or extra piece goes in the first. A piece on the correct square with the wrong legal moves goes in the second. Accidentally combining two lines goes in the third. If most of your problems appear in the first column, practice captures with only a few pieces before adding longer branches. If the board is right but the attacks are wrong, pause after each move to identify what the moving piece stopped defending. If your branches merge, train the reset explicitly: name the common position and say whose move it is before you try the second candidate.

Give your partner the right to ask a question unrelated to your attacking idea, such as “Where is White's queen?” This catches a particular shortcut: tracking the exciting part of the line while assuming the rest of the board took care of itself. You do not need to name every piece after every move in a real game. This is a training check for whether you could recover an apparently irrelevant square when it becomes important. Stop after one position you can now reconstruct reliably and replay it on a physical board. If a move was impossible, identify the earliest illegal transition rather than patching up the final diagram.

To compare sessions, use a fresh line with a similar number of captures and at least one branch. Reproducing yesterday's moves from memory measures something real, but it cannot by itself show that you can update a different position more accurately. A useful practice record might read “kept the material right in both lines, lost track of the rook's new attack after the exchange.” That sentence gives tomorrow's session a clear target without requiring anyone to play an entire blindfold game.

For a final variation, let the partner read the moves and then ask you to supply the move that came immediately before the present diagram. If you remember only where the pieces ended up, the question about the previous move may be surprisingly difficult. Check the written score together. Recovering the sequence can explain a legal option that is invisible in the diagram alone.
