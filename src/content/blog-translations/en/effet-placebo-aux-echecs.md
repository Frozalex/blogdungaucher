There is the jumper you wore when winning a tournament and put on again for important rounds. There is coffee at a fixed time, an opening that reassures you, a gesture repeated before starting the clock.

Is believing it helps enough to play better? Available research does not provide a general yes or no. First specify what changes: how you feel, your behaviour, a test score or the quality of your decisions at the board.

## Better afterwards does not mean better because of it

If you play better after adopting a ritual, several explanations remain possible. You may have studied more, faced different opposition or slept better. One series of games cannot isolate the cause.

Hróbjartsson and Gøtzsche’s Cochrane review (2010) compares placebo interventions with no treatment in clinical contexts. It does not find important clinical effects in general, while noting variable findings, particularly for some patient-reported symptoms.

This does not establish that all objective outcomes are insensitive to expectations. Nor does it test calculation or chess results. Moving between contexts requires additional evidence.

![An observed gain has several explanations](/images/en/placebo-01-comparaisons.svg)

## Cyclists: reading a result with its uncertainty

In Beedie and colleagues’ experiment (2006), cyclists receive a placebo while the information about caffeine varies. The condition announcing the highest dose is associated with mean power 3.1% above baseline. The reported interval runs from −0.4 to 6.7%, including no effect.

That is not a guarantee of improvement for every athlete. It also does not establish that expectations work only through pain. Participants’ accounts may illuminate experience without demonstrating a single mechanism.

For chess, the conclusion remains modest: this study does not tell how many threats you will see or points you will gain. A mechanical-power percentage does not become a cognitive-performance percentage.

## Cognition is not closed to expectations

Foroughi and colleagues (2016) observe test differences after brief cognitive training according to recruitment and induced expectations. Participants selected themselves into groups, so the protocol also needs careful interpretation.

Above all, the study shows why expectations need controlling before attributing improvement to a training method. The literature cannot be summarised as “placebos never affect objective cognitive performance”. It no more proves that a lucky charm improves chess.

In a game, you may feel clearer yet miss a threat. You may also feel poorly prepared and make good decisions. When evaluating a new routine, separate these observations rather than combine them into one overall impression.

![Measuring distinct outcomes](/images/en/placebo-02-ressenti-resultat.svg)

## Open-label placebo: a clinical finding, not a tournament recipe

Kaptchuk and colleagues (2010) study a placebo explicitly described as such in people with irritable bowel syndrome. The open-label placebo group reports greater improvement than the untreated group in this trial.

This shows deception is not always necessary to observe an effect in a clinical protocol. It does not, alone, isolate the contribution of ritual, interactions with healthcare staff or expectations. Nor does it show that wearing your jumper changes calculation quality.

## Keeping a routine without magical powers

Choose a stable arrangement: prepare equipment, check available time, then return to the position. That is a practical suggestion, not a method with an established Elo effect here.

Keep it flexible enough not to depend on an object. If the jumper is missing, the tournament remains playable. “Without it, I will lose” is a prediction, not a completed result. Check it against your games instead of treating it as a rule.

The same applies to “I always lose to them” or “I am useless at endgames”. Check what games actually show. The link with expectations is explored in [the self-fulfilling prophecy in chess](/en/blog/self-fulfilling-prophecy-in-chess/).

The cited studies do not demonstrate that negative expectations always have more force than positive ones in chess. Nor do they promise a cognitive benefit from a supplement merely because it is presented that way. To assess a claim, ask which task was measured, against which comparison group and with what uncertainty.
