There's the sweater. The one you won that tournament in three years ago, and that you put back on for every important round.

There's the coffee, exactly forty minutes before, never thirty.

There's the opening "that works for you," the one you know perfectly well is objectively worse than the other, but that suits you better.

And maybe, for a while now, there's the capsule. Omega-3, creatine, or a blend sold as a cognitive enhancer with a Latin name.

The question in this article is simple: is believing it works enough to make it work? The answer is interesting, because it's **yes for cyclists and no for you**.

## The Founding Myth, and Its Demolition

Let's start with the story everyone tells, which is false.

In 1955, the American anaesthetist Henry Beecher published a paper in *JAMA* titled "The Powerful Placebo." Compiling fifteen clinical trials covering 1,082 patients, he claimed that **35%** of them had been satisfactorily relieved by an inert treatment. The figure entered general culture immediately and never left.

In 1997, two German researchers, Gunver Kienle and Helmut Kiene, went back through the fifteen studies Beecher cited, one by one, in the *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*. Their conclusion is blunt: contrary to what he claimed, **no evidence of a placebo effect was found in any of the studies he cites**.

What Beecher had measured was something else. Mainly **spontaneous improvement**, a major factor in ten of his fifteen trials: people often get better on their own, and if you gave them a pill in the meantime, the pill takes the credit. Add regression to the mean, patients being polite to their doctor, and assorted measurement errors.

So the "power of the placebo" rested, at its origin, on a confusion between "people are better afterwards" and "people are better because of."

## The Great Sorting of 2001

The question got taken up seriously in 2001. Asbjørn Hróbjartsson and Peter Gøtzsche published a systematic review in the *New England Journal of Medicine* built on one decisive methodological idea.

The problem with all the earlier literature was the absence of a good comparison. People compared placebo against active treatment, never placebo against **nothing at all**. Without that third arm, there's no way to separate the placebo's effect from natural progression.

So Hróbjartsson and Gøtzsche collected the trials that included all three conditions. They found **130** of them: 32 with a binary outcome covering 3,795 patients and 82 with a continuous outcome covering 4,730 patients.

The result made a lot of noise, and it's nuanced:

- **No significant effect on objective criteria**: biological measurements, binary outcomes, anything that doesn't depend on the patient's judgment.
- **Possible small effects on continuous subjective criteria**, particularly pain.

Put another way, the placebo acts where the patient is doing the reporting, and not where an instrument is doing the measuring.

That distinction is the heart of everything that follows.

## What Really Works: Sport

Where the placebo genuinely is powerful is in physical effort. And there's a good reason for that.

In 2006, Christopher Beedie and colleagues published an elegant experiment. Trained cyclists ride time trials. They're told they'll receive, in random order, a placebo, a moderate dose of caffeine or a high dose. In reality, **they get a placebo every time**.

Result: average power output rises by **3.1%** when they believe they've had caffeine. With a dose-response effect: the higher the announced dose, the higher the power.

The participants' comments point at the mechanism. "The pain disappeared." "I could give more with less pain."

There's the explanation. In endurance, the limit isn't the muscle's maximum power: it's the moment your brain decides that's enough. That decision rests on a **perception**, the perception of effort and pain. And a perception can be modulated by expectation. The placebo doesn't make the muscle stronger, it shifts the threshold at which you give up.

## And in Chess? The Bad News

Now apply the same grid to what you do at a chessboard.

Where's your pain barrier? There isn't one. Where's your threshold for giving up on effort? It exists, marginally, at the end of a long game. But the bulk of your performance doesn't depend on your tolerance for suffering. It depends on the quality of your calculation, your pattern recognition, your ability not to miss a threat.

Those are **objective criteria**. Exactly the category where Hróbjartsson and Gøtzsche found no effect.

The literature on cognitive enhancement points the same way, and fairly consistently: studies struggle to demonstrate cognitive improvement induced by expectation alone. What moves most clearly is the **subjective perception** of your own performance. The objective measures don't follow, or only in a few very particular domains.

Translated to your scale: believing your sweater brings luck will probably make you **feel** better prepared, sharper, more confident. There's no serious reason to think it'll make you see the move you'd have missed.

And that conclusion fits what we know about this game from other angles. Chess is one of the rare domains where performance is measured with brutal precision, with no element of luck or interpretation. That's what makes it such a good observation ground, as our article on [the Dunning-Kruger effect in chess](/en/blog/effet-dunning-kruger-aux-echecs/) shows. It's also what makes it impervious to expectation effects.

Worth separating what we're discussing here from another question, handled in [does chess make you smarter](/en/blog/les-echecs-rendent-ils-plus-intelligent/): over there, the expectancy effect concerns **researchers and teachers** and skews the measurement of the benefits. Here it's about you, the player, and your own performance in a game.

## The Nocebo, or the Real Risk

Here's the most important point in this article, and the least intuitive one.

Work on expectation and cognition reveals an **asymmetry**. While positive expectation produces little measurable objective improvement, negative expectation comes with more clearly degraded performance. Believing you'd been given a placebo, in some of these experiments, was associated with worse cognitive results, whatever the product actually administered.

That's the **nocebo effect**, the dark side. And it has very concrete consequences at the board.

Your lucky charm probably doesn't make you play better. But **forgetting your lucky charm could make you play worse**. That isn't a symmetry, it's a trap: the ritual brings nothing, and its absence costs something.

Same goes for the convictions every club player has built up through repetition: "I'm terrible in rook endgames," "I always lose to him," "I play badly in the morning." Each one is a negative expectation, stated before the first move. They probably work better than any talisman, in the wrong direction. That's the mechanism described in our upcoming article on the self-fulfilling prophecy.

## What Works Anyway, and Why

So should you throw out the sweater? No, and for two reasons that have nothing magical about them.

### The Ritual Reduces Load, and That's Measurable

A stable pre-game protocol produces a real benefit that isn't a placebo effect: it **removes decisions**.

When you show up for a round, your attention is a limited resource. Every micro-decision eats a share of it: where to sit, when to eat, should I review my line, do I have time for a coffee. A fixed ritual answers those questions in advance, and frees the resource for the game.

That isn't belief, it's conserving attention. And that's well documented.

### The Open-Label Placebo, or the Ritual Without the Belief

The strangest result in the field, and it changes how you think about the question.

In 2010, Ted Kaptchuk and his team published an experiment on irritable bowel syndrome. Patients receive a placebo, but they're told so. Explicitly. The bottles are labelled "placebo." They're told these are inert pills with no active ingredient.

The open-label placebo group shows significantly more improvement than the no-treatment group.

That result demolishes the received idea that a placebo requires deception. It suggests that what acts isn't only belief, but **the ritual, the framing and the attention paid to yourself**.

Transposed: you can keep your sweater while knowing perfectly well it has no power. The act of putting it on marks your entry into a playing state. It's a boundary, not an amulet. And a boundary works even when you know you drew it yourself.

## Four Practical Rules

**Keep the ritual, drop the belief.** A stable protocol has organisational value. Crediting it with causal power turns a tool into a dependency, and sets up the nocebo for the day it's missing.

**Make your rituals robust.** If your ritual requires a specific object, a specific place or a specific time, you've built in a fragility. A good ritual has to survive a late train. Prefer elements that are always available: a breathing sequence, an order of actions, a phrase.

**Hunt down your nocebos, they cost more.** List the sentences you say before you play: "I hate playing this guy," "I'm useless in this structure." Those are explicit negative expectations, and that's the one area where belief has a well-documented action. They're corrected by facts: check your actual score against that opponent, it's rarely what you think.

**Be wary of supplements sold for cognition.** On objective criteria the bar for evidence is high and rarely cleared. Your sleep, on the other hand, has a documented effect on cognitive performance, and it's free. See [chess and sleep](/en/blog/echecs-et-sommeil/).

## What to Take Away

The "power of the placebo" born in 1955 rested on a confusion, dismantled in 1997. The 2001 review, which finally compared placebo against no treatment across 130 trials, settled it with a clean distinction: real effects on subjective criteria, especially pain; nothing significant on objective ones.

Sport is the placebo's ideal domain, because performance there runs through a perception of effort you can shift. That's how cyclists gain 3.1% power by believing they've had caffeine.

Chess is the opposite. No pain barrier to push through, performance measured on entirely objective criteria, and a literature that finds no cognitive improvement from expectation alone. What's left is an effect on your **impression** of playing well.

The risk, though, is real and it runs the other way. Negative expectation does seem to bite into performance. Your lucky charm won't make you win. But the sentence you tell yourself as you sit down can make you lose.

**After reading:** write down the three sentences you say to yourself most often before a hard game. If one of them is a negative prediction about yourself, go check your database to see whether it's true. That's the only placebo that really counts, and it works in reverse.
