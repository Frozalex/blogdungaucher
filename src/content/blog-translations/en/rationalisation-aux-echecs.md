Ask a club player why he played move 19. He'll answer. He'll talk about the d5 square, the rook's activity, the timing of the push.

The explanation will be coherent, technically defensible, and probably false. Not false about the board, but false about him: it isn't what produced the move.

This article is about a machine you have running permanently, whose output is indistinguishable, from the inside, from reasoning.

## The Faces Experiment

Let's start with the most spectacular result in the field, because it makes the mechanism impossible to dispute.

In 2005, Petter Johansson and his colleagues at Lund University published an experiment of rare elegance in *Science*.

A participant is shown two photographs of female faces, side by side, and asked which he finds more attractive. He points to one. The experimenter turns both cards over, hands him the one he chose, and asks him to explain his choice.

Except that, by sleight of hand, the experimenter hands him **the photo he didn't choose**.

Two results.

**Only 13% of participants detect the substitution.** The rest notice nothing.

**And those people explain their choice.** Confidently, in detail, citing specific features of the face they had actually **rejected**. "I like her hair." "She looks warmer." "I prefer the earrings."

They aren't lying. They have no way of knowing. The system that produces explanations runs independently of the system that produced the choice, and it supplies a justification for whatever is presented to it as the decision.

## What Nisbett and Wilson Had Established

Johansson's experiment is the most striking demonstration of a thesis formulated thirty years earlier.

In 1977, in a *Psychological Review* paper that became one of the most cited in the discipline, Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson reviewed hundreds of studies and drew a radical conclusion.

Much of the mental processing responsible for our preferences, choices and emotions **is inaccessible to consciousness**. When you ask someone why they behaved as they did, they don't consult those processes, for the simple reason that they can't. They produce a **plausible theory**, based on what generally seems to cause that sort of behaviour.

The result is often correct, because common-sense theories are often good. But it isn't obtained by introspection: it's **inferred**, exactly the way you'd infer someone else's reasons.

It's the same paper that established the halo effect, covered in our article on [the halo effect in chess](/en/blog/effet-de-halo-aux-echecs/), where participants reversed even the direction of causality.

## The Two Moments of Rationalization

In chess, this machine runs at two moments, with two different costs.

### During the Game: Justifying a Move Decided Elsewhere

An experienced player doesn't choose moves by exhaustive calculation. He perceives. Pattern recognition, described in our article on [memorizing chess games](/en/blog/memoriser-parties-aux-echecs/), proposes a small number of candidate moves **before** any reasoning happens.

Calculation comes next, and its real role is ambiguous. Is it checking the move, or justifying it?

Both happen, and nothing lets you tell them apart from the inside. That's what makes certain moves so hard to give up: they aren't defended by their justification, they **produced** their justification. Attacking the argument doesn't touch the decision.

It's also the direct link to [confirmation bias](/en/blog/biais-de-confirmation-aux-echecs/): if calculation serves to justify, it's oriented from the outset, and will look for what confirms.

### After the Game: Explaining the Loss

The most visible case, and the one that does the most damage.

"I was tired." "Blitz doesn't count." "I'd prepared badly." "I've had a bad week."

These explanations have two remarkable features.

**They're usually true.** You may genuinely have been tired. That's what makes them unassailable.

**And they arrive in under three minutes.** Before any analysis, before any examination of the position, before a single piece of data has been consulted.

An explanation produced in three minutes without consulting the facts isn't the result of an investigation. It's an **output**. Its accuracy is a happy accident, not evidence that it's relevant.

This is the mechanism described in our article on [cognitive dissonance in chess](/en/blog/dissonance-cognitive-aux-echecs/): the explanation has a function, switching off discomfort, and that function is fulfilled whether or not the explanation is accurate.

## The Asymmetry That Gives the Mechanism Away

There's a simple test for spotting a rationalization, and it isn't about content.

**Check whether the explanation is applied in both directions.**

Tiredness explains your losses. Does it explain your wins? You were tired on some of the days you won. Do you then say "I won but I was tired, so this result overstates my level"?

Nobody says that. And yet it's exactly the same inference.

This **asymmetry of application** is the signature of the mechanism. An explanation deployed only when it's convenient isn't an explanation: it's an instrument.

The test works for everything: time control, preparation, form on the day, luck. If a factor only ever appears in your analyses on one side of the result, it isn't describing your games, it's describing how you process them.

## What Rationalization Actually Costs

Let's be precise, because this isn't a moral problem.

The cost is **informational**. An explanation produced before the facts are examined occupies the space the examination would have filled. Once a reason is available, the motivation to keep looking collapses, because the question looks settled.

Put differently, rationalization doesn't make you lie. It makes you **stop looking too early**, and it does so while giving you the feeling of having understood.

And for an activity whose progress rests entirely on identifying your own errors, that's probably the most expensive mechanism in this whole series.

## The Protocol

Everything that follows consists of **dating your explanations**, since their validity depends entirely on when they were produced.

### 1. Write the Reason Before You Play the Move

On three moves per game, note in five words why you're playing this move, before you play it.

A reason written beforehand is a reason. The same sentence produced afterwards knows the outcome, and there's no way to tell what it owes to that knowledge.

### 2. Note the Explanation for the Loss, Then Do Nothing

While it's raw, write the first explanation that comes. One line. Then close it.

Analyse the next day, on the facts. Compare. The gap between the two is your rationalization output, measured on you rather than on students in Lund.

After thirty games, the pattern that keeps recurring in the "raw" column is your favourite rationalization. That isn't information about your losses, it's information about your explanation system.

### 3. Apply the Symmetry Test

Every time you invoke a factor, ask whether you invoked it the last time it was present and you won. If the answer is no, you're holding an instrument, not a cause.

### 4. Separate Description From Explanation

In an analysis, start by establishing **what happened** before looking for **why**. Move 24, evaluation before, evaluation after, time remaining. Those elements can't be rationalized.

The explanation comes afterwards, and it has to account for those facts rather than replace them.

### 5. Accept "I Don't Know"

This is the hardest and most useful answer. The lesson of the faces experiment is that your explanation system will always produce something, including when it has access to nothing. So an answer always exists, which is no guarantee that it's grounded.

Explicitly allowing "I don't know why I played that" frees up the space to go and find out.

## What to Take Away

Eighty-seven per cent of participants handed the photo they had rejected explained, with conviction, why they preferred it. They weren't lying: the system that produces justifications runs independently of the one that produces decisions, and it justifies whatever it's shown.

Nisbett and Wilson had established this back in 1977: the processes responsible for our choices are largely inaccessible, and what we call introspection is really an inference, much like the one we'd make about somebody else.

In chess, this machine runs during the game, justifying moves decided by pattern recognition, and after the game, producing in three minutes an explanation of the loss that's often accurate and almost always sterile.

Its cost isn't dishonesty, it's the premature end of the search. And the only reliable way to sort the real from the manufactured is to date your explanations: the ones that precede the result are worth something, the ones that follow it prove nothing.

**After reading:** at your next loss, write the first explanation that comes to you, then leave it alone. Analyse the next day. The gap between the two texts is the exact measure of what this article is about.
