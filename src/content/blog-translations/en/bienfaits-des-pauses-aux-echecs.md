You finish a session and start another game without really deciding to. A break can begin with that simple choice: stepping out of the sequence long enough to know whether you want to continue.

This article concerns organising rest during and between sessions. For sleep durations and studies of sleep restriction, see [Chess and Sleep](/en/blog/chess-and-sleep/).

## What micro-break studies show

Albulescu and colleagues (2022) combine 22 samples, totalling 2,335 participants. Their meta-analysis concerns micro-breaks of up to ten minutes. It finds small favourable effects on perceived vigour and fatigue.

The overall effect on performance is not statistically significant. Results vary by task; the most demanding activities do not automatically benefit from a short interruption.

This supports making room for breaks within a session. It does not demonstrate that fifty minutes of work followed by ten minutes of rest improves chess calculation, or that a universal brain threshold exists at 90–120 minutes.

## During a training session

Plan a point at which you can stop: the end of an exercise, the end of an analysis or a chosen time. The marker helps interrupt inertia, rather than imposing a duration presented as scientific.

You can stand up, look elsewhere or talk about something different. Choose what rests you. A blitz game can be an enjoyable pastime, but it adds more play; do not assume it always replaces an interruption of the activity.

Before resuming, ask what you want to work on. If the answer is only “win back the points I lost”, you can decide to finish the session rather than keep playing out of disappointment.

## Between rounds

The tournament schedule determines some of your options. Use the available time for ordinary needs: eating, drinking, moving around and checking the information for the next round.

No documented FIDE minimum of thirty to sixty minutes of active recovery between games is established here. A personal recommendation should not be attributed to the federation without a precise text.

If you want to analyse with your opponent, check that you feel like doing so and that it does not make you forget the next start time. You can also save a question and return to it later.

## Between days and tournaments

Look at your schedule as a whole. A busy period at work or in your studies may call for a different volume of play. A day without chess may suit you without becoming a compulsory rule for every player.

A few days off have not been demonstrated to “reset” dopamine. Nor do they guarantee prevention of [burnout](/en/blog/chess-burnout/). If weariness persists, examine what is weighing on your practice rather than waiting for a magic number of days.

## Returning with an intention

Try one change at a time: stopping between games, planning an end to the evening or shortening a session. Note whether you return with clearer motivation and a clearer question.

You can keep learning seriously while making room for rest. The effect on your rating remains to be observed; simply doing less does not promise faster improvement.
