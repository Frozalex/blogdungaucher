Quiet position. Two options.

The first is an attacking move. It creates immediate threats, your opponent will have to find precise answers, and if you don't win on the spot you'll at least have an exciting position.

The second is improving a badly placed knight. Nothing will happen. The position will be slightly better in about ten moves.

You play the first one. Like most club players, and exactly as thirty years of literature on delayed gratification would have predicted.

Except that literature has just been seriously downgraded, and what survives of it says something else.

## The Marshmallow

The protocol is one of the best known in psychology, and one of the simplest.

In the late 1960s at Stanford, [Walter Mischel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_marshmallow_experiment) and his team sat a four- or five-year-old alone in a room with a treat in front of them. The rule was explained clearly: they could eat it right away, or wait for the experimenter to come back and have two.

Then the adult left. Waiting time was measured.

The experiment would have stayed a curiosity if Mischel hadn't, years later, tracked down some of those children. The follow-ups report an association between waiting time at four and various outcomes in adolescence: school achievement, college entrance test scores, coping ability.

The finding became a pillar of developmental psychology, then of the popular discourse about willpower and success.

## The 2018 Replication

In 2018, Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan published in *Psychological Science* a conceptual replication on a sample far larger and far more diverse than the original cohorts, which were small and socially homogeneous.

Two results.

**The association exists, but it's half as strong** as the one reported in the original studies. One extra minute of waiting at age four corresponds to a gain of roughly a tenth of a standard deviation in outcomes at fifteen, which is modest.

**And more importantly, it shrinks by two thirds** once you account for family background, early cognitive ability and the home environment.

In other words, a large part of what the test seemed to be measuring was actually **something else**, correlated both with the ability to wait and with later outcomes. The debate continued, notably with a direct comparison published in 2020 by Falk, Kosse and Pinger, which qualifies how the replication should be read. But the order of magnitude has been revised for good.

### What That Shifts

The popular reading of the marshmallow was moral: some children have willpower, others don't, and willpower predicts success.

The reading that survives is different, and far more interesting.

Waiting is rational **only if the promise gets kept**. A child whose daily experience teaches them that adults don't always come back, that what's announced doesn't happen, that what's put on the table can vanish, takes the treat. That isn't a willpower deficit: it's an **accurate assessment of their environment**.

The capacity to delay, then, isn't only a character trait. It's partly **a learned response to how reliably waiting has led to reward**.

Hold on to that formulation, because it completely transforms the question we're dealing with.

## The Transposition, Corrected

The naive reading would say: players who can delay improve faster, so work on your willpower.

The corrected reading asks a different question: **is the link between what you do and what you get reliable and observable?**

And here, chess has a structural problem, one we already met in our article on [learned helplessness in chess](/en/blog/impuissance-apprise-aux-echecs/).

Delaying, in chess, means working today for a result several months out. But the feedback available, your rating, is slow, noisy, dependent on your opponent and impossible to attribute. You can't know whether March's work produced June's gain.

Put differently, the chess player is in exactly the position of the child who has good reason to doubt the experimenter will return. Their difficulty in delaying isn't a character flaw, **it's a response to an environment where the promise can't be verified**.

Which yields a practical conclusion opposite to the usual one: the lever isn't strengthening your discipline, it's **making the link observable**.

## The Two Scales

### The Scale of the Move

Here the asymmetry is immediate and very concrete.

The attacking move delivers a reward **before it's even been verified**. The excitement of the combination, the aesthetic pleasure, the anticipation of your opponent's reaction: all of it arrives while you're still calculating.

The positional move delivers nothing. Improving a knight produces no sensation at all.

So the imbalance isn't only about timing, it's **structural**: one of the two options pays up front. That's also why combinations get examined less harshly than other moves, a mechanism described in our article on [confirmation bias in chess](/en/blog/biais-de-confirmation-aux-echecs/).

The remedy isn't giving up beautiful combinations, which would be both absurd and depressing. It's **restoring symmetry in the examination**: applying to the move you like the same level of checking you apply to the move that bores you.

### The Scale of Training

Same mechanism, different horizon.

Playing delivers an immediate, variable reward, which makes it, as our article on [habit formation in chess](/en/blog/formation-des-habitudes-aux-echecs/) explains, a near-perfect habit. Working on endgames delivers nothing for weeks.

But now apply the corrected marshmallow lesson. The problem isn't that you lack patience. The problem is that **the promise isn't visibly kept**. You're told that endgames will raise your rating in six months, and you'll have no way of verifying that this particular work is what produced the effect.

An unverifiable promise doesn't deserve to be waited on.

## The Protocol

Everything that follows is about making the promise keepable and observable, rather than demanding more effort.

### 1. Shorten the Loop

Don't measure your endgame work by your rating. Measure it with an endgame test: ten positions, a score, before and after three weeks of work.

That score is immediate, attributable and free of noise. You go from a promise six months out to a verification three weeks out, which changes the nature of the decision.

### 2. Make the Reward Present

The imbalance comes from one side paying immediately. So add something to the good side: the satisfaction of ticking off a session, a visible trace of the streak, a notebook where the work piles up.

That isn't childish. It's precisely the correction of the imbalance described above.

### 3. Separate Choosing the Move from Evaluating It

In a game, when you spot a combination, note the clock time and force yourself to hunt for the refutation for a fixed duration before playing. This protocol doesn't reduce your appetite for attack: it delays the decision until the examination has actually happened.

### 4. Pick Targets with Fast Effects

Not all chess skills convert into results at the same speed. Tactical patterns produce a visible effect within weeks. Fine positional understanding takes years.

Starting with what pays quickly isn't a surrender: it's what builds the reliability of the link, and therefore your ability to delay later on slower targets.

## Key Takeaways

The marshmallow test has been downgraded. On a larger sample, the association between waiting and later success is half as strong as advertised, and it loses two thirds of its magnitude once family background and early ability are taken into account.

What remains shifts the question. Waiting is rational only if the promise gets kept, and a child who doesn't expect it is often making an accurate assessment of their environment rather than a character mistake.

Applied to chess, this flips the usual advice. Your difficulty in preferring slow work isn't primarily a willpower defect: it's a reasonable response to an environment where the link between effort and result can't be verified, because the only signal available is too slow and too noisy.

So the lever isn't forcing yourself to wait longer. It's keeping the promise more often, by building loops where the effect of what you do can actually be seen.

**After reading:** pick one skill, build yourself a ten-position test on it today, note your score. Take the same test again in three weeks. You've just turned a promise into a verification.
