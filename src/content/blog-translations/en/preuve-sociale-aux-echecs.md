You open your database on an opening position. Three playable moves come up.

The first was played in 74% of the games. The second in 19%. The third in 4%.

You take the first.

And at no point did you look at the column right next to it, the one showing what each of those moves actually **scored**.

This article extends our piece on [the psychology of persuasion in chess](/en/blog/psychologie-de-la-persuasion-aux-echecs/), which lays out the seven levers. It covers the most debated lever in the field, and the only ground anywhere where you can put it to the test in three seconds.

## The Principle, and Its Logic

Social proof is the fact that in uncertain situations, we work out the correct behaviour by watching what other people do.

It's a perfectly reasonable shortcut. If one restaurant is packed and the one next door is empty, there's probably a reason. If half a room turns to look in one direction, something's happening over there. The information contained in collective behaviour is real, and ignoring it would be absurd.

The lever becomes exploitable as soon as you can **manufacture the appearance of numbers** without manufacturing the reason that would justify them.

## What the Research Actually Shows

Here, honesty demands a qualification that marketing never mentions.

The reference study, published in 2008 by Noah Goldstein, Robert Cialdini and Vladas Griskevicius, dealt with hotel towel reuse. A message stating that nearly 75% of guests reused their towel got more reuse than a standard appeal to environmental conscience.

In 2014, Gerd Bohner and Lena Schlüter published a replication in two German hotels, covering 724 rooms and then 204.

The result: both messages did increase reuse compared with **no message at all**. But the descriptive-norm message **was no more effective** than the standard message. The specific advantage attributed to social proof didn't show up.

So this isn't a non-existent effect, it's a **context-dependent** one, less robust and less general than its reputation. Hard to predict in advance when it operates.

Which, oddly enough, makes the subject more interesting for us: where the lab struggles to measure, chess offers ground where you can settle the question yourself.

## The Database Case

Here's what makes this field unique.

In almost every situation where social proof operates, it **can't be verified at the moment of decision**. You're told three million people use this product. You have no way of knowing how many are satisfied, or what it did for them.

In front of a chess game database, both pieces of information are displayed **side by side**:

- **the frequency** of the move, which is social proof in its purest form;
- **the score obtained**, which is the data social proof claims to summarize.

And they don't always line up. It happens regularly that a move played in 4% of games posts a better result than the main line.

This isn't a trap or an anomaly. It's simply that **frequency and effectiveness don't measure the same thing**, and we instinctively read the first.

### Why Is a Move Popular?

Four reasons, only one of which is the one you'd assume.

**Because it's objectively the best.** Often true, especially in heavily worked lines.

**Because someone important played it.** One game by a player at the top of the world puts a line into the repertoire of thousands of players within weeks.

**Because it's taught.** Books and courses pass on the main line, which makes it the main line, which justifies teaching it.

**Because it got published.** The corpus consists of tournament games between rated players, with the selection filter described in our article on [survivorship bias in chess](/en/blog/biais-du-survivant-aux-echecs/).

Three of those four reasons have nothing to do with the quality of the move in **your** game.

### The Overall-Percentage Trap

One technical point that's worth more than everything else in this article.

The scoring rate shown by default is the one for **the whole database**, meaning mostly games between strong players.

But a line's practical difficulties aren't the same at every level. A variation demanding extreme precision over ten moves will be handled beautifully by masters and be a disaster at 1,400 Elo. Conversely, a line judged slightly inferior at the top can create enormous practical problems at club level.

**Filtering by rating band before you read anything at all** changes the numbers completely. It's a ten-second operation, and almost nobody does it.

## The Other Forms

**Course rankings by popularity.** The best seller isn't the best: it's the best seller. And since ranking by popularity increases popularity, the loop is closed from the start.

**Audience figures.** Three million users, ten thousand students trained. Those are volumes, presented as results, without the denominator that would make them informative.

**Testimonials.** They combine social proof with survivorship bias. People who quit don't write testimonials.

**And the subtlest version: silence.** A line's absence from a standard repertoire works as negative social proof. You conclude it's bad, when most of the time it's simply absent, which isn't the same thing.

## Four Practices

**Read the second column.** This is the central move, and it takes one second. In front of a database, look at the score before the frequency. That reverses your spontaneous reading order, which is enough to neutralize most of the mechanism.

**Filter on your level.** A percentage calculated on master games doesn't describe your population. It's the most profitable operation in this whole series, and one of the least practised.

**Always ask for the denominator.** Three million users out of how many sign-ups? Ten thousand students trained, of whom how many improved? A number with no denominator is a large anecdote.

**Treat absence as absence.** A rare move hasn't been ruled bad by any authority. It simply hasn't been played by the people whose games make it into the databases. That's also what our article on [conformity in chess](/en/blog/conformisme-aux-echecs/) shows.

## What to Take Away

Social proof is a reasonable shortcut that becomes exploitable as soon as the appearance of numbers gets manufactured. Its experimental base is shakier than advertised: the 2014 replication didn't recover the specific advantage of the norm message over an ordinary one.

But chess offers something unique. Everywhere else, social proof can't be verified at the moment it acts. Here, the data that would correct it is displayed right next to it, free, permanently.

A move's popularity measures what other people played, for reasons that include fashion, teaching and the publication filter. Its score measures what that produced. And for you, the only thing that counts is the score **in your rating band**.

The most powerful lever in marketing can be defused here with a filter and one second of reading.

**After reading:** open your database on your main line, filter on your rating band, and compare the main move's scoring rate with the second move's. You'll maybe learn something about your repertoire, and definitely something about the way you read.
