You look at the position. Seven minutes remain, and your opponent is taking time. You have already identified three candidates. Your mind wanders to yesterday’s loss, then the tournament score. You return to the board and reply too quickly.

This describes an experience you may recognise. It does not show which brain network was active. That is the first distinction to retain when discussing the default mode network, or DMN.

## A brain network and a subjective experience

Christoff and colleagues (2016) distinguish spontaneous thought from its neural supports. The DMN contributes to activities directed towards internal content, but mind-wandering also involves other networks. It is not a switch that turns on the moment you stop calculating.

You can think about a variation without looking at the pieces and remain fully engaged. Conversely, staring at the board does not stop you thinking about dinner. “Internal” and “off-topic” are not synonyms.

## What the 46.9% figure means

Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010) collect phone responses during everyday life. In the analysed sample, mind-wandering appears in 46.9% of responses. These are reports about attention and mood, not imaging measurements.

The result means neither that the DMN occupies half of all conscious life nor that a player loses half of their thinking time. It does not allow converting a four-hour game into two distracted hours.

## A break may help without one proven mechanism

Sio and Ormerod’s meta-analysis (2009) observes an incubation effect: setting a problem aside can facilitate later solving. The effect varies by task and conditions. Their work does not measure the DMN during chess games.

If an idea appears when you revisit a training position after a break, examine it. You need not claim your brain solved it unconsciously or that the DMN caused it. The variation still needs to withstand the opponent’s replies.

## A mistake does not reveal DMN dysfunction

A rushed move can have several explanations: a missed threat, interrupted calculation, an unsuitable habit or a time constraint. The cited publications do not classify errors as “DMN blunders” from the scoresheet.

Likewise, recognising patterns in a known position is not automatically mind-wandering. A quick move may be good or bad; speed does not identify its brain mechanism.

## Returning to a playing question

Use accessible observations. When you notice thoughts about ratings, name the distraction and return to a question: what does the last move threaten? Which defence did I forget? What happens if the opponent declines the exchange?

This is a suggested working routine. It is not attributed to Kotov as a DMN-control technique and does not promise to suppress a brain network.

Afterwards, note a moment when attention changed, the move played and the idea occupying your mind. Compare the impression with position analysis. You may distinguish a calculation error from a move made without checking, but you will not thereby measure neural connectivity.

## Rumination and recovery

If the same missed move repeatedly returns, set aside a time to analyse it, then choose another activity. Walking, talking about something else or resting are personal options. No twenty-minute period is imposed here to “let the DMN work”.

For broader context, read about [breaks](/en/blog/benefits-of-breaks-in-chess/), [sleep](/en/blog/chess-and-sleep/) and [meditation](/en/blog/chess-and-meditation/). Each has its own methods and limits; none reduces to one network.
