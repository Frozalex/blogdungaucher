You're looking at your database. In this position, 82% of games continue with the same move. The evaluation is good. The players who played it are strong.

You play that move.

Question: did you choose it, or did you follow it?

The difference doesn't show on the board, since the move is the same. It shows three moves later, when your opponent leaves the book and you have to understand the position on your own.

## Three Lines, and an Obvious Answer

Solomon Asch's experiment, run from 1951 onward, has the same elegance as Forer's: it fits inside a classroom.

A participant joins a group of seven to nine people for what he's told is a visual perception test. Two cards are shown. On the first, a reference line. On the second, three numbered lines, only one of which is the same length as the reference.

The answer is **obvious**. Tested alone, with no pressure, the error rate is under 1%.

Everyone answers out loud, in turn. The real participant is placed second to last. Everyone else is a confederate.

On the first few trials, everybody gives the right answer. Then, on certain trials called critical, **the confederates unanimously give an obviously wrong answer**.

The results, often badly cited, are these:

**Around 37% of the answers** given on the critical trials follow the mistaken majority.

**Around 75% of participants** conform at least once.

**Around 25% never conform.**

Those three numbers don't tell the same story. The first shows we resist in almost two thirds of cases. The second shows almost nobody resists every time. The headline version, "people follow the group," flattens that nuance.

Asch also established a decisive variable: **unanimity**. A single confederate giving the right answer is enough to make conformity collapse. It isn't numbers that crush you, it's the absence of dissent.

### What the Meta-Analysis Added

In 1996, Rod Bond and Peter Smith published a meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin* covering **133 studies run in 17 countries**.

Two conclusions.

**Conformity is markedly stronger in collectivist cultures** than in individualist ones.

**It has declined in the United States since the 1950s.**

Which rules out treating it as a constant of human nature. It's a **dated and situated** phenomenon, sensitive to the norms of the moment. Which is in itself an ironic observation about an effect meant to measure sensitivity to norms.

## The Difference That Changes Everything

Now let's transpose, and note the obstacle straight away.

With Asch, the majority is **wrong**. That's what gives the demonstration its force: you can measure conformity because you can tell it apart from the truth.

In chess, that isn't the case. If 82% of games play this move, it's very probably because it's a good move. The main line is the main line for largely objective reasons: it's been tested, analysed, validated by engines that don't make mistakes.

**Conforming, in chess, usually means being right.**

Which has an awkward consequence: there's no simple test for spotting your own conformity. The move is good either way. You can't notice it from the result.

So the cost is elsewhere, and you have to go looking for it.

## Where Conformity Actually Costs You

### 1. A Conclusion Without the Reasoning

This is the main cost.

Adopting a move because it's the majority choice means acquiring **an answer without the question**. The answer is correct, but it's only valid in this exact position. It transfers to nothing.

And your strength in chess is measured precisely by what you can do in the positions **that aren't in the book**. Which is where your opponent will take you, because he doesn't know the theory either.

A club player who knows fifteen moves of theory and doesn't understand the resulting structure is in the worst possible setup: he arrives at move 16 with a theoretical edge and no idea what to do.

### 2. Predictability

A practical argument, and often underrated.

If you play what everyone plays, you can be prepared for. At the top level, that's the core of a team's work. At club level, the effect is more diffuse but real: the most played lines are the ones your opponents have seen most.

Which partly reverses the theoretical argument. A line rated +0.10 where your opponent finds himself without bearings can be worth more, in practice, than a line at +0.25 he knows by heart. The engine's evaluation is about perfect play, and nobody around you plays perfectly.

### 3. The Disappearance of Alternatives

This cost is collective, and it works over decades.

A rarely played line is rarely analysed. Rarely analysed, it stays in an uncertain theoretical state. Uncertain, it gets avoided by cautious players. Avoided, it gets played even less.

So the corpus contracts around what's already been explored, which isn't the same thing as what's good. Our article on [survivorship bias in chess](/en/blog/biais-du-survivant-aux-echecs/) describes this filter in detail. Individual conformity is its engine, one player at a time.

It's also why openings "refuted" decades ago come back to life regularly whenever a strong player decides to look at them seriously. They weren't bad: they were deserted.

## Club Conformity

A word about a more social form, and one closer to Asch's experiment.

In a group post-mortem, when three players stronger than you insist a position is bad and you find it playable, you say nothing. That's the Asch situation, plus a competence hierarchy that makes the silence reasonable.

Two things are worth keeping in mind here.

First, **the unanimity result applies**. A single dissenting voice is enough to free up speech. If you doubt in silence, there's a good chance somebody else doubts too, and that you're mutually confirming a false obvious truth for each other. That's the pluralistic ignorance described in our article on [the bystander effect in chess](/en/blog/effet-spectateur-aux-echecs/).

Second, the Bond and Smith qualification is useful here: conformity isn't inevitable, it depends on context. A club where being contradicted goes down well mechanically produces less conformity than a club where it reads as arrogance. That's a parameter you can act on.

## Four Practices

**The explanation test.** Before playing a theory move, ask yourself whether you can say what it **does**, without citing a source. Not "it's the main line" or "the engine approves," but "it stops the knight from going to d5" or "it prepares the f-pawn push." If you have no answer, you've got a conclusion without the reasoning.

**Choose your conformity moves.** It's perfectly rational to follow theory in lines you don't have time to understand, as long as you know that's what you're doing. The problem isn't following, it's believing you chose.

**Look at what isn't on the list.** In front of a database, the eye goes to the most played moves. Take ten seconds to examine a playable rare move, and ask yourself whether it's absent because it's bad or bad because it's absent.

**Be the dissenting voice in analysis.** This is Asch's most actionable result. A single objection said out loud changes the dynamic of the whole group, and it costs almost nothing, including when it's wrong.

## What to Take Away

Asch showed that around 37% of answers given in the face of a unanimously mistaken majority follow that majority, on a perceptual task whose answer is obvious. Three participants out of four give in at least once, but one in four never gives in, and a single dissenting voice is enough to break the effect.

In chess, the transposition hits an obstacle: the majority is generally right, since it follows engines that don't make mistakes. So conformity doesn't produce bad moves, which makes it invisible.

It costs you elsewhere: conclusions acquired without the reasoning, hence unusable outside the book; predictability; and collectively, a corpus contracting around what's already been explored.

So the useful question isn't "am I original," which is a bad goal. It's: **can I explain what this move does?** If you can, it doesn't matter that everyone plays it.

**After reading:** over the first ten moves of your next game, note the ones whose function you can explain without citing a source. The ratio you get is an honest measure of how much you understand about your own repertoire.
