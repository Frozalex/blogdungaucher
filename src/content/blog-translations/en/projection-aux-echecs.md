You see an idea. An exchange sacrifice that opens the file. You calculate, you find it doesn't quite work, you drop the idea.

Three moves later, your opponent plays precisely that sacrifice. And it works, because you hadn't pushed the calculation far enough.

This isn't a problem of level: you had seen the idea. It's a problem of attribution. You assumed his view of the position resembled yours, including in what you had discarded.

## The Problem With Freudian Projection

We have to start with a clarification, because the word carries baggage.

In the classical vocabulary inherited from Freud and taken up by his daughter in the inventory we presented in our article on [defense mechanisms in chess](/en/blog/mecanismes-de-defense-aux-echecs/), projection is a **defensive** mechanism. You attribute to another person a mental content you can't acknowledge in yourself: the aggressive man sees aggression everywhere, the cheat suspects cheating.

That version is poorly supported. Like the defense model as a whole, it suffers from an insurmountable measurement problem: the mechanism is supposed to be unconscious, therefore unreachable by questionnaire, and observing it from outside depends heavily on the observer's interpretation. The removal of the corresponding scale from the DSM-5 fits that assessment.

Which doesn't mean nothing is there. It means the solid mechanism has to be looked for elsewhere, and it exists.

## False Consensus, on the Other Hand, Is Well Established

In 1977, Lee Ross, David Greene and Pamela House published a series of experiments on what they called the **false consensus effect**.

The best-known protocol is disarmingly simple. Students were asked whether they'd agree to walk around campus wearing a sandwich board reading "Eat at Joe's." Then they were asked to estimate **what proportion of other students would agree**.

Those who agreed estimated that a majority would agree. Those who refused estimated that a majority would refuse.

Each group overestimates how common its own choice is. And the result reproduces across a wide range of opinions, preferences and behaviours.

The mechanism isn't defensive at all. There's nothing shameful about accepting or refusing a sandwich board. It's a problem of **inference under limited information**: to estimate what others think, you have access to exactly one mind from the inside, your own. So it serves as the starting point, and the adjustment from it is insufficient, exactly as in [anchoring bias](/en/blog/biais-d-ancrage-aux-echecs/).

It's this version, non-defensive and solidly documented, that explains what happens at the board.

## Projection Is a Failure of Theory of Mind

The most useful formulation is this one, and it connects this article to the previous one in the series.

[Theory of mind](/en/blog/theorie-de-l-esprit-aux-echecs/) means building a model of another person's mind **distinct from your own**. It's what lets you set a trap: knowing that he doesn't see what you see.

Projection is what happens when that model is built **by default**, meaning filled in with your own content.

So it isn't another mechanism: it's **the degraded mode of the same mechanism**. Modelling someone else's mind is expensive; absent explicit effort, the system fills the slots with whatever is closest to hand.

## The Four Forms at the Board

### 1. "He Saw What I Saw"

The most frequent and the most expensive.

You find a strong idea, you calculate the refutation, you drop the idea. The error is assuming that **he too** dismissed it for the same reason, when he may never have considered it at all, or may have pushed the calculation one move further than you did.

That's the opening scene of this article. It produces a very identifiable category of error: you fail to watch an idea **you had actually seen**, because you filed it as refuted for both of you.

### 2. "He Didn't See What I Didn't See"

The mirror image, and more insidious.

You perceive no threat in a position, so you play confidently. The absence of a threat in your representation becomes an absence of threat in his.

But his plan may be built on a square, a structure or a pattern you simply haven't mastered. It isn't that he calculates better: he sees something else.

### 3. "He'd Play the Way I'd Play"

Projection applied to style.

You prepare a line assuming he'll choose the most natural continuation, meaning the one that looks natural to you. An attacking player prepares against attacking moves; a solid player prepares against solid ones.

It also explains a common experience: players often report being surprised by moves that aren't objectively surprising at all, and that simply fall outside their own repertoire of preferences.

### 4. Social Projection Away From the Board

This one deserves flagging because it produces accusations.

Assuming your opponent feels what you would feel in his place, contempt, mockery, irritation, is an act of projection. And it can feed interpretations with no support in the facts, as our article on [the bystander effect in chess](/en/blog/effet-spectateur-aux-echecs/) describes in relation to cheating suspicions.

## Why Chess Amplifies the Mechanism

Three reasons, and the third is specific to this game.

**The information is shared.** Unlike poker, you both see exactly the same position. So it's extremely tempting to conclude that you both see the same thing. But what's shared is the **stimulus**, not the **perception**. What a player perceives depends entirely on his repertoire of patterns.

**The modelling effort is expensive mid-game.** Holding two representations of the position simultaneously, yours and an estimate of his, consumes a resource already saturated by calculation. So the default mode is the cheap mode.

**And the engine trains you to project.** This is the least obvious point. When you analyse with a machine, you work in a world where the opponent sees everything, since that's the assumption behind minimax. Hundreds of hours of analysis in that frame install a habit of reasoning: assume the other player sees what there is to see. In a game against a human, that assumption is false in both directions.

## The Protocol

**1. Ask the question of source.** This is the central rule, and it fits in one sentence. Faced with any assumption about what your opponent sees, prepares or fears, ask yourself: **where does this information come from?**

There are only two possible answers. Either from something in the position, a move played, a think time, a piece placed. Or from your own head. In the second case it's a projection, and it has no evidential value.

**2. Never file an idea as refuted for both of you.** If you considered a strong move and dismissed it, mark it as **live** on your opponent's side. The fact that you refuted it is information about you, not about him. And your refutation may be wrong.

**3. Look for the move you'd never play.** A direct corrective for style projection. Once per critical position, ask what would be played by someone who likes exactly the opposite of what you like.

**4. Use what you actually know about him.** Projection fills a vacuum. The best remedy is not to leave the vacuum there: three lines of concrete observations about a regular opponent beat a default model.

**5. In analysis, separate two questions.** What was the best move, and what was humanly visible in this position? The engine answers the first. Only a player can help you with the second, and it's the second that corrects projection.

## What to Take Away

The defensive projection of the psychoanalytic tradition, attributing to others what you refuse in yourself, suffers from the same measurement problems as the defense model as a whole.

But a neighbouring and far better established mechanism produces the same effects: social projection, of which the false consensus effect is the demonstration. We systematically overestimate how much others resemble us, not out of defense but because we have access to exactly one mind from the inside.

In chess this produces two symmetrical, perfectly identifiable errors: failing to watch an idea you discarded yourself, and failing to fear a threat you didn't see. The fact that both players are looking at the same position makes the mechanism all the more tempting, when it's precisely what makes it wrong.

One question is enough to catch it: where does what I think I know about what he sees actually come from?

**After reading:** in your next game, note one strong idea you considered and then discarded. Watch it from then on as though it were still on the table. That's the gesture that cancels this error.
