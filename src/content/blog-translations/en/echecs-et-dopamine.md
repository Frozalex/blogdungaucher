You find a combination you could not see a minute earlier. You smile and start another exercise. Or you win a game and immediately click “Play again”.

Wanting to continue is a real experience. Describing it in terms of dopamine is tempting; we still need to distinguish what was measured from what we imagine.

## Reward is not a single sensation

Berridge and Robinson (1998) distinguish, among other things, the motivational attraction of a reward from its subjective pleasure. Their review discusses dopamine’s role in these processes. It does not justify saying that a pizza and checkmate produce exactly the same brain response.

You may want to start another game while no longer enjoying it much. This distinction helps describe your experience without claiming to measure neurotransmitters.

## What prediction error shows

Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997) link dopaminergic-neuron activity to the difference between expected and obtained reward. In the experiments discussed, an unexpected reward may trigger a response; after learning, the response may shift to the predictive signal. The absence of an expected reward produces a different pattern.

This is a framework for understanding some learning processes. It does not demonstrate that every opponent blunder triggers more dopamine than your own good move, or that one Elo point corresponds to a quantity of neurotransmitter.

## The moment you see the solution

Kounios and Beeman (2014) review the cognitive neuroscience of *insight*, the experience of suddenly discovering a solution. Their methods and tasks do not constitute dopamine measurement during chess-puzzle solving.

You can therefore say “it clicked” without adding “my nucleus accumbens released a spike”. Lived experience, activity measured through imaging and the release of a molecule are different levels of description.

## Why comparing games requires caution

The sources presented do not rank chess, Go, video games or gambling on a dopamine scale. Nor do they show that every classical game is healthy while every blitz game is compulsive.

An interface enabling successive games makes stopping easy to postpone: that is an observable property of the design. Saying it “saturates the circuit” or requires twenty-four hours of recovery would need specific measurements not provided here.

## Observing what you actually do

For your practice, begin with concrete questions: had you chosen to play this game? How much time did you intend to spend? Did you continue to recover points after already wanting to stop?

You can decide an end time before starting, choose a study theme or alternate with another activity. These organise your time; they are not a dopamine-reset protocol.

Keep the option of appreciating a session without a final win. Understanding why a variation fails can be a useful achievement. You do not have to solve one last easy puzzle to “positively condition” your brain.

If playing regularly interferes with obligations or causes distress, seek support for the situation you are experiencing. A supposed neurochemical explanation does not replace that conversation.
